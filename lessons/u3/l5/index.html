The Sine Law

SOH CAH TOA only works in right triangles. The sine law works in every triangle: each side divided by the sine of the angle opposite it gives the same number. Today: why it is true, and how to use it to find sides and angles.

  • split an oblique triangle into two right triangles with an altitude

Labelling Triangles and a Right-Triangle Review

From now on a triangle is not always a right triangle. Name the vertices with capital letters: in \triangle ABC the angles are \angle A, \angle B and \angle C, and the side opposite each angle (the side it does not touch) gets the matching lowercase letter: \textcolor{#1f5bd6}{a} is opposite \textcolor{#1f5bd6}{\angle A}, \textcolor{#12805a}{b} is opposite \textcolor{#12805a}{\angle B}, \textcolor{#7b4fc4}{c} is opposite \textcolor{#7b4fc4}{\angle C}. The three angles always add to 180^\circ.
Degree mode first. MODE, ▼▼ to the RADIAN DEGREE row, ► or ◄ onto DEGREE if it isn’t already highlighted, ENTER. (Casio: SHIFT MENU, Angle: Deg.) Quick check: sin30)ENTER must give 0.5.

In a right triangle, the primary ratios connect an acute angle \theta to two sides.

\sin\theta=\dfrac{\text{opposite}}{\text{hypotenuse}}    \cos\theta=\dfrac{\text{adjacent}}{\text{hypotenuse}}    \tan\theta=\dfrac{\text{opposite}}{\text{adjacent}}    (SOH CAH TOA)
Example 1

Right-triangle review

Round sides to the nearest tenth and angles to the nearest degree.

(a) Determine x.
x is opposite 34^\circ and 15.0 is the hypotenuse:
\sin34^\circ=\dfrac{x}{15.0}, so x=15.0\sin34^\circ\approx\boldsymbol{8.4} cm
(b) Determine \theta.
7.0 is opposite \theta and 11.0 is adjacent:
\tan\theta=\dfrac{7.0}{11.0}, so \theta=\tan^{-1}\!\left(\dfrac{7.0}{11.0}\right)\approx\boldsymbol{32^\circ}

Splitting a Triangle into Right Triangles

A triangle with no right angle is an oblique triangle; SOH CAH TOA does not apply to it directly. Drawing an altitude (a height, perpendicular to a side) splits it into two right triangles that SOH CAH TOA can solve.
Example 2

Two right triangles inside one

In \triangle ABC, \angle A=38^\circ, \angle B=67^\circ and AC=14.0 cm. The altitude CD has length h.

(a) Use \triangle ACD to determine h to the nearest hundredth.
\sin38^\circ=\dfrac{h}{14.0}, so h=14.0\sin38^\circ\approx\boldsymbol{8.62} cm
(keep 8.6193\dots in the calculator)
(b) Use \triangle BCD to determine BC to the nearest tenth.
\sin67^\circ=\dfrac{h}{BC}, so BC=\dfrac{h}{\sin67^\circ}=\dfrac{8.6193\dots}{\sin67^\circ}\approx\boldsymbol{9.4} cm

3

The Sine Law

Example 2 wrote the same altitude two ways. Doing this for any triangle gives a rule that skips the altitude.

Example 3

Complete the proof

CD is the altitude from C, with length h. Tap each blank to check it.

In \triangle ACD:   \sin A= \dfrac{h}{b},  so  h= b\sin A.
In \triangle BCD:   \sin B= \dfrac{h}{a},  so  h= a\sin B.
Both equal h, so  a\sin B = b\sin A.
Divide both sides by \sin A\sin B:   \dfrac{a}{\sin A}=\dfrac{b}{\sin B}
\sin A=\dfrac{h}{b},\ h=b\sin A;   \sin B=\dfrac{h}{a},\ h=a\sin B;   a\sin B=b\sin A;   \boldsymbol{\dfrac{a}{\sin A}=\dfrac{b}{\sin B}}

Obtuse angles: \sin(180^\circ-\theta)=\sin\theta

An altitude from A gives \dfrac{b}{\sin B}=\dfrac{c}{\sin C} in the same way. If an angle is obtuse, the altitude falls outside the triangle, and the proof uses \sin(180^\circ-\theta)=\sin\theta: the two angles have the same reference angle and sine is positive in quadrant II (Lesson 3). For example, \sin130^\circ=\sin50^\circ\approx0.766. The law holds in every triangle.

The Sine Law. In any \triangle ABC:   \dfrac{\textcolor{#1f5bd6}{a}}{\sin \textcolor{#1f5bd6}{A}}=\dfrac{\textcolor{#12805a}{b}}{\sin \textcolor{#12805a}{B}}=\dfrac{\textcolor{#7b4fc4}{c}}{\sin \textcolor{#7b4fc4}{C}}   or   \dfrac{\sin \textcolor{#1f5bd6}{A}}{\textcolor{#1f5bd6}{a}}=\dfrac{\sin \textcolor{#12805a}{B}}{\textcolor{#12805a}{b}}=\dfrac{\sin \textcolor{#7b4fc4}{C}}{\textcolor{#7b4fc4}{c}}
Use two of the ratios at a time. One of them must be a known pair: an angle and its opposite side. Put the unknown on top: sides on top to find a side, sines on top to find an angle.
Example 4

Example 2 again, in one step

Use the sine law in the triangle from Example 2 (\angle A=38^\circ, \angle B=67^\circ, b=14.0 cm). Round to the nearest tenth.

(a) Determine a=BC. Compare with Example 2.
Known pair: \angle B=67^\circ and b=14.0.
\dfrac{a}{\sin38^\circ}=\dfrac{14.0}{\sin67^\circ}
a=\dfrac{14.0\sin38^\circ}{\sin67^\circ}\approx\boldsymbol{9.4} cm, the same answer in one step.
(b) Determine c=AB.
\angle C=180^\circ-38^\circ-67^\circ=75^\circ
\dfrac{c}{\sin75^\circ}=\dfrac{14.0}{\sin67^\circ}
c=\dfrac{14.0\sin75^\circ}{\sin67^\circ}\approx\boldsymbol{14.7} cm

4

Finding a Side

When two angles and any side are known (AAS or ASA), the sine law finds the other sides.

  1. If the known side is not opposite a known angle, find the third angle first (180^\circ minus the other two). Now there is a known pair.
  2. Write \dfrac{\text{unknown side}}{\sin(\text{its opposite angle})}=\dfrac{\text{known side}}{\sin(\text{its opposite angle})} and multiply to isolate the side.
  3. Keep unrounded values in the calculator and round only the final answer.
Example 5

Finding sides

Round sides to the nearest tenth.

(a) In \triangle PQR, \angle P=52^\circ, \angle Q=46^\circ and p=23.5 m. Determine q.
Known pair: \angle P and p.
\dfrac{q}{\sin46^\circ}=\dfrac{23.5}{\sin52^\circ}
q=\dfrac{23.5\sin46^\circ}{\sin52^\circ}\approx\boldsymbol{21.5} m
(b) In \triangle DEF, \angle D=33^\circ, \angle E=109^\circ and DE=18.0 cm. Determine d and e.
DE=f is opposite \angle F=180^\circ-33^\circ-109^\circ=38^\circ.
e=\dfrac{18.0\sin109^\circ}{\sin38^\circ}\approx\boldsymbol{27.6} cm  (opposite the obtuse angle: longest)
d=\dfrac{18.0\sin33^\circ}{\sin38^\circ}\approx\boldsymbol{15.9} cm
Same pattern every time: known side × sin(unknown’s angle) ÷ sin(known side’s angle). (a) 23.5sin46)÷sin52)ENTER gives 21.452… (b) 18sin109)÷sin38)ENTER gives 27.643…
5

Finding an Angle

To find an angle, put the sines on top: \dfrac{\sin B}{b}=\dfrac{\sin A}{a} gives \sin B=\dfrac{b\sin A}{a}, then \angle B=\sin^{-1}\!\left(\dfrac{b\sin A}{a}\right) (TI-84: 2ndsin⁻¹).

The inverse sine key only gives an angle from 0^\circ to 90^\circ. Since \sin(180^\circ-B)=\sin B, an obtuse angle has the same sine, so we must know the angle is acute. In this lesson every question tells you why it is:
  • the triangle is acute (all its angles are less than 90^\circ), or
  • the known angle is opposite the longer of the two given sides. The larger side is opposite the larger angle, so the unknown angle is smaller than the known angle; a triangle has at most one angle of 90^\circ or more, so the unknown angle is acute.

When neither is true there may be two triangles: that is the ambiguous case (Lesson 7). Solving a triangle means finding all of its unknown sides and angles.

Example 6

Finding an angle, then solving

Round sides to the nearest tenth and angles to the nearest degree.

(a) In \triangle ABC, \angle A=64^\circ, a=12.5 cm and b=9.2 cm. Determine \angle B, \angle C and c.
a>b, so \angle B<\angle A and \angle B is acute.
\sin B=\dfrac{9.2\sin64^\circ}{12.5}\approx0.6615
\angle B=\sin^{-1}(0.6615\dots)\approx41.4^\circ\approx\boldsymbol{41^\circ}
\angle C\approx180^\circ-64^\circ-41.4^\circ=74.6^\circ\approx\boldsymbol{75^\circ}
c=\dfrac{12.5\sin74.6^\circ}{\sin64^\circ}\approx\boldsymbol{13.4} cm
(b) In \triangle XYZ, \angle Z=127^\circ, z=20.0 m and x=14.0 m. Solve the triangle.
\angle Z is obtuse, so \angle X must be acute.
\sin X=\dfrac{14.0\sin127^\circ}{20.0}\approx0.5590
\angle X\approx34.0^\circ\approx\boldsymbol{34^\circ}
\angle Y\approx180^\circ-127^\circ-34.0^\circ=19.0^\circ\approx\boldsymbol{19^\circ}
y=\dfrac{20.0\sin19.0^\circ}{\sin127^\circ}\approx\boldsymbol{8.2} m

Problems in Context

Sketch the situation, label what is known, and look for a triangle with a known pair (or one you can complete with the angle sum). Some problems need a second step with SOH CAH TOA. Two kinds of angles appear often:

Bearing
A bearing such as N52^\circE means: face north, then turn 52^\circ toward the east.
Angle of elevation
An angle of elevation is measured up from the horizontal to the line of sight.
Example 7

Fire lookout towers

Fire lookout tower B is 12.0 km due east of tower A. A fire F is spotted on a bearing of N52^\circE from A and N28^\circW from B. How far is the fire from each tower, to the nearest tenth of a kilometre?

At A: \angle FAB=90^\circ-52^\circ=38^\circ. At B: \angle FBA=90^\circ-28^\circ=62^\circ.
\angle F=180^\circ-38^\circ-62^\circ=80^\circ, opposite AB=12.0 km.
AF=\dfrac{12.0\sin62^\circ}{\sin80^\circ}\approx\boldsymbol{10.8} km    BF=\dfrac{12.0\sin38^\circ}{\sin80^\circ}\approx\boldsymbol{7.5} km
Both distances use the known pair \angle F=80^\circ, AB=12.0: 12sin62)÷sin80)ENTER gives 10.758… and 12sin38)÷sin80)ENTER gives 7.5019…

A second step: the sine law, then SOH CAH TOA

Some distances can’t be measured directly, like the width of a river. Measure a baseline on your side and two angles, use the sine law to get a side of the big triangle, then use a right triangle inside it for the distance straight across.

Example 8

How wide is the river?

To find the width of a river, a surveyor marks points P and Q, 250 m apart, along one straight bank. A tree T stands on the far bank. She measures \angle TPQ=57^\circ and \angle TQP=68^\circ.

(a) Determine PT to the nearest tenth of a metre.
\angle T=180^\circ-57^\circ-68^\circ=55^\circ, opposite PQ=250.
PT=\dfrac{250\sin68^\circ}{\sin55^\circ}\approx\boldsymbol{283.0} m
(b) Determine the width of the river to the nearest tenth of a metre.
The width w is the altitude from T. In the right triangle with hypotenuse PT:
w=PT\sin57^\circ\approx282.97\sin57^\circ\approx\boldsymbol{237.3} m
(c) Determine the area of \triangle PQT to the nearest square metre.
\text{Area}=\tfrac{1}{2}(\text{base})(\text{height})=\tfrac{1}{2}(250)(237.32\dots)\approx\boldsymbol{29\,665} m2
Chain it with Ans. 250sin68)÷sin55)ENTER gives PT, 282.97…; Anssin57)ENTER gives w, 237.31…; .5×250×AnsENTER gives the area, 29 664.8…

Key ideas

Labels. Side a is opposite \angle A (the side A does not touch). The angles add to 180^\circ. The longest side is opposite the largest angle. Degree mode!
The sine law. \dfrac{a}{\sin A}=\dfrac{b}{\sin B}=\dfrac{c}{\sin C} in every triangle: the altitude h=b\sin A=a\sin B. Obtuse angles work too: \sin(180^\circ-\theta)=\sin\theta.
Finding a side. Get a known pair (use 180^\circ for the third angle if needed). Side on top: x=\dfrac{(\text{known side})\sin(\text{opp. }x)}{\sin(\text{opp. known side})}.
Finding an angle. Sines on top, then \sin^{-1}. It only gives 0^\circ–90^\circ, so know why the angle is acute (an acute triangle, or the known angle is opposite the longer side).
