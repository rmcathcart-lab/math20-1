Clock Hands

At 3:00, the minute hand and the hour hand of a clock make a right angle.

How many times in one whole day (24 hours) do the hands make a right angle?

?
How did you count?
44 times, not 48. In 12 hours the minute hand laps the hour hand only 11 times (not 12), and every lap has 2 right angles: 11\times2=22 right angles every 12 hours, so 44 in a day.

Angles in Standard Position and Reference Angles

Every angle in this unit starts in the same place: its vertex at the origin and its first arm on the positive x-axis. We turn a second arm, the terminal arm, name the quadrant it stops in, and find its reference angle: how far the arm is from the x-axis.

I can…
  • sketch an angle in standard position and name its vertex, initial arm and terminal arm
  • state the quadrant an angle terminates in, or recognise a quadrantal angle (on an axis)
  • find the angle from 0^\circ to 360^\circ with the same terminal arm as a clockwise rotation or a rotation past 360^\circ
  • determine the reference angle \theta_R in any quadrant, and work back from \theta_R to \theta
  • find the four angles from 0^\circ to 360^\circ that share one reference angle
  • use reflections of P(a,\ b) in the axes to find related rotation angles
T1 Demonstrate an understanding of angles in standard position [0° to 360°].
Today: standard position, quadrants and quadrantal angles, reference angles and the four angles that share one.
1

Angles in Standard Position

An angle is in standard position when its vertex is at the origin and its initial arm lies along the positive x-axis. The angle is made by rotating a second arm, the terminal arm, away from the initial arm. The amount of turning is the rotation angle, \textcolor{#7a1f3d}{\theta}.
  • A counterclockwise rotation gives a positive angle. One full turn is 360^\circ.
  • A clockwise rotation gives a negative angle, and a rotation of more than 360^\circ goes past a full turn. Either way the terminal arm lands on the same arm as some angle from 0^\circ to 360^\circ. In this course, 0^\circ\le\theta\le360^\circ.
2

Quadrants and Quadrantal Angles

The axes split the plane into four quadrants, numbered counterclockwise from the positive x-axis. An angle whose terminal arm lies on an axis (0^\circ, 90^\circ, 180^\circ, 270^\circ, 360^\circ) is a quadrantal angle; it is not in any quadrant.
Quadrant 1: 0^\circ<\theta<90^\circ
Quadrant 2: 90^\circ<\theta<180^\circ
Quadrant 3: 180^\circ<\theta<270^\circ
Quadrant 4: 270^\circ<\theta<360^\circ

To sketch an angle, start on the positive x-axis, turn counterclockwise, and stop in the right quadrant: halfway through a quadrant is 45^\circ past the axis.

Example 2

Clockwise, and past a full turn

Sketch each rotation. Then state the angle from 0^\circ to 360^\circ that has the same terminal arm.

(a) a clockwise rotation of 135^\circ (that is, -135^\circ)
Turning 135^\circ clockwise stops in quadrant 3, which is the same arm as 360^\circ-135^\circ=\boldsymbol{225^\circ}.
(b) a counterclockwise rotation of 420^\circ
One full turn is 360^\circ; the extra 420^\circ-360^\circ=60^\circ stops in quadrant 1. Same arm as \boldsymbol{60^\circ}.

A point on the terminal arm

If a point P(x,\ y) lies on the terminal arm, the terminal arm runs from the origin through P. The signs of x and y tell you the quadrant: right of the y-axis x>0, left x<0; above the x-axis y>0, below y<0.

Example 3

x>0, y<0: quadrant 4
(c) C(-2,\ -5)
x<0, y<0: quadrant 3
3

Reference Angles

The reference angle, \textcolor{#c27a0e}{\theta_R}, is the acute angle between the terminal arm and the x-axis. It is always positive and between 0^\circ and 90^\circ, and it is always measured to the x-axis, never to the y-axis. In each quadrant, look at how far the terminal arm is from the nearest part of the x-axis (0^\circ, 180^\circ or 360^\circ).
Quadrant 1
\theta_R=\theta
Quadrant 2
\theta_R=180^\circ-\theta
Quadrant 3
\theta_R=\theta-180^\circ
Quadrant 4
\theta_R=360^\circ-\theta
For a quadrantal angle, the arm is on an axis: \theta_R=0^\circ for 0^\circ, 180^\circ, 360^\circ (on the x-axis) and \theta_R=90^\circ for 90^\circ, 270^\circ.
!
To the x-axis, every time. Sketch first, then measure the gap between the terminal arm and the nearest part of the x-axis. If your answer is more than 90^\circ or negative, something went wrong.

Working backwards: from \theta_R to \theta

Knowing the reference angle and where the terminal arm lies fixes the rotation angle. Find where that quadrant meets the x-axis (0^\circ, 180^\circ or 360^\circ), turn \theta_R from there into the quadrant, then read \theta from the positive x-axis.

Four Angles with the Same Reference Angle

Turning the same acute angle away from the x-axis in each quadrant gives four angles with one reference angle. Rearranging the formulas from Section 3:
For a reference angle \textcolor{#c27a0e}{\theta_R}, the angles from 0^\circ to 360^\circ are
\theta_R (Q1)  ·  180^\circ-\theta_R (Q2)  ·  180^\circ+\theta_R (Q3)  ·  360^\circ-\theta_R (Q4)

5

Reflecting a Point in the Axes

Reflecting the terminal arm in an axis keeps the reference angle and moves the arm to another quadrant. If P(a,\ b) with a,\ b>0 is on the terminal arm of \theta in quadrant 1:
reflect in the y-axis:(-a,\ b)on the arm of 180^\circ-\theta (Q2)
reflect in both axes (through the origin):(-a,\ -b)on the arm of 180^\circ+\theta (Q3)
reflect in the x-axis:(a,\ -b)on the arm of 360^\circ-\theta (Q4)

\boldsymbol{Q(-3,\ 5)},   \boldsymbol{R(-3,\ -5)},   \boldsymbol{S(3,\ -5)}
(b) State the rotation angle whose terminal arm passes through each of Q, R and S.
All have \theta_R=59^\circ.
Q (Q2): 180^\circ-59^\circ=\boldsymbol{121^\circ}   R (Q3): 180^\circ+59^\circ=\boldsymbol{239^\circ}   S (Q4): 360^\circ-59^\circ=\boldsymbol{301^\circ}
6

Common Mistakes with Reference Angles

Most wrong reference angles come from measuring to the y-axis, or from using the formula for the wrong quadrant. A quick sketch prevents both: the answer must be the gap between the arm and the x-axis, and it must be between 0^\circ and 90^\circ.
Example 8

Who is correct?

Three students found the reference angle for 251^\circ. Ana said 19^\circ, Ben said 109^\circ and Cole said 71^\circ. Who is correct? Explain each mistake with a sketch.

251^\circ is in quadrant 3, so \theta_R=251^\circ-180^\circ=71^\circ. Cole is correct.
Ana measured to the negative y-axis: 270^\circ-251^\circ=19^\circ (dotted arc).
Ben used the quadrant 4 formula: 360^\circ-251^\circ=109^\circ, which is not even acute.
✓

Key ideas

Standard position. Vertex at the origin, initial arm on the positive x-axis. Counterclockwise is positive. A clockwise turn or a turn past 360^\circ lands on the arm of an angle from 0^\circ to 360^\circ: -135^\circ\to225^\circ, 420^\circ\to60^\circ.
Quadrants. Q1 0^\circ–90^\circ, Q2 90^\circ–180^\circ, Q3 180^\circ–270^\circ, Q4 270^\circ–360^\circ. On an axis: quadrantal. For a point, the signs of x and y name the quadrant.
Reference angle \theta_R: the acute angle to the x-axis. Q1 \theta · Q2 180^\circ-\theta · Q3 \theta-180^\circ · Q4 360^\circ-\theta. Never to the y-axis, never over 90^\circ.
One \theta_R, four angles: \theta_R, 180^\circ-\theta_R, 180^\circ+\theta_R, 360^\circ-\theta_R. Reflecting P(a,\ b) in the y-axis, both axes or the x-axis gives those Q2, Q3, Q4 angles.
