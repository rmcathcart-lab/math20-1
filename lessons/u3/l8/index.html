The Bear Walk

A bear walks 1 km south, then 1 km east, then 1 km north. It ends up exactly where it started.

What colour is the bear?

?
How could that walk end where it started?
White: it is a polar bear. Start at the North Pole. From the pole, every direction is south; walking “east” keeps you 1 km from the pole, on a circle around it; walking north takes you straight back to the pole. (There are also starting points near the South Pole where the walk works, but no bears live there.)

Choosing the Sine Law or the Cosine Law

We now have three tools for triangles: primary ratios, the sine law and the cosine law. Today we decide which one fits by looking at what is known, then use them together: in bearings problems, with two triangles, in circles, and to find areas.

Choosing a Method

Before solving, look at what is known in the triangle that contains the unknown. A full pair is an angle together with the side opposite it.
Only angles (AAA)
The shape is known but not the size: no side can be found.
Cosine law, then sine law
Once the cosine law gives one missing part, a full pair is known, so the (easier) sine law usually finishes the triangle.
Finding an angle?
The cosine law is safer: it is never ambiguous, and a negative cosine shows an obtuse angle.
Example 1

Which method?

For each, name the most appropriate method and justify the choice. Do not solve.

(a) \triangle ABC: \angle C=90^\circ, \angle A=34^\circ, c=12; find b
Right triangle: primary ratios (b=12\cos34^\circ).
(b) \triangle DEF: d=7, e=9, \angle F=52^\circ; find f
SAS (the angle between the sides): cosine law.
(c) \triangle GHJ: \angle G=41^\circ, g=10, h=13; find \angle H
Full pair \angle G, g: sine law. SSA, so check for two answers.
(d) \triangle KLM: k=5, l=8, m=11; find the largest angle
SSS: cosine law for \angle M (opposite the longest side).
(e) \triangle PQR: \angle P=50^\circ, \angle Q=60^\circ, \angle R=70^\circ; find p
AAA: cannot be solved (any size has these angles).
(f) \triangle STU: \angle S=35^\circ, \angle T=105^\circ, s=8; find u
\angle U=40^\circ, then full pair \angle S, s: sine law.

Clock hands make a triangle

The two hands of a clock and the line joining their tips form a triangle. The 12 hour marks are 360^\circ\div12=30^\circ apart. The minute hand moves 6^\circ each minute, and the hour hand moves 0.5^\circ each minute (halfway between marks at the half hour).

Example 2

The hands of a clock

The minute hand of a clock is 9 cm long and the hour hand is 6 cm long.

(a) Find the angle between the hands at 4:00, and the distance between their tips to the nearest tenth.
At 4:00 the hands are 4 marks apart: 4\times30^\circ=120^\circ. SAS, so the cosine law:
d^2=9^2+6^2-2(9)(6)\cos120^\circ=81+36+54=171, d\approx\boldsymbol{13.1} cm
(b) Repeat for 2:30.
Minute hand at 6 (180^\circ from 12). Hour hand halfway between 2 and 3: 2.5\times30^\circ=75^\circ from 12. Angle =180^\circ-75^\circ=105^\circ.
d^2=81+36-108\cos105^\circ\approx144.95, d\approx\boldsymbol{12.0} cm

Bearings and Directions

A bearing gives a direction as an angle measured clockwise from north, written with three digits: east is 090^\circ, south is 180^\circ, west is 270^\circ. The bearing of B from A is measured at A.
A direction can also be written from north or south toward east or west: \text{S}\,40^\circ\,\text{E} means “face south, turn 40^\circ toward east” (the same as 140^\circ).
The back bearing (the bearing of A from B) differs by 180^\circ: if B is on a bearing of 065^\circ from A, then A is on a bearing of 245^\circ from B. North lines at different points are parallel, so alternate and co-interior angles give the angles inside a triangle.
bearing 140^\circ=\text{S}\,40^\circ\,\text{E}

From bearings to the angle inside a triangle

Draw a north line at every point where a direction is measured. Two paths from the same point: the angle between them is the difference of the bearings. A path that turns: at the turn, the back bearing points back along the first leg, and the angle inside the triangle is between the back bearing and the new bearing.

Problems with Two Triangles

When the unknown is in a triangle that does not have enough information, solve a neighbouring triangle first to get a shared side or angle. Mark every known value on the sketch and look for supplementary angles along straight lines (180^\circ) and right angles.
Example 6

The radio mast

From point A on level ground, the angle of elevation to the top T of a radio mast is 28^\circ. After walking 40 m straight toward the mast to point B, the angle of elevation is 47^\circ. Find the height of the mast, to the nearest tenth of a metre.

In \triangle ABT: \angle ABT=180^\circ-47^\circ=133^\circ, \angle ATB=180^\circ-28^\circ-133^\circ=19^\circ.
Full pair (19^\circ, 40 m): BT=\dfrac{40\sin28^\circ}{\sin19^\circ}\approx57.68 m
Right \triangle BFT: TF=BT\sin47^\circ\approx42.2 m
The mast is about 42.2 m tall.
Example 7

The garden plot

A garden plot ABCD is split by the diagonal path AC. AB=7.0 m, BC=9.5 m, \angle ABC=112^\circ, CD=6.0 m and \angle ADC=80^\circ. Find the length of AC, the length of AD and the perimeter of the plot, to the nearest tenth of a metre.

\triangle ABC (SAS): AC^2=7^2+9.5^2-2(7)(9.5)\cos112^\circ\approx189.07, AC\approx\boldsymbol{13.8} m
\triangle ACD: full pair (\angle D=80^\circ, AC), so the sine law:
\sin\angle CAD=\dfrac{6.0\sin80^\circ}{13.75}\approx0.4297, \angle CAD\approx25.4^\circ (only acute: CD<AC)
\angle ACD=180^\circ-80^\circ-25.4^\circ\approx74.6^\circ
AD=\dfrac{13.75\sin74.6^\circ}{\sin80^\circ}\approx\boldsymbol{13.5} m
Perimeter \approx7.0+9.5+6.0+13.46\approx\boldsymbol{36.0} m

Triangles in Circles

Radii of the same circle are equal, so a chord and two radii form an isosceles triangle. Useful facts from earlier courses:
Chord
The perpendicular from the centre to a chord bisects the chord.
Inscribed angle
The central angle is twice the inscribed angle on the same arc. Inscribed angles on the same arc are equal. (An angle inscribed in a semicircle is 90^\circ.)
Tangent
A tangent is perpendicular to the radius at the point of tangency.
Example 8

A chord and two tangents

A circle with centre O has radius 6.5 cm. Chord AB is 10.0 cm long, and C is a point on the major arc. The tangents at A and B meet at T. Round angles to the nearest degree and lengths to the nearest tenth.

(a) Find \angle AOB.
SSS: \cos\angle AOB=\dfrac{6.5^2+6.5^2-10^2}{2(6.5)(6.5)}\approx-0.1834, \angle AOB\approx100.6^\circ\approx\boldsymbol{101^\circ}
(b) Find \angle ACB.
Inscribed angle on the same arc: \angle ACB=\tfrac12(100.6^\circ)\approx\boldsymbol{50^\circ}
(c) Find the distance from O to the chord.
The perpendicular bisects AB: half-chord 5.0. d=\sqrt{6.5^2-5^2}\approx\boldsymbol{4.2} cm
(d) Find \angle ATB and the length TA.
\angle OAT=\angle OBT=90^\circ (tangent \perp radius). In quadrilateral OATB:
\angle ATB=360^\circ-90^\circ-90^\circ-100.6^\circ\approx\boldsymbol{79^\circ}
Right \triangle OAT with \angle AOT=\tfrac12(100.6^\circ)=50.3^\circ: TA=6.5\tan50.3^\circ\approx\boldsymbol{7.8} cm

Area and Perimeter of a Triangle

In \triangle ABC, the height from B to side b is a\sin C, so \text{Area}=\tfrac12(\text{base})(\text{height})=\tfrac12ab\sin C. This uses two sides and the angle between them (SAS); for SSS, find an angle with the cosine law first.
\text{Area}=\tfrac12ab\sin C=\tfrac12bc\sin A=\tfrac12ac\sin B   (two sides and the contained angle)
Example 9

(a) A triangular lot has two sides of 42 m and 55 m with an angle of 74^\circ between them. Find its area (nearest square metre) and its perimeter (nearest tenth of a metre).

Key ideas

Choose the tool. Right triangle: primary ratios. A full pair (angle + opposite side): sine law (check the ambiguous case for an angle). SAS or SSS: cosine law. AAA: no size, can’t be solved.
Bearings. Clockwise from north, three digits: 140^\circ=\text{S}\,40^\circ\,\text{E}. Back bearing: \pm180^\circ. Draw a north line at every point; at a turn, the inside angle is between the back bearing and the new bearing.
Two triangles. Solve the triangle with enough information first; carry the shared side or angle across. Look for 180^\circ on straight lines and right angles. Keep unrounded values (Ans).
Circles and area. Radii are equal (isosceles); central =2\times inscribed; tangent \perp radius. \text{Area}=\tfrac12ab\sin C with the angle between the sides.
