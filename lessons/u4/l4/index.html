Math 20-1 · Unit 4 Factoring and Equations · Lesson 4

Differences of Squares, Including a^2(f(x))^2-b^2(g(y))^2

When one perfect square is subtracted from another, the expression factors into two brackets that differ only in the sign between their terms: \textcolor{#a33357}{A}^2-\textcolor{#c27a0e}{B}^2=(\textcolor{#a33357}{A}-\textcolor{#c27a0e}{B})(\textcolor{#a33357}{A}+\textcolor{#c27a0e}{B}). The squares can hold fractions, higher powers and even whole binomials.

I can…
  • recognize a difference of squares (two terms, both perfect squares, subtracted) and factor it as (A-B)(A+B)
  • explain why a sum of squares such as x^2+36 does not factor
  • take out the GCF first (a negative one if the first term is negative)
  • factor differences of squares with fractions and decimals as coefficients
  • keep factoring when a factor is again a difference of squares, as in x^4-81
  • factor a^2(f(x))^2-b^2(g(y))^2 by keeping the brackets while I substitute back
  • choose a method with the factoring checklist, including grouping three terms into a perfect square
RF1 Factor polynomial expressions of the form ax^2+bx+c, a^2x^2-b^2y^2, a(f(x))^2+b(f(x))+c and a^2(f(x))^2-b^2(g(y))^2 (a, b and c rational).
Today: differences of squares, with fractions, higher powers and squares of binomials, and a checklist for choosing a method (no calculator).
Warm-up

The Chocolate Trade

You have a square chocolate bar. A friend offers a trade: a bar that is a few rows shorter but the same number of columns longer.

1
How many pieces are in a 4 by 4 bar? In a 3 by 5 bar?
16 and 15. One row shorter and one column longer, but one piece fewer.
2
Press Make the trade on the 4 by 4 bar. The top row turns to become the new column. What is left over?
One piece, in the corner: 4^2-1^2=3\times5.
!
The big one. Trade your 20 by 20 bar for a 17 by 23 bar. How many pieces do you lose? Guess first.
A None: 3 less, 3 more
B 3 pieces
C 6 pieces
D 9 pieces
D, 9 pieces. 20\times20=400 but 17\times23=391. Cut the top 3 rows off and turn them into 3 new columns: a 3 by 3 square is left in the corner. 20^2-3^2=(20-3)(20+3).
You always lose a square of chocolate: a square minus a square is a rectangle. That is today’s lesson.

Challenge your neighbour: trade a 30 by 30 bar for a 25 by 35 bar. How many pieces do you lose? No multiplying allowed!

1

The Difference of Squares Pattern

Multiply two binomials that differ only in the sign between their terms:

(A-B)(A+B)=A^2+AB-AB-B^2=A^2-B^2

The middle terms cancel. Read backwards, this is a factoring pattern.

Difference of squares
\textcolor{#a33357}{A}^2-\textcolor{#c27a0e}{B}^2=(\textcolor{#a33357}{A}-\textcolor{#c27a0e}{B})(\textcolor{#a33357}{A}+\textcolor{#c27a0e}{B})
It applies when there are two terms, both are perfect squares, and they are subtracted. Find A and B by taking the square root of each term: 49x^2=(7x)^2, 25y^2=(5y)^2, so 49x^2-25y^2=(7x-5y)(7x+5y).

Why it works: cut a square out of a square

Take a square of side \textcolor{#a33357}{A} and cut a square of side \textcolor{#c27a0e}{B} out of its corner. The area left is A^2-B^2. Cut that L shape in two and slide one piece round: it becomes a rectangle.

Think In Unit 2 you multiplied conjugates: (3-\sqrt{2})(3+\sqrt{2}). Why does the answer have no radical in it?
Conjugates are (A-B)(A+B) with A=3, B=\sqrt{2}. The middle terms cancel and only the squares are left: 3^2-(\sqrt{2})^2=9-2=\boldsymbol{7}.

A sum of squares does not factor

A sum of squares A^2+B^2 does not factor over the real numbers. For x^2+36 we would need two numbers with sum 0 and product +36; numbers with sum 0 are opposites, and opposites have a negative product.

Is it a difference of squares? Find A and B.

Check the three conditions, then take the square root of each term. If the negative term comes first, reorder the terms.

Example 1

Factor, if possible

Factor, if possible.

(a) 25x^2-64
(5x)^2-8^2=\boldsymbol{(5x-8)(5x+8)}
(b) 49a^2-81b^2
\boldsymbol{(7a-9b)(7a+9b)}
(c) 100m^2n^2-9p^2
(10mn)^2-(3p)^2
=\boldsymbol{(10mn-3p)(10mn+3p)}
(d) x^2+36
A sum of squares: it does not factor.
(e) -y^2+4x^2
Reorder: 4x^2-y^2=\boldsymbol{(2x-y)(2x+y)}
(f) 1-\sin^2x
1^2-(\sin x)^2=\boldsymbol{(1-\sin x)(1+\sin x)}
2

Common Factor First

Always look for a GCF first. Numbers like 12 and 27 are not perfect squares, but after removing the common factor 3 the binomial is 4x^2-9y^2. If the first term is negative, taking out a negative common factor makes the pattern easier to see.
Example 2

GCF, then the pattern

Factor fully.

(a) 12x^2-27y^2
3(4x^2-9y^2)=\boldsymbol{3(2x-3y)(2x+3y)}
(b) 5a^3b-45ab^3
5ab(a^2-9b^2)=\boldsymbol{5ab(a-3b)(a+3b)}
(c) 8x^2+50
\boldsymbol{2(4x^2+25)}. The sum of squares does not factor, so this is fully factored.
(d) -18x^2+8
-2(9x^2-4)=\boldsymbol{-2(3x-2)(3x+2)}
(or 2(2-3x)(2+3x))

Dimensions from a factored area

If the area of a rectangle factors into two binomials, they can be its length and width. In the perimeter the opposite terms cancel, which often hands you one variable straight away.

Example 3

The banner

A rectangular banner has an area of 36x^2-25y^2 square centimetres. Its length and width are both of the form ax+by, where a and b are integers.

(a) Write expressions for the length and the width.
36x^2-25y^2=(6x+5y)(6x-5y). Length \boldsymbol{6x+5y}, width \boldsymbol{6x-5y} (with x,y>0).
(b) The perimeter is 96 cm. Determine x.
2(6x+5y)+2(6x-5y)=24x=96
\Rightarrow\boldsymbol{x=4}
(c) The length is also 3 times the width. Determine y and the dimensions of the banner.
24+5y=3(24-5y)
\Rightarrow24+5y=72-15y\Rightarrow20y=48\Rightarrow\boldsymbol{y=2.4}
Length 24+12=\boldsymbol{36} cm, width 24-12=\boldsymbol{12} cm.
(Check: 36\times12=432=36(16)-25(5.76).)
3

Rational Coefficients

A fraction is a perfect square when its numerator and denominator are both perfect squares: \dfrac{1}{4}x^2=\left(\dfrac{1}{2}x\right)^2 and \dfrac{y^2}{25}=\left(\dfrac{y}{5}\right)^2. Decimals work too: 0.09=0.3^2. Another way is to take out a fractional common factor first, as in Lesson 2.
Example 4

Fractions and decimals

Factor fully.

(a) \dfrac{1}{4}x^2-9 (two ways)
\boldsymbol{\left(\tfrac{1}{2}x-3\right)\left(\tfrac{1}{2}x+3\right)}
or \tfrac{1}{4}(x^2-36)=\boldsymbol{\tfrac{1}{4}(x-6)(x+6)}
(the same: \tfrac{1}{2}(x-6)=\tfrac{1}{2}x-3)
(b) \dfrac{x^2}{16}-\dfrac{y^2}{25}
\boldsymbol{\left(\dfrac{x}{4}-\dfrac{y}{5}\right)\left(\dfrac{x}{4}+\dfrac{y}{5}\right)}
(c) 0.09a^2-0.49
\boldsymbol{(0.3a-0.7)(0.3a+0.7)}
4

Higher Powers and Repeated Factoring

Even powers are perfect squares: x^4=(x^2)^2, x^6=(x^3)^2, 16y^4=(4y^2)^2. After one use of the pattern, the difference factor may be a difference of squares again, so keep going. The sum factor stays.
!
Odd powers are not squares. x^3-7 is not a difference of squares: x^3 is not a perfect square (and neither is 7). Stop there.
Example 5

Keep going

Factor fully.

(a) x^4-81
(x^2-9)(x^2+9)
=\boldsymbol{(x-3)(x+3)(x^2+9)}
(b) 16a^4-b^4
(4a^2-b^2)(4a^2+b^2)
=\boldsymbol{(2a-b)(2a+b)(4a^2+b^2)}
(c) 3x^4-48y^4
3(x^4-16y^4)=3(x^2-4y^2)(x^2+4y^2)
=\boldsymbol{3(x-2y)(x+2y)(x^2+4y^2)}
(d) m^8-1
(m^4-1)(m^4+1)=(m^2-1)(m^2+1)(m^4+1)
=\boldsymbol{(m-1)(m+1)(m^2+1)(m^4+1)}
(e) x^6-49
\boldsymbol{(x^3-7)(x^3+7)}
5

Squares of Binomials: a^2(f(x))^2-b^2(g(y))^2

The squared quantities can be binomials. Let \textcolor{#a33357}{A}=a\,f(x) and \textcolor{#c27a0e}{B}=b\,g(y), use (A-B)(A+B), then keep the brackets while you substitute back (the minus sign applies to every term of B), simplify each factor and take out any common factor.
4(2x-1)^2-9(y+2)^2=\big[\textcolor{#a33357}{2(2x-1)}\big]^2-\big[\textcolor{#c27a0e}{3(y+2)}\big]^2\qquad A=2(2x-1),\ B=3(y+2)
Example 6

Binomials inside the squares

Factor fully.

(a) (x+3)^2-25
\big[(x+3)-5\big]\big[(x+3)+5\big]=\boldsymbol{(x-2)(x+8)}
(b) 16-(y-1)^2
\big[4-(y-1)\big]\big[4+(y-1)\big]=\boldsymbol{(5-y)(y+3)}
(c) 4(2x-1)^2-9(y+2)^2
\big[2(2x-1)-3(y+2)\big]\big[2(2x-1)+3(y+2)\big]
=(4x-2-3y-6)(4x-2+3y+6)
=\boldsymbol{(4x-3y-8)(4x+3y+4)}
(d) (3x+1)^2-(x-5)^2
\big[(3x+1)-(x-5)\big]\big[(3x+1)+(x-5)\big]
=(2x+6)(4x-4)=2(x+3)\cdot4(x-1)
=\boldsymbol{8(x+3)(x-1)}

The patio: the square cut from a square, with binomial sides

The picture from Section 1 works with binomial sides too. Here \textcolor{#a33357}{A}=2x+5 and \textcolor{#c27a0e}{B}=x+1.

Example 7

The patio

A square patio has side length (2x+5) m. A square planter with side length (x+1) m is cut out of it.

(a) Write an expression for the area of the patio that remains.
\boldsymbol{(2x+5)^2-(x+1)^2}
(b) Factor it fully using a difference of squares.
\big[(2x+5)-(x+1)\big]\big[(2x+5)+(x+1)\big]
=(x+4)(3x+6)=\boldsymbol{3(x+4)(x+2)}
(c) Check by expanding each square first, then factoring.
4x^2+20x+25-(x^2+2x+1)
=3x^2+18x+24=3(x^2+6x+8)
=3(x+2)(x+4)\ \checkmark
(d) Find the remaining area when x=3.
3(7)(5)=\boldsymbol{105\text{ m}^2}
(Check: 11^2-4^2=121-16=105.)
6

Choosing a Method

Factoring checklist
  1. Take out the GCF (a negative one if the first term is negative).
  2. Count the terms.
    Two: a difference of squares? (A sum of squares does not factor.)
    Three: x^2+bx+c; ax^2+bx+c by decomposition; a perfect square; quadratic form (substitution).
    Four: grouping (two and two, or three and one to make a difference of squares).
  3. Check every factor and factor again if you can.   4. Check by expanding.
A binomial is a factor of a polynomial if it appears in the polynomial’s fully factored form (or is a product of factors that appear there).
Example 8

Use the checklist

Factor fully.

(a) 2x^3-18x
2x(x^2-9)=\boldsymbol{2x(x-3)(x+3)}
(b) x^3+2x^2-9x-18
x^2(x+2)-9(x+2)
=(x+2)(x^2-9)
=\boldsymbol{(x+2)(x-3)(x+3)}
(c) 4x^4-37x^2+9
Product 36, sum -37: -36, -1
=(4x^2-1)(x^2-9)
=\boldsymbol{(2x-1)(2x+1)(x-3)(x+3)}
(d) 6x^2-7x-20
Product -120, sum -7: -15, 8
=3x(2x-5)+4(2x-5)
=\boldsymbol{(3x+4)(2x-5)}
(e) x^2-6x+9-y^2
Group the first three: (x-3)^2-y^2
=\boldsymbol{(x-3-y)(x-3+y)}
(f) 3a^2+12
\boldsymbol{3(a^2+4)}; the sum of squares does not factor.

Is it a factor? Factor fully, then look.

Write the polynomial in fully factored form. A binomial is a factor if it is one of the pieces, or a product of pieces.

Example 9

Factor or not?

Determine whether each binomial is a factor of 3x^4-48.   (a) x-2    (b) x-4    (c) x^2+4

3x^4-48=3(x^4-16)=3(x^2-4)(x^2+4)=3(x-2)(x+2)(x^2+4).
(a) Yes.   (b) No: x-4 is not in the factored form.   (c) Yes.
✓

Key ideas

The pattern. A^2-B^2=(A-B)(A+B): two terms, both perfect squares, subtracted. Find A and B with square roots. A sum of squares A^2+B^2 does not factor.
GCF first. 12x^2-27y^2=3(4x^2-9y^2). Negative first term: take out a negative. Fractions: \tfrac{1}{4}x^2=\left(\tfrac{1}{2}x\right)^2, decimals: 0.09=0.3^2.
Keep going. Even powers are squares. x^4-81=(x^2-9)(x^2+9)=(x-3)(x+3)(x^2+9): the difference opens again, the sum stays.
Binomials inside. Keep the brackets: \big[(x+3)-5\big]\big[(x+3)+5\big]; the minus hits every term of B. Simplify, take out a GCF, use the checklist, then check.
