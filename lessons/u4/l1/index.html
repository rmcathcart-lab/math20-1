Factoring Review: Common Factors and x^2+bx+c

Factoring writes a sum or difference of terms as a product: it is expanding run backwards. Today we take out common factors (numbers, variables, even whole brackets), group four terms in pairs, and split x^2+\textcolor{#12805a}{b}x+\textcolor{#c27a0e}{c} into two brackets, always factoring fully.

I can…
  • take out the greatest common factor (GCF), including a negative GCF when the leading term is negative
  • take out a common binomial factor, and match opposite brackets such as (2-b) and (b-2)
  • factor a polynomial with four terms by grouping
  • factor x^2+bx+c and x^2+bxy+cy^2 with two integers that multiply to c and add to b
  • factor fully (GCF first), and show that a trinomial does not factor over the integers
  • decide whether a binomial is a factor of a polynomial
RF1 Factor polynomial expressions of the form ax^2+bx+c, a^2x^2-b^2y^2, a(f(x))^2+b(f(x))+c and a^2(f(x))^2-b^2(g(y))^2 (a, b and c rational).

Warm-up

The Mind-Reading Trick

Think of a whole number n. Square it, add 11 times your number, then add 30. Tell me only the answer, and I will tell you your number.

Your number n:
1
Try n=1, then n=5. What comes out?
1+11+30=42 and 25+55+30=110. Notice: 42=6\times7 and 110=10\times11.
2
Try n=10. Is the answer two numbers in a row multiplied?
100+110+30=240=15\times16. Yes: always (n+5) times (n+6).
!
The big one. Your friend does the trick and gets 182. What was their number? Guess first.
A 8
B 13
C 14
D 7
A, 8. 182=13\times14, two numbers in a row. They are n+5 and n+6, so n=8. Check: 64+88+30=182.
The secret: n^2+11n+30=(n+5)(n+6). The dots always make a rectangle. Turning a sum into a product is factoring.

Make your own trick: what product is n^2+3n+2 always equal to? Test it on your neighbour.

1

The Greatest Common Factor

Factoring writes a sum or difference of terms as a product. It is the reverse of expanding. The first step in every factoring question is to look for the greatest common factor (GCF): the largest number and the highest power of each variable that divide every term. Write the GCF in front and divide each term by it to get what goes in the brackets.
\underbrace{6x^3-15x^2}_{\text{sum or difference}}=\underbrace{\textcolor{#a33357}{3x^2}(2x-5)}_{\text{product}}    Check by expanding: 3x^2(2x)-3x^2(5)=6x^3-15x^2\ \checkmark

When the first term is negative, it is usually neater to take out a negative common factor. Every sign inside the brackets changes: -4x^2+10x=\textcolor{#a33357}{-2x}(2x-5).

Example 1

Take out the GCF

Factor by taking out the GCF.

(a) 12x^3-18x^2
GCF =6x^2
12x^3-18x^2=\boldsymbol{6x^2(2x-3)}
(b) 10a^4b^2+15a^2b^3-5a^2b
GCF =5a^2b
=\boldsymbol{5a^2b(2a^2b+3b^2-1)}
(the last term leaves 1, not 0)
(c) -4m^3+12m^2-8m
Leading term negative: take out -4m.
=-4m(m^2-3m+2)
=\boldsymbol{-4m(m-1)(m-2)} (Section 4)
!
A term that is all GCF leaves 1, not 0. \dfrac{-5a^2b}{5a^2b}=-1, so the bracket ends in -1. Expanding is the check: three terms in, three terms out.
2

A Binomial as the Common Factor

A common factor can be a whole bracket. In 4x\textcolor{#7b4fc4}{(x-3)}+7\textcolor{#7b4fc4}{(x-3)} both terms contain \textcolor{#7b4fc4}{(x-3)}, so take it out just like a monomial: \textcolor{#7b4fc4}{(x-3)}(4x+7). Brackets that are opposites, like (2-b) and (b-2), are not the same yet: use 2-b=-(b-2) to make them match.
Example 2

A common bracket

Factor.

(a) 5y(y+6)-2(y+6)
Common factor (y+6):
=\boldsymbol{(y+6)(5y-2)}
(b) 3x(2x-1)+(2x-1)
The second term is 1(2x-1):
=\boldsymbol{(2x-1)(3x+1)}
(c) a(b-2)+7(2-b)
7(2-b)=-7(b-2)
=a(b-2)-7(b-2)
=\boldsymbol{(b-2)(a-7)}
3

Factoring by Grouping

A polynomial with four terms often has no common factor in all four terms, but does in each pair. Group the terms in pairs, take the GCF out of each pair, and then take out the common bracket. If the brackets do not match, try taking a negative factor out of the second pair, or rearrange the terms.
\underbrace{x^3+4x^2}_{x^2\textcolor{#7b4fc4}{(x+4)}}\ \underbrace{-\,3x-12}_{-3\textcolor{#7b4fc4}{(x+4)}}=x^2\textcolor{#7b4fc4}{(x+4)}-3\textcolor{#7b4fc4}{(x+4)}=\textcolor{#7b4fc4}{(x+4)}(x^2-3)
Example 3

Factor by grouping

Factor by grouping.

(a) 6ab-9a+4b-6
=3a(2b-3)+2(2b-3)
=\boldsymbol{(2b-3)(3a+2)}
(b) xy-5x-2y+10
Take -2 out of the second pair:
=x(y-5)-2(y-5)
=\boldsymbol{(y-5)(x-2)}
(c) 2xy+15+6x+5y
The pairs as written share nothing. Rearrange:
2xy+6x+5y+15
=2x(y+3)+5(y+3)
=\boldsymbol{(y+3)(2x+5)}
!
Signs in the second pair. In -2y+10, taking out +2 gives 2(-y+5), which does not match (y-5). Take out -2: -2(y-5). Check: -2\times(-5)=+10\ \checkmark
4

Factoring x^2+bx+c

Expanding (x+m)(x+n) gives x^2+\textcolor{#12805a}{(m+n)}x+\textcolor{#c27a0e}{mn}. So to factor a trinomial with leading coefficient 1, find two integers m and n that multiply to c and add to b.

With algebra tiles you can see it: an x^2 tile, \textcolor{#12805a}{b} x-tiles and \textcolor{#c27a0e}{c} unit tiles ( red means negative). The trinomial factors exactly when the tiles make a rectangle; its side lengths are the factors.

The product–sum hunt

x^2+\textcolor{#12805a}{b}x+\textcolor{#c27a0e}{c}=(x+m)(x+n)\quad\text{where}\quad \textcolor{#c27a0e}{m\times n=c}\quad\text{and}\quad \textcolor{#12805a}{m+n=b}
Signs: c>0 means m and n have the same sign (the sign of b); c<0 means opposite signs (the larger size takes the sign of b).

List the factor pairs of c in order until one pair has the right sum. Check every answer by expanding. The same method works with two variables: x^2+bxy+cy^2=(x+my)(x+ny).

Example 4

Product and sum

Factor. Check one answer by expanding.

(a) n^2+13n+36
Product 36, sum 13:
4,\ 9
\boldsymbol{(n+4)(n+9)}
(b) x^2-10x+21
Product 21, sum -10:
-3,\ -7
\boldsymbol{(x-3)(x-7)}
(c) y^2+2y-35
Product -35, sum 2:
7,\ -5
\boldsymbol{(y+7)(y-5)}
(d) p^2-9p-36
Product -36, sum -9:
-12,\ 3
\boldsymbol{(p-12)(p+3)}
Check (d) by expanding.
(p-12)(p+3)=p^2+3p-12p-36=p^2-9p-36\ \checkmark
Example 5

Two variables

Factor.

(a) x^2-7xy+12y^2
Product 12, sum -7: -3,\ -4
\boldsymbol{(x-3y)(x-4y)}
(b) a^2+ab-20b^2
Product -20, sum 1: 5,\ -4
\boldsymbol{(a+5b)(a-4b)}
!
Keep the second variable. The pair for (a) is -3 and -4, but the factors are (x-3y)(x-4y), not (x-3)(x-4). Expanding gives the y^2 back: (-3y)(-4y)=12y^2.
5

Factor Fully

To factor fully, keep going until no factor can be factored again. Always take out the GCF first: it makes the numbers smaller. If the x^2 term is negative, take out -1 (or a negative GCF) so the leading coefficient in the brackets is positive.
Factoring checklist
1GCF first (negative if the leading term is negative).
2Count the terms: four terms \to grouping; trinomial x^2+bx+c \to product and sum.
3Check each factor: can it be factored again?
4Check by expanding.

Constant first: ascending order

A trinomial written in ascending order (constant first) can also be factored directly: 35+2x-x^2=(7-x)(5+x). This equals -(x-7)(x+5), since 7-x=-(x-7). Both forms are correct.

Example 6

Factor fully

Factor fully.

(a) 4x^2-8x-60
=4(x^2-2x-15)
=\boldsymbol{4(x-5)(x+3)}
(b) 2a^3+14a^2+24a
=2a(a^2+7a+12)
=\boldsymbol{2a(a+3)(a+4)}
(c) -x^2+2x+48
=-(x^2-2x-48)
=\boldsymbol{-(x-8)(x+6)}  or  \boldsymbol{(8-x)(x+6)}
(d) 5x^2y-15xy^2-50y^3
=5y(x^2-3xy-10y^2)
=\boldsymbol{5y(x-5y)(x+2y)}
6

Trinomials That Do Not Factor, and Testing a Factor

If no pair of integers has product c and sum b, the trinomial does not factor over the integers. Say so, and show the pairs you checked. To decide whether a binomial is a factor of a polynomial, factor the polynomial fully and see whether the binomial appears (a factor such as -(x-7) or (7-x) also counts as x-7).

Is it a factor? Factor fully, then look

Example 7

Does it factor? Is it a factor?

(a) Show that x^2+5x+8 does not factor over the integers.
Pairs with product 8: 1,\ 8 (sum 9); 2,\ 4 (sum 6); -1,\ -8 (sum -9); -2,\ -4 (sum -6).
No pair has sum 5, so it does not factor.
(b) Find all integers b so that x^2+bx+10 can be factored.
b is the sum of a pair with product 10:
1+10=11, 2+5=7, -1-10=-11, -2-5=-7.
\boldsymbol{b=\pm7} or \boldsymbol{\pm11}.
(c) Is x-4 a factor of 2x^2-2x-24?
=2(x^2-x-12)=2(x-4)(x+3)
Yes, x-4 is a factor.
(d) Is x+2 a factor of x^3+2x^2-9x-18?
Grouping: x^2(x+2)-9(x+2)=(x+2)(x^2-9)
Yes, x+2 is a factor.

Factoring and area

An area of x^2+bx+c is a rectangle whose length and width are the two factors. Put in a value of x and you get real dimensions, and length \times width must give the area back.

Example 8

The patio

A rectangular patio has an area of (x^2+11x+28) m². Its length and width are binomials with integer coefficients.

(a) Write expressions for the length and the width.
x^2+11x+28=(x+7)(x+4).
Length \boldsymbol{x+7}, width \boldsymbol{x+4} (m).
(b) Write and simplify an expression for the perimeter.
P=2(x+7)+2(x+4)=\boldsymbol{4x+22} (m)
(c) Find the dimensions, perimeter and area when x=5. Check that length \times width equals the area.
Length 12 m, width 9 m, perimeter 4(5)+22=\boldsymbol{42} m.
Area =5^2+11(5)+28=\boldsymbol{108} m², and 12\times9=108\ \checkmark
✓

Key ideas

GCF first. The largest number and highest power of each variable in every term. Divide each term by it for the bracket (a term that is all GCF leaves 1). Leading term negative: take out a negative GCF and every sign flips.
Common brackets and grouping. 4x(x-3)+7(x-3)=(x-3)(4x+7). Opposites: 2-b=-(b-2). Four terms: GCF of each pair, then the common bracket (try a negative, or rearrange).
x^2+bx+c=(x+m)(x+n) with mn=c, m+n=b. c>0: same signs (sign of b). c<0: opposite signs. Two variables: (x+my)(x+ny). Tiles: the sides of the rectangle.
Factor fully, then check. Can any factor factor again? Expand to check. No pair with product c and sum b: does not factor over the integers. A factor test: factor fully and look (7-x counts as x-7).
