Squares on a Chessboard

A chessboard is an 8 by 8 grid of little squares.

How many squares can you find on a chessboard?

?
How did you count them?
204, not 64! Count the squares of every size: 64 small ones (1\times1), 49 of size 2\times2, 36 of size 3\times3, … down to 1 big one (8\times8).
64+49+36+25+16+9+4+1=204, the sum of the first eight perfect squares.

Before You Square: Binomial Squares and Restrictions

When a radical equation has a variable outside the radical, squaring both sides squares a binomial. Use the pattern for the square of a binomial; never square each term separately.

Key idea
(a+b)^2=a^2+\textcolor{#c27a0e}{2ab}+b^2(a-b)^2=a^2-\textcolor{#c27a0e}{2ab}+b^2(x-2)^2=x^2-4x+4,\ \ \textbf{not}\ \ x^2+4
A radical squared gives its radicand: \left(\sqrt{A}\right)^2=A, and \left(k\sqrt{A}\right)^2=k^2A.
Example 1

Expand and simplify

Expand and simplify. Assume each radicand is non-negative.

(a) (x-3)^2
=\boldsymbol{x^2-6x+9}
(b) (2x+5)^2
=\boldsymbol{4x^2+20x+25}
(c) (6-x)^2
=36-12x+x^2
=\boldsymbol{x^2-12x+36}
(d) \left(2+\sqrt{x}\right)^2
=4+2(2)\sqrt{x}+x
=\boldsymbol{x+4\sqrt{x}+4}
(e) \left(3\sqrt{x}\right)^2
=\boldsymbol{9x}
(f) \left(\sqrt{x+1}-3\right)^2
=(x+1)-6\sqrt{x+1}+9
=\boldsymbol{x+10-6\sqrt{x+1}}

Restrictions: two conditions

A square root is never negative, so in \sqrt{\textcolor{#7b4fc4}{A}}=\textcolor{#1f5bd6}{B} two things must be true: the radicand \textcolor{#7b4fc4}{A}\ge0, and the other side \textcolor{#1f5bd6}{B}\ge0. Isolate the radical first, then write both conditions and combine them.
Example 2

State the restrictions

State the restrictions on x for each equation.

(a) \sqrt{x+12}=x
x+12\ge0\Rightarrow x\ge-12
right side: x\ge0
Together: \boldsymbol{x\ge0}
(b) \sqrt{3x+4}=x-2
3x+4\ge0\Rightarrow x\ge-\tfrac{4}{3}
right side: x-2\ge0\Rightarrow x\ge2
Together: \boldsymbol{x\ge2}
(c) \sqrt{10-3x}+2=4-x
Isolate: \sqrt{10-3x}=2-x
10-3x\ge0\Rightarrow x\le\tfrac{10}{3}
2-x\ge0\Rightarrow x\le2
Together: \boldsymbol{x\le2}

Solving: Isolate, Square, Factor, Check

Solving a radical equation
1 State the restrictions.
2 Isolate the radical.
3 Square both sides.
4 Write the quadratic in standard form (ax^2+bx+c=0), factor, and use the zero product property.
5 Check every root in the original equation.

A root that does not satisfy the original equation is an extraneous root: reject it.

Squaring can create extraneous roots because two different numbers can have the same square: 3^2=(-3)^2, but 3\ne-3. The restrictions predict which roots will fail, but the check by substitution is still required.

Example 3

Solve and verify

Solve \sqrt{x+12}=x and verify.

Restriction (Example 2(a)): x\ge0.
Square: x+12=x^2\Rightarrow x^2-x-12=0\Rightarrow(x-4)(x+3)=0, so x=4 or x=-3.
Check x=4: LS =\sqrt{16}=4, RS =4 ✓
Check x=-3: LS =\sqrt{9}=3, RS =-3. LS \ne RS, so -3 is extraneous (it also breaks x\ge0).
The root is \boldsymbol{x=4}.
Example 4

Solve and verify

Solve and verify.

(a) \sqrt{2x+7}-x=2
Isolate: \sqrt{2x+7}=x+2 (so x\ge-2)
Square: 2x+7=x^2+4x+4
0=x^2+2x-3
0=(x+3)(x-1), so x=-3 or x=1.
x=1: LS =\sqrt{9}-1=2= RS ✓
x=-3: LS =\sqrt{1}+3=4\ne2 (extraneous)
The root is \boldsymbol{x=1}.
(b) 2\sqrt{x+1}=x-2
Restrictions: x\ge-1 and x-2\ge0, so x\ge2.
Square (square the 2 as well): 4(x+1)=x^2-4x+4
0=x^2-8x
0=x(x-8), so x=0 or x=8.
x=8: LS =2\sqrt{9}=6, RS =6 ✓
x=0: LS =2\sqrt{1}=2, RS =-2 (extraneous)
The root is \boldsymbol{x=8}.
(c) 3+\sqrt{2x-3}=2x
Isolate: \sqrt{2x-3}=2x-3. Restrictions: x\ge\tfrac{3}{2} (both conditions give the same thing).
Square: 2x-3=4x^2-12x+9\Rightarrow0=4x^2-14x+12\Rightarrow0=2(2x^2-7x+6)=2(2x-3)(x-2)
So x=\tfrac{3}{2} or x=2.
x=\tfrac{3}{2}: LS =3+\sqrt{0}=3, RS =2\left(\tfrac{3}{2}\right)=3 ✓    x=2: LS =3+\sqrt{1}=4, RS =4 ✓
Both roots are valid: \boldsymbol{x=\tfrac{3}{2}} or \boldsymbol{x=2}. (Squaring does not always create an extraneous root.)

Why Extraneous Roots Appear: A Graph

The roots of \sqrt{\textcolor{#7b4fc4}{A}}=\textcolor{#1f5bd6}{B} are the x-coordinates of the points where the graphs of y=\sqrt{A} and y=B meet. After squaring, A=B^2 is also true when \sqrt{A}=-B, so the squared equation picks up the intersections with y=-B as well. Those are the extraneous roots.

To check on a calculator, graph Y_1= left side and Y_2= right side and use the intersect feature.

Example 5

Algebra, then the graph

(a) Solve \sqrt{x+3}=x-3 algebraically.
Restrictions: x\ge-3 and x\ge3, so x\ge3.
Square: x+3=x^2-6x+9
0=x^2-7x+6=(x-1)(x-6)
x=6: \sqrt{9}=3 and 6-3=3 ✓
x=1: \sqrt{4}=2 but 1-3=-2 (extraneous)
The root is \boldsymbol{x=6}.
(b) The graphs of y=\sqrt{x+3} and y=x-3 are shown. Explain how the graph shows that one root is extraneous.
The graphs meet only once, at (6,3), so the equation has one root. At x=1 the curve is at y=2 and the line is at y=-2: they do not meet. x=1 is where the curve meets y=-(x-3)=3-x (dashed), which is the equation \sqrt{x+3}=-(x-3) that squaring also solved.

Equations Like x+\sqrt{x}=12

When the variable appears both inside and outside the radical, isolate the radical and square as usual. Another way is substitution: let u=\sqrt{x} (so u\ge0 and x=u^2), factor the quadratic in u, reject any negative u, then square to get x.

Example 6

Two ways

Solve x+\sqrt{x}=12 in two ways.

(a) Isolate the radical and square.
\sqrt{x}=12-x (so 0\le x\le12)
x=144-24x+x^2
0=x^2-25x+144=(x-9)(x-16)
x=9: 9+3=12 ✓
x=16: 16+4=20\ne12 (extraneous)
The root is \boldsymbol{x=9}.
(b) Substitute u=\sqrt{x}.
u^2+u=12
u^2+u-12=0
(u+4)(u-3)=0, so u=-4 or u=3.
u=\sqrt{x}\ge0, so reject u=-4.
\sqrt{x}=3\Rightarrow\boldsymbol{x=9}.
(No extraneous root appears this way.)
5

Two Radicals: Squaring Twice

If there are two radicals, isolate one of them (usually the more complicated one) and square. One radical will remain: isolate it and square again. Then solve and check. When you square 1+\sqrt{x}, the middle term \textcolor{#c27a0e}{2\sqrt{x}} is the radical that remains.

Example 7

Square twice

Solve and verify.

(a) \sqrt{x+7}-\sqrt{x}=1
Restrictions: x\ge-7 and x\ge0, so x\ge0.
Isolate: \sqrt{x+7}=1+\sqrt{x}
Square: x+7=1+2\sqrt{x}+x
6=2\sqrt{x}
\sqrt{x}=3
Square again: x=9
Check: \sqrt{16}-\sqrt{9}=4-3=1 ✓
The root is \boldsymbol{x=9}.
(b) \sqrt{x-1}-\sqrt{2x-4}=1
Restrictions: x\ge1 and x\ge2, so x\ge2.
Isolate: \sqrt{x-1}=1+\sqrt{2x-4}
Square: x-1=1+2\sqrt{2x-4}+2x-4
-x+2=2\sqrt{2x-4}
Square again: x^2-4x+4=4(2x-4)
x^2-12x+20=0
(x-2)(x-10)=0, so x=2 or x=10.
x=2: \sqrt{1}-\sqrt{0}=1 ✓
x=10: \sqrt{9}-\sqrt{16}=-1\ne1 (extraneous)
The root is \boldsymbol{x=2}.

Problems Modelled by Radical Equations

Let a variable stand for the unknown, write the radical equation, solve and check it, then answer in a sentence. A root can satisfy the equation and still be inadmissible in the context (a negative length, for example): reject it for that reason, not because it is extraneous.

Rootsatisfies the original equation and makes sense in the problem
Extraneouscame from squaring; does not satisfy the original equation
Inadmissiblesatisfies the equation, but is impossible in the context
Example 8

Problems

(a) A positive number decreased by its square root is 30. Determine the number.
Let the number be x: x-\sqrt{x}=30.
\sqrt{x}=x-30 (so x\ge30) \Rightarrow x=x^2-60x+900\Rightarrow0=x^2-61x+900=(x-25)(x-36)
x=36: 36-6=30 ✓    x=25: 25-5=20\ne30 (extraneous)
The number is 36.
(b) A rectangular cutting board is x cm wide. Its length is 10 cm more than its width, and its diagonal is 20 cm more than its width. Write a radical equation for the diagonal, then determine the dimensions of the board.
Pythagoras: d=\sqrt{x^2+(x+10)^2}, so \sqrt{x^2+(x+10)^2}=x+20.
Square: x^2+x^2+20x+100=x^2+40x+400
x^2-20x-300=0\Rightarrow(x-30)(x+10)=0, so x=30 or x=-10.
x=30: \sqrt{900+1600}=50=30+20 ✓
x=-10: \sqrt{100+0}=10=-10+20 satisfies the equation, but a width cannot be negative: inadmissible.
The board is 30 cm by 40 cm (diagonal 50 cm).
✓

Key ideas

Square the whole side. (x-3)^2=x^2-6x+9: don't lose the middle term. \left(k\sqrt{A}\right)^2=k^2A. Restrictions for \sqrt{A}=B: A\ge0 and B\ge0.
Isolate, square, factor, check. Standard form, factor, zero product property, then check every root in the original. One root or both can be valid.
Where ghosts come from. Squaring also solves \sqrt{A}=-B. On the graph, the extraneous root is where the curve meets the mirror line y=-B, not y=B.
More kinds. x+\sqrt{x}=12: square, or let u=\sqrt{x}\ge0. Two radicals: square twice. In a problem, a root that fails the context is inadmissible, not extraneous.
