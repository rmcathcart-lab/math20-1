Factoring by Substitution: a(f(x))^2+b(f(x))+c

Some big expressions are an ordinary trinomial in disguise. Spot the repeated part, wrap it in a box called \textcolor{#c27a0e}{A}, factor the simple trinomial in \textcolor{#c27a0e}{A}, then unwrap the box and simplify. No calculator today.

I can…
  • recognize a trinomial in quadratic form, such as x^4-7x^2+12=(x^2)^2-7(x^2)+12
  • factor by substitution: let \textcolor{#c27a0e}{A}=f(x), factor, then replace \textcolor{#c27a0e}{A} with f(x)
  • factor fully: take out the GCF first, and finish with a difference of squares when a factor allows it
  • factor quadratic-form trinomials with a leading coefficient that is not 1 by decomposition
  • substitute for a binomial, put it back in brackets and simplify each factor
  • use the same idea for other repeated expressions, like \sin x or two binomials A and B
RF1 Factor polynomial expressions of the form ax² + bx + c, a²x² − b²y², a(f(x))² + b(f(x)) + c and a²(f(x))² − b²(g(y))² (a, b and c rational).
Today: a(f(x))² + b(f(x)) + c, factoring by substitution.

1

Trinomials in Quadratic Form

In x^2+bx+c the powers of x are 2, 1 and 0. Many other trinomials have the same shape: the variable part of the first term is the square of the variable part of the middle term, and the last term is a constant. Such an expression is in quadratic form:

x^4-7x^2+12=(\textcolor{#c27a0e}{x^2})^2-7(\textcolor{#c27a0e}{x^2})+12x^6+2x^3-8=(\textcolor{#c27a0e}{x^3})^2+2(\textcolor{#c27a0e}{x^3})-8

Replace the repeated expression with a single letter, factor the simpler trinomial, then put the expression back. This is factoring by substitution.

Factoring a(f(x))^2+b(f(x))+c by substitution
  1. Find the repeated expression f(x) and write the trinomial as a(\ \cdot\ )^2+b(\ \cdot\ )+c.
  2. Let \textcolor{#c27a0e}{A}=f(x).
  3. Factor a\textcolor{#c27a0e}{A}^2+b\textcolor{#c27a0e}{A}+c.
  4. Replace \textcolor{#c27a0e}{A} with f(x).
  5. Simplify inside each bracket and factor each factor fully.
Example 1

Wrap, factor, unwrap

Factor.

(a) x^4+9x^2+14
Let A=x^2: A^2+9A+14
=(A+2)(A+7)
=\boldsymbol{(x^2+2)(x^2+7)}
(b) y^4-3y^2-28
Let A=y^2: A^2-3A-28
=(A-7)(A+4)
=\boldsymbol{(y^2-7)(y^2+4)}
(c) x^6+4x^3-21
Let A=x^3: A^2+4A-21
=(A+7)(A-3)
=\boldsymbol{(x^3+7)(x^3-3)}
★
Shortcut. With practice the letter A can be skipped: think “two numbers with product 14 and sum 9” and write the x^2 factors directly.
Think Why can’t x^4+5x-6 be factored with A=x^2?
The middle term has x, not x^2. With A=x^2 it would be A^2+5x-6: the box does not appear twice, so it is not in quadratic form.
2

Finishing with a Difference of Squares

After substituting back, a factor such as x^2-9 can often be factored again. Recall the difference of squares pattern from Math 10C (Lesson 4 develops it fully):

A^2-B^2=(A-B)(A+B)
e.g. x^2-9=(x-3)(x+3),   4x^2-1=(2x-1)(2x+1)
A sum of squares such as x^2+9 does not factor. Neither does x^2-7 over the integers (7 is not a perfect square).
To factor fully: take out the GCF first, and check every factor at the end. Keep splitting until no factor can be split again: a factor tree for an expression.
Example 2

Factor fully

Factor fully.

(a) x^4-13x^2+36
=(x^2-4)(x^2-9)
Both factors are differences of squares:
=\boldsymbol{(x-2)(x+2)(x-3)(x+3)}
(b) m^4-5m^2-36
=(m^2-9)(m^2+4)
=\boldsymbol{(m-3)(m+3)(m^2+4)}
(m^2+4 is a sum: it does not factor.)
(c) 3y^5-54y^3+243y
GCF: 3y(y^4-18y^2+81)
=3y(y^2-9)(y^2-9)=3y(y^2-9)^2
=3y\big[(y-3)(y+3)\big]^2
=\boldsymbol{3y(y-3)^2(y+3)^2}
3

Leading Coefficient Not 1: Decomposition

When a\ne1, factor a\textcolor{#c27a0e}{A}^2+b\textcolor{#c27a0e}{A}+c by decomposition (Lesson 2): find two numbers with product ac and sum b, split the middle term, and group. You can also split the middle term without writing A at all.
Example 3

Two ways to the same answer

Factor 3x^4-10x^2-8 fully two ways.

(a) Substitute A=x^2 first.
3A^2-10A-8: product -24, sum -10: -12 and 2.
3A^2-12A+2A-8=3A(A-4)+2(A-4)
=(3A+2)(A-4)=(3x^2+2)(x^2-4)
=\boldsymbol{(3x^2+2)(x-2)(x+2)}
(b) Split -10x^2 directly.
3x^4-12x^2+2x^2-8
=3x^2(x^2-4)+2(x^2-4)
=(x^2-4)(3x^2+2)
=\boldsymbol{(x-2)(x+2)(3x^2+2)}   (same answer)

Hunting for the pair

List the factor pairs of ac in order and stop when the sum is b. The signs tell you where to look: a positive product means both numbers have the sign of b; a negative product means one of each.

Example 4

Leading coefficient not 1

Factor fully.

(a) 9x^4-37x^2+4
A=x^2: 9A^2-37A+4
product 36, sum -37: -36, -1
=9A(A-4)-1(A-4)
=(9x^2-1)(x^2-4)
=\boldsymbol{(3x-1)(3x+1)(x-2)(x+2)}
(b) 6a^2b^2-5ab-6
A=ab: 6A^2-5A-6
product -36, sum -5: -9, 4
=3A(2A-3)+2(2A-3)
=(3A+2)(2A-3)
=\boldsymbol{(3ab+2)(2ab-3)}
(c) 2p^6+5p^3-12
A=p^3: 2A^2+5A-12
product -24, sum 5: 8, -3
=2A(A+4)-3(A+4)
=(2A-3)(A+4)
=\boldsymbol{(2p^3-3)(p^3+4)}
4

Substituting for a Binomial

The repeated expression can be a binomial, as in (\textcolor{#c27a0e}{x+2})^2-3(\textcolor{#c27a0e}{x+2})-10. Let \textcolor{#c27a0e}{A}=x+2, factor, then replace \textcolor{#c27a0e}{A} with (x+2) in brackets, simplify each factor and take out any common factor.
!
Brackets when you unwrap. 2A-1 with A=3x-1 is 2(3x-1)-1=6x-3, not 2\cdot3x-1-1. The whole box gets multiplied.
Example 5

A binomial in the box

Factor fully.

(a) (x+2)^2-3(x+2)-10
Let A=x+2: A^2-3A-10=(A-5)(A+2)
=\big[(x+2)-5\big]\big[(x+2)+2\big]
=\boldsymbol{(x-3)(x+4)}
(b) 2(3x-1)^2+5(3x-1)-3
Let A=3x-1: 2A^2+5A-3=(2A-1)(A+3)
=\big[2(3x-1)-1\big]\big[(3x-1)+3\big]
=(6x-3)(3x+2)
=\boldsymbol{3(2x-1)(3x+2)}   (common factor 3)
Think In (b), why do we look for a common factor after unwrapping, when we already took out the GCF at the start?
The common factor 3 was hidden inside the box: it only appears once 2(3x-1)-1 is simplified to 6x-3. Always check each simplified factor.

Or expand first?

You could instead expand first and factor the result. It gives the same answer but takes longer, and the expanded trinomial usually has bigger numbers.

Example 6

Substitute or expand?

Factor 4(x-1)^2-4(x-1)-3 two ways.

(a) By substitution.
A=x-1: 4A^2-4A-3
product -12, sum -4: -6, 2
=2A(2A-3)+1(2A-3)=(2A+1)(2A-3)
=\big[2(x-1)+1\big]\big[2(x-1)-3\big]
=\boldsymbol{(2x-1)(2x-5)}
(b) By expanding first.
4(x^2-2x+1)-4x+4-3
=4x^2-8x+4-4x+4-3=4x^2-12x+5
product 20, sum -12: -10, -2
=2x(2x-5)-1(2x-5)=\boldsymbol{(2x-1)(2x-5)}
Same answer, more steps.

Perfect squares in quadratic form

A trinomial in quadratic form can be a perfect square: A^2+2kA+k^2=(A+k)^2 and A^2-2kA+k^2=(A-k)^2. The first and last terms are squares and the middle term is twice their product. Its area picture is a square.

Example 7

Perfect squares

A trinomial in quadratic form can be a perfect square. Factor fully.

(a) (2x+1)^2-6(2x+1)+9
A=2x+1: A^2-6A+9=(A-3)^2
=(2x+1-3)^2=(2x-2)^2
=\big[2(x-1)\big]^2=\boldsymbol{4(x-1)^2}
(b) x^4+10x^2+25
A=x^2: A^2+10A+25=(A+5)^2
=\boldsymbol{(x^2+5)^2}
(x^2+5 does not factor.)
5

Other Repeated Expressions

Any expression can play the role of \textcolor{#c27a0e}{A}. In trigonometry, \sin^2x means (\sin x)^2, so 2\sin^2x+\sin x-1 is a trinomial in \sin x. An expression can also contain two repeated binomials, like a\textcolor{#c27a0e}{A}^2+b\textcolor{#c27a0e}{A}\textcolor{#1f5bd6}{B}+c\textcolor{#1f5bd6}{B}^2: factor it as you would 6x^2+xy-2y^2, then substitute both back.
Example 8

Any expression can be the box

Factor fully.

(a) 2\sin^2x+\sin x-1
Let A=\sin x: 2A^2+A-1
product -2, sum 1: 2, -1
=2A(A+1)-1(A+1)=(2A-1)(A+1)
=\boldsymbol{(2\sin x-1)(\sin x+1)}
(b) 2(x+2)^2-3(x+2)(2x-1)-2(2x-1)^2
A=x+2, B=2x-1: 2A^2-3AB-2B^2
=2A^2-4AB+AB-2B^2=(A-2B)(2A+B)
=\big[(x+2)-2(2x-1)\big]\big[2(x+2)+(2x-1)\big]
=(x+2-4x+2)(2x+4+2x-1)
=(-3x+4)(4x+3)=\boldsymbol{-(3x-4)(4x+3)}
✓

Key ideas

Quadratic form. The first term’s variable part is the square of the middle term’s, and the last term is a constant: x^4-7x^2+12=(x^2)^2-7(x^2)+12.
Wrap, factor, unwrap. Let A=f(x), factor aA^2+bA+c (decomposition when a\ne1), then put f(x) back in brackets and simplify each factor.
Factor fully. GCF first. Then check every factor: x^2-9=(x-3)(x+3), but a sum like x^2+9 and x^2-7 do not factor. A common factor can appear after simplifying.
Any repeated chunk. x^2, x^3, ab, x+2, \sin x, or two chunks A and B. Substituting is faster than expanding first.
