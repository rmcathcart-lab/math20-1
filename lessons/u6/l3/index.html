The Midnight Mouse

A mouse finds a block of cheese.

Night 1, it eats \tfrac{1}{2} of the block. Night 2, it eats \tfrac{1}{3} of what is left. Night 3, \tfrac{1}{4} of what is left, and so on, until night 9: \tfrac{1}{10} of what is left.

How much of the block is left the next morning?

?
Guess first, then work it out.
Exactly \tfrac{1}{10} of the block! Each night the mouse leaves \tfrac{1}{2}, then \tfrac{2}{3}, then \tfrac{3}{4}, …, then \tfrac{9}{10} of what was there: \tfrac{1}{2}\cdot\tfrac{2}{3}\cdot\tfrac{3}{4}\cdots\tfrac{9}{10}. Every numerator matches the next denominator, so they all divide out and only \tfrac{1}{10} is left. (After 99 nights: \tfrac{1}{100}.)

Multiplying Rational Expressions

Rational expressions multiply just like fractions: numerator times numerator, denominator times denominator. The trick is to factor everything first, write down the non-permissible values, and divide out the common factors before you multiply, so the answer comes out in simplest form.

  • multiply three rational expressions, and expressions in two variables (restrictions such as x\ne3y)
  • write and simplify an area as a product of rational expressions, and evaluate it exactly

Multiplying Fractions and Monomials

To multiply fractions, multiply the numerators and multiply the denominators. It is easier to divide out common factors first (a factor of any numerator with a factor of any denominator), so the numbers stay small and the answer is already in simplest form. Rational expressions are multiplied the same way.

\dfrac{A}{B}\cdot\dfrac{C}{D}=\dfrac{AC}{BD},\quad B\ne0,\ D\ne0    Divide out common factors before multiplying.

For monomials, use the exponent laws (\dfrac{x^5}{x^2}=x^3). A variable is restricted only if it appears in a denominator: each such variable \ne0.

Example 1

Fractions and monomials

Simplify. State the non-permissible values where there are variables.

(a) \dfrac{14}{15}\times\dfrac{25}{21}
=\dfrac{2\cdot\cancel{7}}{3\cdot\cancel{5}}\times\dfrac{\cancel{5}\cdot5}{3\cdot\cancel{7}}=\boldsymbol{\dfrac{10}{9}}
(b) \dfrac{9}{16}\times\left(-\dfrac{20}{27}\right)
=-\dfrac{\cancel{9}}{\cancel{4}\cdot4}\times\dfrac{\cancel{4}\cdot5}{\cancel{9}\cdot3}=\boldsymbol{-\dfrac{5}{12}}
(c) \dfrac{6x^3}{5y}\cdot\dfrac{10y^4}{9x}
=\dfrac{60x^3y^4}{45xy}=\boldsymbol{\dfrac{4x^2y^3}{3}}
x\ne0,\ y\ne0
(d) \dfrac{-4a^2b}{15c^3}\cdot\dfrac{25bc}{6a^5}
=\dfrac{-100a^2b^2c}{90a^5c^3}=\boldsymbol{-\dfrac{10b^2}{9a^3c^2}}
a\ne0,\ c\ne0 (b is never in a denominator, so it has no restriction)

Multiplying Rational Expressions with Polynomials

With polynomials, you can only divide out common factors, so factor first. A polynomial such as x^2-9 on its own is a rational expression with denominator 1.

To multiply rational expressions
1 · Factor
every numerator and denominator fully.
2 · NPVs
every value that makes any original denominator 0.
3 · Divide out
factors common to any numerator and any denominator.
4 · Multiply
what is left. Leave the answer in factored form.
!
A factor that is divided out still gives an NPV: the original product is undefined there, even though the simplified answer is not.
Example 2

Factor, NPVs, divide out, multiply

Simplify. State the non-permissible values.

(a) \dfrac{x+3}{4x}\cdot\dfrac{10x^2}{(x+3)(x-1)}
NPVs: x\ne0,\ -3,\ 1
=\dfrac{\cancel{(x+3)}\cdot10x^2}{4x\cancel{(x+3)}(x-1)}
=\dfrac{10x^2}{4x(x-1)}=\boldsymbol{\dfrac{5x}{2(x-1)}}
(b) \dfrac{5x-15}{x^2+2x}\cdot\dfrac{3x+6}{x^2-9}
=\dfrac{5(x-3)}{x(x+2)}\cdot\dfrac{3(x+2)}{(x-3)(x+3)}
NPVs: x\ne0,\ -2,\ 3,\ -3
=\boldsymbol{\dfrac{15}{x(x+3)}}
(c) \dfrac{8y^2}{y^2-25}\cdot\dfrac{2y+10}{12y}
=\dfrac{8y^2}{(y-5)(y+5)}\cdot\dfrac{2(y+5)}{12y}
NPVs: y\ne5,\ -5,\ 0
=\dfrac{16y^2}{12y(y-5)}=\boldsymbol{\dfrac{4y}{3(y-5)}}
Example 3

Trinomials

Simplify. State the non-permissible values.

(a) \dfrac{x^2-x-12}{x^2+5x+6}\cdot\dfrac{x^2-4}{x^2-6x+8}

(b) \dfrac{2a^2+7a-4}{a^2-16}\cdot\dfrac{a^2-3a-4}{4a^2-1}

Opposite Factors

From Lesson 2, \dfrac{a-b}{b-a}=-1. When a factor is the opposite of another, such as (7-x) and (x-7), write it as -(x-7) so that the two can divide out, leaving -1. Also factor out -1 (or a negative common factor) when the leading coefficient is negative: 9-a^2=-(a^2-9)=-(a-3)(a+3).

Example 4

Opposite factors

Simplify. State the non-permissible values.

(a) \dfrac{x-7}{x^2+3x}\cdot\dfrac{4x+12}{14-2x}
=\dfrac{x-7}{x(x+3)}\cdot\dfrac{4(x+3)}{2(7-x)}
NPVs: x\ne0,\ -3,\ 7
=\dfrac{x-7}{x(x+3)}\cdot\dfrac{4(x+3)}{-2(x-7)}=\dfrac{4}{-2x}=\boldsymbol{-\dfrac{2}{x}}
(b) \dfrac{9-a^2}{a^2+a-6}\cdot\dfrac{a^2-4a+4}{2a^2-6a}
=\dfrac{-(a-3)(a+3)}{(a+3)(a-2)}\cdot\dfrac{(a-2)^2}{2a(a-3)}
NPVs: a\ne-3,\ 2,\ 0,\ 3
=\boldsymbol{-\dfrac{a-2}{2a}}

Products of Three Expressions and Two Variables

The same steps work for any number of factors: factor all of them, take the NPVs from every denominator, and divide out across the whole product. With two variables, a factor such as x-3y in a denominator gives the restriction x\ne3y; a factor y gives y\ne0.

Example 5

Three expressions, two variables

Simplify. State the non-permissible values.

(a) \dfrac{x^2-4}{2x+6}\cdot\dfrac{x^2+7x+12}{x^2+2x}\cdot\dfrac{4x^2}{x^2+2x-8}
=\dfrac{(x-2)(x+2)}{2(x+3)}\cdot\dfrac{(x+3)(x+4)}{x(x+2)}\cdot\dfrac{4x^2}{(x+4)(x-2)}
NPVs: x\ne-3,\ 0,\ -2,\ -4,\ 2
=\dfrac{4x^2}{2x}=\boldsymbol{2x}
(b) \dfrac{x^2-9y^2}{x^2+xy-2y^2}\cdot\dfrac{x^2-xy}{2x-6y}
=\dfrac{(x-3y)(x+3y)}{(x+2y)(x-y)}\cdot\dfrac{x(x-y)}{2(x-3y)}
Restrictions: x\ne-2y,\ x\ne y,\ x\ne3y
=\boldsymbol{\dfrac{x(x+3y)}{2(x+2y)}}

Problems with Products

Areas and volumes multiply lengths, so a shape whose dimensions are rational expressions has a product of rational expressions as its area. In a context, the variable must also make every length positive.

Example 6

The area of a rectangle

A rectangle has length \dfrac{x^2-9}{4x} cm and width \dfrac{8x^2}{x^2+x-6} cm, where x>3.

\dfrac{x^2-9}{4x}
\dfrac{8x^2}{x^2+x-6}
(a) Write and simplify an expression for its area.
A=\dfrac{(x-3)(x+3)}{4x}\cdot\dfrac{8x^2}{(x+3)(x-2)}
=\dfrac{8x^2(x-3)}{4x(x-2)}=\boldsymbol{\dfrac{2x(x-3)}{x-2}} square centimetres
(NPVs x\ne0,\ -3,\ 2; the context needs x>3 so the length is positive.)
(b) Determine the exact area when x=2\sqrt{3}, in simplest form. (Use Unit 2: rationalize the denominator.)
A=\dfrac{2(2\sqrt{3})(2\sqrt{3}-3)}{2\sqrt{3}-2}=\dfrac{4\sqrt{3}(2\sqrt{3}-3)}{2(\sqrt{3}-1)}=\dfrac{24-12\sqrt{3}}{2(\sqrt{3}-1)}=\dfrac{12-6\sqrt{3}}{\sqrt{3}-1}
=\dfrac{(12-6\sqrt{3})(\sqrt{3}+1)}{(\sqrt{3}-1)(\sqrt{3}+1)}=\dfrac{12\sqrt{3}+12-18-6\sqrt{3}}{2}=\dfrac{6\sqrt{3}-6}{2}=\boldsymbol{3\sqrt{3}-3} square centimetres (\approx2.20)

Key ideas

Fractions and monomials. \tfrac{A}{B}\cdot\tfrac{C}{D}=\tfrac{AC}{BD}. Divide out common factors of any numerator and any denominator first; use the exponent laws. Each variable in a denominator \ne0.
Four steps. (1) Factor everything fully. (2) NPVs from every original denominator, before anything divides out. (3) Divide out common factors. (4) Multiply what is left; leave it factored.
Opposites. (7-x)=-(x-7), so the pair divides out to -1. A negative leading coefficient: take out -1 first, 9-a^2=-(a-3)(a+3).
Bigger products. Three expressions: divide out across the whole product. Two variables: x-3y in a denominator gives x\ne3y. Areas multiply lengths, and every length must be positive.
