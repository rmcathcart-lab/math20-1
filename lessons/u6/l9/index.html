The Hill Climb

A car drives 1 km up a hill road at 30 km/h. Then it drives 1 km down the other side.

How fast must it go down so that its average speed for the whole 2 km is 60 km/h?

?
Make a guess first. Then test it.
It can’t be done! To average 60 km/h (1 km every minute) over 2 km, the whole trip must take 2 minutes. But the climb, 1 km at 30 km/h, already took 2 minutes. There is no time left for the way down, so no speed is fast enough.
The popular guess, 90 km/h, gives an average of only 45 km/h: 2 km in 2+\tfrac{2}{3} minutes.

Problems Involving Rational Equations

Many problems put the unknown in a denominator: a reciprocal \tfrac{1}{n}, a time \tfrac{\text{distance}}{\text{speed}}, or a rate of work \tfrac{1}{\text{time}}. A five-step plan turns the words into a rational equation, and the situation decides which roots to keep.

  • solve number problems with a number and its reciprocal, consecutive integers and fractions

  • handle a current or a wind: one way s+c, the other way s-c

A Plan, and Number Problems

Many problems lead to an equation with the variable in a denominator: a reciprocal, a time \dfrac{\text{distance}}{\text{speed}}, or a rate of work \dfrac{1}{\text{time}}.

Solving a problem with a rational equation
1 Let a variable stand for one unknown and write the other unknowns in terms of it (a table helps).
2 Write the equation from the sentence that links the quantities.
3 State the non-permissible values, multiply every term by the LCD and solve (factor, or use the quadratic formula and round as asked).
4 Reject inadmissible roots: NPVs, negative speeds, times or lengths, and non-integers when the answer must be an integer.
5 Check in the words of the problem and answer the question with units.

The reciprocal of a number n (n\ne0) is \dfrac{1}{n}. Consecutive integers are n,\ n+1,\ \dots; consecutive odd (or even) integers are n,\ n+2,\ \dots

Example 1

Number problems

(a) The sum of a number and twice its reciprocal is \dfrac{11}{3}. Determine the number.
Let the number be n, n\ne0.
n+\dfrac{2}{n}=\dfrac{11}{3}
Multiply by 3n: 3n^2+6=11n
3n^2-11n+6=0
(3n-2)(n-3)=0
\boldsymbol{n=\tfrac{2}{3}} or \boldsymbol{n=3} (both work: 3+\tfrac{2}{3}=\tfrac{11}{3})
(b) The sum of the reciprocals of two consecutive odd integers is \dfrac{12}{35}. Determine the integers.
Let the integers be n and n+2; n\ne0,\ -2.
\dfrac{1}{n}+\dfrac{1}{n+2}=\dfrac{12}{35}
Multiply by 35n(n+2): 35(n+2)+35n=12n(n+2)
70n+70=12n^2+24n\Rightarrow 6n^2-23n-35=0
(n-5)(6n+7)=0, n=5 or n=-\tfrac{7}{6} (not an integer: reject)
The integers are 5 and 7. Check: \tfrac{1}{5}+\tfrac{1}{7}=\tfrac{12}{35} ✓
(c) The denominator of a fraction is 5 more than its numerator. When 3 is added to the numerator and to the denominator, the new fraction is equal to \dfrac{3}{4}. Determine the original fraction.
Let the numerator be n; the denominator is n+5. \dfrac{n+3}{n+8}=\dfrac{3}{4}, n\ne-8.
4(n+3)=3(n+8)\Rightarrow 4n+12=3n+24\Rightarrow n=12
The fraction is \boldsymbol{\dfrac{12}{17}}. Check: \dfrac{15}{20}=\dfrac{3}{4} ✓

Distance, Speed and Time

d=stt=\dfrac{d}{s}s=\dfrac{d}{t}distance d, average speed s, time t
Make a table with a row for each trip and the columns Distance, Speed, Time =\dfrac{\text{Distance}}{\text{Speed}}. Then use the time sentence:
Same timethe times are equal
k hours longerlonger time - shorter time =k
Total timeadd the times
Change minutes to hours: 40\text{ min}=\tfrac{40}{60}=\tfrac{2}{3}\text{ h}.
Example 2

Same time

A cyclist rides 54 km in the same time that a runner covers 24 km. The cyclist’s average speed is 15 km/h greater than the runner’s. Determine each average speed.

Distance (km)Speed (km/h)Time (h)
Cyclist54s+15\dfrac{54}{s+15}
Runner24s\dfrac{24}{s}
Same time: \dfrac{54}{s+15}=\dfrac{24}{s}, s\ne0,\ -15
54s=24(s+15)
54s=24s+360\Rightarrow 30s=360\Rightarrow s=12
Runner: 12 km/h; cyclist: 27 km/h.
Check: \tfrac{54}{27}=2 h and \tfrac{24}{12}=2 h ✓
Example 3

Time saved

A courier drives 160 km to a town. On the way back she drives 20 km/h faster and saves 40 minutes. Determine her speed each way and the time for the return trip.

Distance (km)Speed (km/h)Time (h)
Going160s\dfrac{160}{s}
Returning160s+20\dfrac{160}{s+20}
Going takes longer: \dfrac{160}{s}-\dfrac{160}{s+20}=\dfrac{2}{3}, s\ne0,\ -20
Multiply by 3s(s+20): 480(s+20)-480s=2s(s+20)
9600=2s^2+40s\Rightarrow s^2+20s-4800=0
(s-60)(s+80)=0; s=60 (-80 rejected)
Going 60 km/h (2\tfrac{2}{3} h = 2 h 40 min); returning 80 km/h, taking 2 h.
Example 4

Total time

In a duathlon, Mara cycles 15 km and then runs 6 km. Her running speed is 12 km/h less than her cycling speed, and the race takes her 1.5 h. Determine her cycling and running speeds.

Cycling speed s; running speed s-12. Times: \dfrac{15}{s} and \dfrac{6}{s-12} (s\ne0,\ 12).
Total time: \dfrac{15}{s}+\dfrac{6}{s-12}=\dfrac{3}{2}
Multiply by 2s(s-12): 30(s-12)+12s=3s(s-12)
42s-360=3s^2-36s\Rightarrow 3s^2-78s+360=0\Rightarrow s^2-26s+120=0\Rightarrow(s-6)(s-20)=0
s=6 would make the running speed 6-12=-6 km/h: inadmissible. So s=20.
Cycling 20 km/h, running 8 km/h. Check: \tfrac{15}{20}+\tfrac{6}{8}=0.75+0.75=1.5 h ✓

Current and Wind

A current or a wind adds to the speed in one direction and subtracts from it in the other.

Boat with still-water speed s, current c:
downstream speed s+c,  upstream speed s-c  (s>c)
Plane with airspeed s, wind w:
with a tailwind s+w,  into a headwind s-w
Example 5

Up the river and back

A tour boat travels 24 km upstream and then 24 km back downstream in a total of 5 h. The current flows at 2 km/h. Determine the speed of the boat in still water and the time for each leg.

Distance (km)Speed (km/h)Time (h)
Upstream24s-2\dfrac{24}{s-2}
Downstream24s+2\dfrac{24}{s+2}
\dfrac{24}{s-2}+\dfrac{24}{s+2}=5, s\ne\pm2
24(s+2)+24(s-2)=5(s^2-4)
48s=5s^2-20\Rightarrow 5s^2-48s-20=0
(5s+2)(s-10)=0; s=10 (-\tfrac{2}{5} rejected)
Still water: 10 km/h. Upstream 8 km/h for 3 h; downstream 12 km/h for 2 h. (3+2=5 ✓)

Work Rate

If a job takes 6 h alone, the worker’s rate is \dfrac{1}{6} of the job per hour, and in t hours the worker does \dfrac{t}{6} of the job. The parts done by everyone add to 1 (one whole job).

Times alone a and b, working together for t hours:
\dfrac{t}{a}+\dfrac{t}{b}=1 or \dfrac{1}{a}+\dfrac{1}{b}=\dfrac{1}{t}
A drain or anything that undoes the work subtracts:
\dfrac{t}{a}-\dfrac{t}{b}=1
If people work for different times, each part is \dfrac{\text{time that person works}}{\text{time that person needs alone}}.
Example 6

Working together

(a) Ari can paint a fence in 6 h and Bea can paint it in 4 h. How long will it take them working together?
\dfrac{t}{6}+\dfrac{t}{4}=1
Multiply by 12: 2t+3t=12
t=\tfrac{12}{5}= 2.4 h (2 h 24 min)
(b) A large pump empties a flooded basement in 3 h. With a small pump also working, the job takes 2 h. How long would the small pump take alone?
Small pump alone: x hours, x\ne0.
\dfrac{2}{3}+\dfrac{2}{x}=1
Multiply by 3x: 2x+6=3x
x= 6 h
(c) An inlet pipe fills a pool in 8 h and the drain empties it in 12 h. The drain is left open by mistake while the pool fills. How long does it take to fill?
\dfrac{t}{8}-\dfrac{t}{12}=1
Multiply by 24: 3t-2t=24
t= 24 h
(d) Dev can stain a deck in 10 h and Eli in 15 h. Dev works alone for 5 h, then Eli joins him. How long do they work together to finish?
Together for t hours: Dev works 5+t hours, Eli t hours.
\dfrac{5+t}{10}+\dfrac{t}{15}=1
Multiply by 30: 15+3t+2t=30
t= 3 h (the deck takes 8 h in all)
Example 7

Work rate with a quadratic

(a) Working alone, Jordan takes 5 h longer than Kai to clear the snow from an outdoor rink. Together they take 6 h. How long does each take alone?
Kai x h, Jordan x+5 h; x\ne0,\ -5.
\dfrac{6}{x}+\dfrac{6}{x+5}=1
6(x+5)+6x=x(x+5)
12x+30=x^2+5x
x^2-7x-30=0\Rightarrow(x-10)(x+3)=0
x=10 (-3 rejected). Kai 10 h, Jordan 15 h.
Check: \tfrac{6}{10}+\tfrac{6}{15}=0.6+0.4=1 ✓
(b) A new printer finishes a job 2 h faster than an old one. Together they finish it in 3 h. To the nearest tenth of an hour, how long does each take alone?
New x h, old x+2 h; x\ne0,\ -2.
\dfrac{3}{x}+\dfrac{3}{x+2}=1
3(x+2)+3x=x(x+2)\Rightarrow x^2-4x-6=0
Does not factor: x=\dfrac{4\pm\sqrt{16+24}}{2}=2\pm\sqrt{10}
2-\sqrt{10}<0: reject. x=2+\sqrt{10}\approx5.16
New printer about 5.2 h, old about 7.2 h.
Quadratic formula on the calculator. For (b), type the root you keep in one line: (4+2nd√40))÷2ENTER gives 5.16227766, then round at the end.

Other Rational Models

A ratio or percent that changes (\dfrac{\text{part}}{\text{whole}} with the same amount added to both), or a given formula with reciprocals, also leads to a rational equation.

Example 8

A percent and a formula

(a) A basketball player has made 14 of her 25 free throws this season. How many free throws in a row must she make to raise her success rate to 75%?
Let x be the number of shots in a row.
\dfrac{14+x}{25+x}=\dfrac{3}{4}, x\ne-25
4(14+x)=3(25+x)
56+4x=75+3x\Rightarrow x=19
She must make 19 in a row. Check: \tfrac{33}{44}=0.75 ✓
(b) For a lens with focal length f, an object at distance u forms an image at distance v, where \dfrac{1}{f}=\dfrac{1}{u}+\dfrac{1}{v}. A lens has f=4 cm and the image is 6 cm farther from the lens than the object. Determine u and v.
v=u+6: \dfrac{1}{4}=\dfrac{1}{u}+\dfrac{1}{u+6}, u\ne0,\ -6
Multiply by 4u(u+6): u(u+6)=4(u+6)+4u
u^2+6u=8u+24\Rightarrow u^2-2u-24=0
(u-6)(u+4)=0; u=6 (-4 rejected: a distance)
u = 6 cm, v = 12 cm. Check: \tfrac{1}{6}+\tfrac{1}{12}=\tfrac{1}{4} ✓
✓

Key ideas

The five-step plan. Let a variable, write the equation, state the NPVs and multiply by the LCD, reject inadmissible roots (NPVs, negative speeds, times or lengths, non-integers), and answer in words with units.
Distance, speed, time. A row per trip, \text{time}=\tfrac{d}{s}. Same time: equal. Time saved or longer: subtract. Total time: add. Minutes into hours: 40\text{ min}=\tfrac{2}{3}\text{ h}.
Current and wind. With it: s+c. Against it: s-c. The still-water speed must be bigger than the current.
Work rate. Alone in a hours: \tfrac{1}{a} per hour, \tfrac{t}{a} in t hours. The parts add to 1; a drain subtracts; a late joiner works fewer hours. Percent: add the same amount on top and bottom.
