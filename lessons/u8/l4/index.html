The Nugget Order

At Cluck Shack, chicken nuggets only come in boxes of 4 and boxes of 7. You can order exactly 11 nuggets (4 + 7) or exactly 12 (4 + 4 + 4), but not exactly 5.

What is the largest number of nuggets you can NOT order exactly?

?
Make a guess first. Then test some numbers.
17! You can’t make 17 with 4s and 7s: take away no 7s, one 7 or two 7s and you are left with 17, 10 or 3, and none of those is a multiple of 4. But 18 = 4 + 7 + 7, 19 = 4 + 4 + 4 + 7, 20 = five 4s and 21 = three 7s. Once four numbers in a row work, add one more box of 4 to each, and every bigger number works too: there are only finitely many impossible orders (1, 2, 3, 5, 6, 9, 10, 13 and 17).
For two box sizes with no common factor, the largest impossible order is always (size × size) − size − size: 4 × 7 − 4 − 7 = 17.

Review: Linear Inequalities in One Variable

An inequality uses one of the symbols \lt,\ \le,\ \gt,\ \ge. Solve a linear inequality like an equation, with one extra rule.

Multiplying or dividing both sides by a negative number reverses the inequality sign:  -2x\ge12\ \Rightarrow\ x\le-6.

Check with a test value: a number in the solution should make the original inequality true, and one outside it false. On a number line, an open circle means the endpoint is not included (\lt,\ \gt) and a closed dot means it is (\le,\ \ge). In set notation, x\le-6 is written \{x\mid x\le-6,\ x\in\mathbb{R}\}. Unless told otherwise, variables are real numbers.

Example 1

Solve, test, graph, set notation

Solve, check with a test value, graph the solution on the number line and write it in set notation.

(a) 3-2(x+4)\ge7
3-2x-8\ge7
-2x-5\ge7
-2x\ge12
x\le-6 (dividing by -2 reverses the sign)
Test x=-7: 3-2(-3)=9\ge7 ✓;  test x=0: -5\ge7 is false ✓
\{x\mid x\le-6,\ x\in\mathbb{R}\}
(b) \dfrac{x-1}{3}\lt\dfrac{2x+5}{4}
Multiply both sides by 12:
4(x-1)\lt3(2x+5)
4x-4\lt6x+15
-2x\lt19\ \Rightarrow\ x\gt-\tfrac{19}{2}  (x\gt-9.5)
Test x=0: -\tfrac{1}{3}\lt\tfrac{5}{4} ✓
\{x\mid x\gt-9.5,\ x\in\mathbb{R}\}

Solutions of an Inequality in Two Variables

In a linear inequality in two variables, such as 2x-y\lt4, a solution is an ordered pair (x,\ y) that makes the inequality true. There are infinitely many solutions; together they fill a solution region. The line 2x-y=4, the boundary line, splits the plane into two half-planes, and the solution region is one of them.

Boundary
\le or \ge: solid linepoints on the line are solutions
\lt or \gt: dashed linepoints on the line are not solutions
Example 2

Which points are solutions?

(a) Determine whether each point is a solution of 2x-y\lt4.
(i) (0,\ 0) yes: 0\lt4
(ii) (3,\ 1) no: 5\lt4 false
(iii) (1,\ -3) no: 5\lt4 false
(iv) (2,\ 0) no: 4\lt4 false
(v) (-2,\ 3) yes: -7\lt4
(b) Graph the boundary line 2x-y=4 (should it be solid or dashed?), plot the five points, and shade the side of the line that holds the solutions.
Dashed: the sign is \lt, so points on the line, such as (2,\ 0), are not solutions.
The solutions (0,\ 0) and (-2,\ 3) are on the upper-left side, so shade that side; (3,\ 1) and (1,\ -3) are on the other side.
Dashed line, the upper-left side shaded.

Graphing a Linear Inequality

To graph a linear inequality
1Graph the boundary line by replacing the sign with = (use y=mx+b, or the two intercepts).
2Make it solid for \le,\ \ge or dashed for \lt,\ \gt.
3Choose a test point not on the line ((0,\ 0) is easiest unless the line passes through it) and substitute it into the inequality.
4If it is true, shade the side with the test point; if false, shade the other side.

Shortcut: once the inequality is solved for y, y\gt mx+b (or \ge) is the region above the line and y\lt mx+b (or \le) is below. Remember to reverse the sign if you divide by a negative while isolating y. Use a test point to confirm.

Graphing calculator (optional). Solve for y, enter mx+b in Y=, move the cursor to the left of Y_1 and press ENTER until the shade-above or shade-below symbol shows, then GRAPH. The calculator does not show solid or dashed lines, so you must decide that yourself.
Example 3

Solved for y

Graph each inequality.

(a) y\lt2x-1
Slope 2, y-intercept -1; dashed.
Test (0,\ 0): 0\lt-1 is false, so shade the side without the origin (below).
(b) y\ge-\tfrac{2}{3}x+3
Slope -\tfrac{2}{3}, y-intercept 3; solid.
Test (0,\ 0): 0\ge3 is false, so shade above.
Example 4

Standard form

Graph each inequality. Use intercepts, or solve for y first.

(a) 4x-3y\ge12
Intercepts (3,\ 0) and (0,\ -4); solid.
Test (0,\ 0): 0\ge12 false, so shade the side away from the origin.
(Solving: -3y\ge-4x+12\Rightarrow y\le\tfrac{4}{3}x-4, below.)
(b) 2x+5y\lt0
5y\lt-2x\Rightarrow y\lt-\tfrac{2}{5}x; dashed, through the origin.
(0,\ 0) is on the line, so test (0,\ 1): 5\lt0 is false; shade below.
(c) 5x+2y-10\le0
Intercepts (2,\ 0) and (0,\ 5); solid.
Test (0,\ 0): -10\le0 is true, so shade the side with the origin (below).
Example 5

Horizontal and vertical boundaries

x\gt a is the half-plane to the right of the vertical line x=a, and y\le b is the half-plane below the horizontal line y=b. Graph each inequality.

(a) x\gt-2
Dashed vertical line x=-2; shade to the right.
(b) y+1\le0
y\le-1: solid horizontal line y=-1; shade below.
(c) -3\le y\lt2
Both at once: solid y=-3, dashed y=2; shade the band between them.

Writing an Inequality from Its Graph

1Write the equation of the boundary line (from its slope and y-intercept, two points, or the intercepts).
2Dashed \Rightarrow\ \lt or \gt;  solid \Rightarrow\ \le or \ge.
3Substitute a point from the shaded region to choose the direction of the sign (or use above/below when the line is written y=mx+b).

Dashed, shaded above: y\gt\tfrac{2}{3}x-1.

Inequalities in Context

In a problem, define the variables, then write the inequality from the words:

at least\ge
at most, no more than\le
more than\gt
less than\lt

Add the restrictions the situation needs: quantities that cannot be negative give x\ge0 and y\ge0 (the graph stays in the first quadrant), and counts of people or objects must be whole numbers, so only the points with whole-number coordinates in the region are real answers.

Example 7

The school play

A school play sells adult tickets for \$15 and student tickets for \$10. To cover its costs it must take in at least \$1500. Let a be the number of adult tickets and s the number of student tickets.

(a) Write an inequality, with restrictions, for the situation.
15a+10s\ge1500,  a\ge0,  s\ge0,  a and s whole numbers.
(b) Graph the solution region.
Intercepts (100,\ 0) and (0,\ 150); solid. Test (0,\ 0): 0\ge1500 false, so shade away from the origin, in the first quadrant only.
(c) Are 60 adult and 50 student tickets enough?
15(60)+10(50)=1400\lt1500. No.
(d) If 40 adult tickets are sold, what is the least number of student tickets needed?
600+10s\ge1500\Rightarrow s\ge90. At least 90 student tickets.
Solid line from (0,\ 150) to (100,\ 0), shaded away from the origin.
5

Two Conditions at Once

When two conditions must hold at once, graph both inequalities; the solution region is where the shadings overlap.

Example 8

Tutoring and lifeguarding

Jordan earns \$20 per hour tutoring (x hours) and \$15 per hour lifeguarding (y hours). He can work at most 20 hours a week and wants to earn at least \$330.

(a) Write a system of inequalities, with restrictions.
x+y\le20,  20x+15y\ge330,  x\ge0,  y\ge0.
(b) Graph the solution region.
x+y=20: intercepts (20,\ 0), (0,\ 20), solid, shade toward the origin.
20x+15y=330: intercepts (16.5,\ 0), (0,\ 22), solid, shade away from the origin.
The overlap is the triangle with corners (6,\ 14), (16.5,\ 0) and (20,\ 0).
(c) Can he work 10 hours tutoring and 8 hours lifeguarding?
10+8=18\le20 ✓, but 200+120=320\lt330. No.
(d) What is the least number of tutoring hours he can work and still meet both conditions?
The left corner of the region: x+y=20 and 20x+15y=330.
20x+15(20-x)=330\Rightarrow5x=30\Rightarrow x=6, y=14.
At least 6 hours (with 14 hours lifeguarding).
The overlap: the triangle with corners (6,\ 14), (16.5,\ 0), (20,\ 0).
✓

Key ideas

One variable. Solve like an equation, but reverse the sign when you multiply or divide by a negative. Check with a test value; open circle for \lt,\ \gt, closed dot for \le,\ \ge.
Boundary line. Replace the sign with =. Solid for \le,\ \ge (the line is included), dashed for \lt,\ \gt.
Which side? Substitute a test point such as (0,\ 0): true → shade its side, false → the other side. Solved for y: y\gt is above, y\lt is below.
In context. Add x\ge0,\ y\ge0 and whole numbers where needed. Two conditions: the solution is where the shadings overlap; its corners come from solving the boundary lines as a system.
