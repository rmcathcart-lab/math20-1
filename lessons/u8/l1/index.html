The Two Ferries

Two ferries leave opposite banks of a river at the same moment, each at its own steady speed. They pass 720 m from the west bank.

Each turns straight around at the far side. They pass again 400 m from the east bank.

How wide is the river?

?
Make a guess, then sketch where the two ferries are each time they pass.
1760 m. At the first pass the two ferries have covered one river width between them, and the west ferry has done 720 m of it. At the second pass they have covered three widths between them (each has crossed once and come part of the way back), so the west ferry has done 3\times720=2160 m: one full width plus 400 m on the way back. The width is 2160-400=1760 m. Check: the east ferry has done 3\times1040=3120=1760+1360 m, and 1760-1360=400 ✓. The twist: you never need either ferry’s speed.

Solving Linear–Quadratic Systems

A line and a parabola can cross twice, touch once or miss. The points where they meet are the solutions of the system: we find them with a graph, by substitution or by elimination, and the discriminant tells how many there are.

I can…
  • explain that a solution of a linear–quadratic system is a point of intersection (2, 1 or 0), and verify one
  • solve a system by graphing, with the calculator’s intersect
  • solve a system by substitution and by elimination, with exact answers
  • use the discriminant to find the number of solutions, and find tangent lines
  • find where a line meets a circle x^2+y^2=r^2

1

Linear–Quadratic Systems

A linear–quadratic system is a pair of equations in x and y: one linear (its graph is a line, such as y=mx+b) and one quadratic (its graph is a parabola, such as y=ax^2+bx+c). A solution of the system is an ordered pair (x,y) that makes both equations true, so the solutions are the points of intersection of the line and the parabola.

Two, one or none. A line and a parabola can meet in two points, one point or no points, so the system has 2, 1 or 0 real solutions. A line that touches a parabola at exactly one point without crossing it is tangent to the parabola. (A vertical line x=k also meets a parabola y=ax^2+bx+c exactly once, but it crosses it, so it is not a tangent.)

To verify a solution, substitute it into each original equation; both must be true.

Example 1

Graph the line, count the points

The parabola y=x^2-2x-3 is graphed. For each line, graph the line on the grid, then state the number of solutions of the system and the solution(s).

(a) y=-2x+1
Two solutions: \boldsymbol{(-2,5)} and \boldsymbol{(2,-3)}
(b) y=-2x-3
One solution: \boldsymbol{(0,-3)}. The line is tangent to the parabola.
(c) y=-2x-6
No solution: the line passes below the parabola.
(d) Verify the solution (-2,5) from part (a).
y=x^2-2x-3:  (-2)^2-2(-2)-3=4+4-3=5 ✓
y=-2x+1:  -2(-2)+1=5 ✓
(-2,5) is on both graphs, so it is a solution.

Solving by Graphing with a Calculator

When the intersection points are not on grid lines, a graphing calculator finds them.

TI-84 Plus CE (the Casio has the same tool under G-Solv, ISCT):
  1. Write each equation in the form y=\dots Enter the quadratic as Y_1 and the linear one as Y_2, then press GRAPH.
  2. Adjust the WINDOW until every intersection point is on the screen.
  3. Press 2ndCALC (above TRACE), 5:intersect. Press ENTER for the first curve and for the second curve, move the cursor near one intersection point, and press ENTER for the guess.
  4. Repeat step 3 near the other intersection point. Round only at the end, as the question asks.

A graph gives decimal approximations; an algebraic method (Sections 3 and 4) gives exact answers.

Example 2 on the calculator. Y_1=X^2-4X+1, Y_2=2X-3, window x from -2 to 8 and y from -5 to 10, then 2ndCALC5 intersect, ENTERENTER, and a guess near each point (here 1, then 5).

3

Solving by Substitution

Substitution:
  1. Write the linear equation as y=\dots (or x=\dots).
  2. Substitute that expression into the quadratic equation.
  3. Write the resulting quadratic equation in standard form and solve it (factor, or use the quadratic formula).
  4. Substitute each x-value into the linear equation to find y.
  5. Verify in both equations and write each solution as an ordered pair.

Each x-value gives exactly one point. If the resulting equation has a double root, the line is tangent to the parabola.

Example 3

Substitute and solve

Solve each system algebraically.

(a) y=x^2+2x-5y=3x+1
x^2+2x-5=3x+1
x^2-x-6=0
(x-3)(x+2)=0
x=3 or x=-2
x=3: y=3(3)+1=10
x=-2: y=3(-2)+1=-5
Check (3,10): 9+6-5=10 ✓

(b) y=2x^2+x-44x-y=2

2x^2+x-4=4x-2
2x^2-3x-2=0
(2x+1)(x-2)=0

x=-\frac{1}{2}: y=-2-2=-4
x=2: y=8-2=6

Example 4

The tunnel beam

A tunnel entrance has the shape of the parabola y=-0.25x^2+4, where x and y are in metres and the ground is the x-axis. A straight support beam follows the line y=0.5x+1. Determine, to the nearest hundredth of a metre, the point where the beam meets the arch. Explain why one solution of the system is rejected.

The beam and the arch
-0.25x^2+4=0.5x+1
-0.25x^2-0.5x+3=0; multiply by -4: x^2+2x-12=0
x=\dfrac{-2\pm\sqrt{4+48}}{2}=\dfrac{-2\pm2\sqrt{13}}{2}=-1\pm\sqrt{13}, so x\approx2.61 or x\approx-4.61
x=2.606: y=0.5(2.606)+1\approx2.30.   x=-4.606: y\approx-1.30
Reject (-4.61,-1.30): it is below the ground, where the arch does not exist. The beam meets the arch at about \boldsymbol{(2.61,\ 2.30)}.
4

Solving by Elimination

If both equations are written with y alone on one side (or with matching y-terms), subtracting one equation from the other eliminates y and leaves a quadratic equation in x. If the y-terms are opposites, add the equations instead. Then finish as in substitution: solve for x, find each y from the simpler equation, and verify.

Example 5

Make y disappear

Solve each system by elimination.

(a) y=-x^2+4x+5y=-2x+10
Subtract the second equation from the first:
0=-x^2+6x-5
x^2-6x+5=0
(x-1)(x-5)=0, so x=1 or x=5
y=-2(1)+10=8;  y=-2(5)+10=0
Solutions: \boldsymbol{(1,8)} and \boldsymbol{(5,0)}
Check (5,0): -25+20+5=0 ✓
(b) x^2+y=73x-y=-3
The y-terms are opposites, so add:
x^2+3x=4
x^2+3x-4=0
(x+4)(x-1)=0, so x=-4 or x=1
From y=3x+3: y=-9 or y=6
Solutions: \boldsymbol{(-4,-9)} and \boldsymbol{(1,6)}
Check: 16+(-9)=7 ✓,  1+6=7 ✓

The Number of Solutions and the Discriminant

Substitution turns the system into one quadratic equation ax^2+bx+c=0. Each real root gives one point of intersection, so the discriminant b^2-4ac of that equation tells how many solutions the system has, without solving it.

Discriminant of the resulting equationLine and parabolaSolutions of the system
b^2-4ac>0intersect at two pointstwo
b^2-4ac=0the line is tangent to the parabolaone
b^2-4ac<0do not intersectnone

Always write the resulting equation in standard form (one side 0) before reading a, b and c.

Example 6

How many, without solving?

Without solving, determine the number of solutions of each system.

(a) y=2x^2-3x+1y=x-1
2x^2-4x+2=0
(-4)^2-4(2)(2)=16-16=0
One solution (the line is tangent). It is (1,0).
(b) y=x^2-4x+7y=2x-3
x^2-6x+10=0
36-4(1)(10)=-4<0
No solution.
(c) y=-x^2+5y=-3x+2
-x^2+3x+3=0
9-4(-1)(3)=21>0
Two solutions.
Example 7

Two, one or none: find the value

(a) For which values of k does the line y=2x+k intersect the parabola y=x^2-4x+3 at two points? at one point? at no points? For the one-point case, determine the point of tangency.
x^2-4x+3=2x+k\Rightarrow x^2-6x+(3-k)=0
Discriminant: (-6)^2-4(1)(3-k)=36-12+4k=24+4k
One point: 24+4k=0\Rightarrow \boldsymbol{k=-6}.   Two points: 24+4k>0\Rightarrow \boldsymbol{k>-6}.   No points: \boldsymbol{k<-6}.
When k=-6: x^2-6x+9=0\Rightarrow(x-3)^2=0, x=3, y=2(3)-6=0. Point of tangency \boldsymbol{(3,0)}.
(b) The line y=mx-1 is tangent to the parabola y=x^2+3. Determine the possible values of m.
x^2+3=mx-1\Rightarrow x^2-mx+4=0
Tangent: (-m)^2-4(1)(4)=0\Rightarrow m^2=16\Rightarrow \boldsymbol{m=4} or \boldsymbol{m=-4}.
(Check m=4: x^2-4x+4=(x-2)^2, one point (2,7). For m=-4 the point is (-2,7).)
6

A Line and a Circle

The same methods work for other curves. The equation x^2+y^2=r^2 describes a circle with centre (0,0) and radius r: by the Pythagorean theorem, every point (x,y) on it is r units from the origin. A line meets a circle at 2, 1 (a tangent) or 0 points. Substitute the line into the circle’s equation, solve, then use the line to find y.

Example 8

A line and a circle

(a) Determine the exact points of intersection of the circle x^2+y^2=20 and the line y=2x.
x^2+(2x)^2=20
5x^2=20
x^2=4, so x=2 or x=-2
y=2x: y=4 or y=-4
Points: \boldsymbol{(2,4)} and \boldsymbol{(-2,-4)}
(b) Show that the line x+2y=10 is tangent to the circle x^2+y^2=20, and determine the point of contact.
x=10-2y
(10-2y)^2+y^2=20
100-40y+4y^2+y^2-20=0
5y^2-40y+80=0\Rightarrow y^2-8y+16=0
Discriminant 64-64=0: one solution, so the line is tangent.
(y-4)^2=0, y=4, x=10-8=2. Point of contact \boldsymbol{(2,4)}.
✓

Key ideas

Solutions are meeting points. A solution makes both equations true: it is a point where the line and the parabola meet. There are 2, 1 (the line is tangent) or 0. Verify in each original equation.
Graphing. Y_1 = the quadratic, Y_2 = the line, a window that shows every point, then 2ndCALC 5:intersect near each point. A graph gives decimals.
Substitution and elimination. Replace y with the line’s expression, or subtract (add) the equations so y drops out. Solve the quadratic, then find each y from the linear equation. Exact answers.
How many? The discriminant of the resulting equation: b^2-4ac>0 two, =0 one (tangent), <0 none. A line and a circle x^2+y^2=r^2 work the same way.
