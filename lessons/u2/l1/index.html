Roots and Radicals

A radical is an expression of the form \sqrt[\textcolor{#7b4fc4}{n}]{\textcolor{#1f5bd6}{x}}, where n is a natural number greater than 1. The number \textcolor{#7b4fc4}{n} is the index and \textcolor{#1f5bd6}{x} is the radicand. When no index is written, the index is 2 (a square root).
\sqrt[\textcolor{#7b4fc4}{3}]{\textcolor{#1f5bd6}{40}}
index 3 the small number in the crook: a cube root
radicand 40 the number under the radical sign
coefficient a number in front, as in \textcolor{#c27a0e}{4}\sqrt{\textcolor{#1f5bd6}{3}} (it multiplies the root)
  • Every positive number has two square roots: 36 has square roots 6 and -6. The symbol \sqrt{\phantom{x}} means only the principal (non-negative) root: \sqrt{36}=6, and -\sqrt{36}=-6.
  • A negative number has no real square root (or fourth root, or any even root): (\text{any real number})^2\ge0.
  • Every real number has exactly one cube root (or fifth root, or any odd root): \sqrt[3]{-64}=-4 because (-4)^3=-64.
Even index \sqrt{x},\ \sqrt[4]{x},\ \dots
The radicand must be \ge0, and the root is \ge0.
Odd index \sqrt[3]{x},\ \sqrt[5]{x},\ \dots
Any radicand works: \sqrt[3]{-x}=-\sqrt[3]{x}.

What number, used n times?

\sqrt[n]{x} asks for the number that, used as a factor n times, makes x.

Rational or irrational?

A rational number can be written as a fraction \dfrac{a}{b} of integers (b\ne0); its decimal ends or repeats. An irrational number cannot; its decimal goes on forever without repeating. The root of a perfect square, cube, etc. is rational (\sqrt{0.25}=0.5, \sqrt[3]{\tfrac{8}{27}}=\tfrac23). Any other root is irrational (\sqrt2=1.414\,213\dots). Together, the rational and irrational numbers make up the real numbers.

The ladder of perfect powers

The perfect squares (or cubes, …) are the rungs. A radicand that lands on a rung has a rational root; one that lands between two rungs has an irrational root, and the root is between the two whole numbers.

Example 2

Rational, irrational, and in between

(a) Classify each number as rational or irrational.

(i) \sqrt{50}
Irrational (50 is not a perfect square).
(ii) \sqrt[3]{-216}
Rational: -6.
(iii) \sqrt{\dfrac{49}{64}}
Rational: \dfrac78.
(iv) \sqrt[4]{40}
Irrational (40 is not a perfect fourth power).
(v) \sqrt{0.4}
Irrational: 0.4=\tfrac25, and neither 2 nor 5 is a perfect square.
(vi) \sqrt{0.49}
Rational: 0.7.

(b) Between which two consecutive whole numbers does each number lie?

(i) \sqrt{50}
49<50<64, so \sqrt{49}<\sqrt{50}<\sqrt{64}: between 7 and 8.
(ii) \sqrt[3]{50}
27<50<64, so \sqrt[3]{27}<\sqrt[3]{50}<\sqrt[3]{64}: between 3 and 4.

Multiplying and Dividing Under One Root

Roots with the same index can be multiplied or divided under one radical sign. Check: \sqrt4\times\sqrt9=2\times3=6 and \sqrt{4\times9}=\sqrt{36}=6. This does not work for addition or subtraction: \sqrt9+\sqrt{16}=3+4=7, but \sqrt{9+16}=\sqrt{25}=5.

\sqrt[n]{a}\times\sqrt[n]{b}=\sqrt[n]{ab}\dfrac{\sqrt[n]{a}}{\sqrt[n]{b}}=\sqrt[n]{\dfrac ab}
(b\ne0; when n is even, a\ge0 and b\ge0)
!
Roots don’t split over + or −. In general \sqrt{a+b}\ne\sqrt a+\sqrt b and \sqrt{a-b}\ne\sqrt a-\sqrt b. Only × and ÷ go under one sign.
Example 3

True or false?

State whether each statement is true or false. Show a check.

(a) \sqrt2\times\sqrt{18}=6
True: \sqrt{2\times18}=\sqrt{36}=6.
(b) \sqrt{36+64}=\sqrt{36}+\sqrt{64}
False: left side \sqrt{100}=10; right side 6+8=14.
(c) \dfrac{\sqrt{72}}{\sqrt2}=6
True: \sqrt{\tfrac{72}{2}}=\sqrt{36}=6.
(d) \sqrt{169}-\sqrt{25}=\sqrt{144}
False: 13-5=8, but \sqrt{144}=12.
(e) \sqrt[3]4\times\sqrt[3]{16}=4
True: \sqrt[3]{64}=4.
(f) \sqrt[3]2+\sqrt[3]6=\sqrt[3]8
False: roots cannot be added under one sign. (\sqrt[3]8=2, but \sqrt[3]2+\sqrt[3]6>1+1=2.)

Entire Radicals to Mixed Radicals

Entire radical
\sqrt{\textcolor{#1f5bd6}{48}},\quad \sqrt[3]{\textcolor{#1f5bd6}{54}}
The whole value is under the radical sign.
Mixed radical
\textcolor{#c27a0e}{4}\sqrt{\textcolor{#1f5bd6}{3}},\quad \textcolor{#c27a0e}{3}\sqrt[3]{\textcolor{#1f5bd6}{2}}
A number in front multiplies the radical.
A radical is in simplest form when the radicand has no perfect-square factor (index 2), no perfect-cube factor (index 3), and so on.

Why can a number come out? Cut the square into tiles

To simplify, write the radicand as a product using the largest perfect power that divides it, split the root, and take that root out:

\sqrt{48}=\sqrt{\textcolor{#c27a0e}{16}\times\textcolor{#1f5bd6}{3}}=\sqrt{\textcolor{#c27a0e}{16}}\times\sqrt{\textcolor{#1f5bd6}{3}}=\textcolor{#c27a0e}{4}\sqrt{\textcolor{#1f5bd6}{3}}\sqrt[3]{54}=\sqrt[3]{\textcolor{#c27a0e}{27}\times\textcolor{#1f5bd6}{2}}=\sqrt[3]{\textcolor{#c27a0e}{27}}\times\sqrt[3]{\textcolor{#1f5bd6}{2}}=\textcolor{#c27a0e}{3}\sqrt[3]{\textcolor{#1f5bd6}{2}}
Perfect squares 4,\ 9,\ 16,\ 25,\ 36,\ 49,\ 64,\ 81,\ 100,\ 121,\ 144,\ 169,\ 196,\ 225,\ \dots
Perfect cubes 8,\ 27,\ 64,\ 125,\ 216,\ 343,\ 512,\ 729,\ 1\,000,\ \dots
Perfect fourth powers 16,\ 81,\ 256,\ 625,\ \dots

Big radicands: use prime factors

For a large radicand, use its prime factorization. For a square root, each pair of equal factors comes out as one factor; for a cube root, each triple.

Example 5

Prime factors and simplest form

(a) Simplify \sqrt{2\,700} using prime factors.
2\,700=2\times2\times3\times3\times3\times5\times5
Pairs: 2\cdot2, 3\cdot3, 5\cdot5; one 3 is left.
\sqrt{2\,700}=2\times3\times5\sqrt3=\boldsymbol{30\sqrt3}
(b) Simplify \sqrt[3]{3\,024} using prime factors.
3\,024=2\times2\times2\times2\times3\times3\times3\times7
Triples: 2\cdot2\cdot2, 3\cdot3\cdot3; left: 2\times7=14.
\sqrt[3]{3\,024}=2\times3\sqrt[3]{14}=\boldsymbol{6\sqrt[3]{14}}
(c) Kai wrote \sqrt{72}=2\sqrt{18}. Is this in simplest form? Explain and finish the simplification.
No: 18 still has the perfect-square factor 9. 2\sqrt{18}=2\sqrt{9\times2}=2\times3\sqrt2=\boldsymbol{6\sqrt2}.
Using the largest perfect square first does it in one step: \sqrt{72}=\sqrt{36\times2}=6\sqrt2.

Estimating from one known root

Simplifying also lets you estimate a radical from one known value. Since 100 and \tfrac{1}{100} are perfect squares, \sqrt{300}=10\sqrt3 and \sqrt{0.03}=\sqrt{\tfrac{3}{100}}=\tfrac{\sqrt3}{10}.

Mixed Radicals to Entire Radicals

Every mixed radical can be written as an entire radical. Write the number in front as a radical with the same index (raise it to the power of the index), then multiply the radicands:

\textcolor{#c27a0e}{5}\sqrt{\textcolor{#1f5bd6}{2}}=\sqrt{\textcolor{#c27a0e}{5^2}}\times\sqrt{\textcolor{#1f5bd6}{2}}=\sqrt{\textcolor{#c27a0e}{25}\times\textcolor{#1f5bd6}{2}}=\sqrt{50}\textcolor{#c27a0e}{2}\sqrt[3]{\textcolor{#1f5bd6}{7}}=\sqrt[3]{\textcolor{#c27a0e}{2^3}}\times\sqrt[3]{\textcolor{#1f5bd6}{7}}=\sqrt[3]{56}
a\sqrt[n]{b}=\sqrt[n]{a^n\times b}
Negative number in front: for an odd index the sign can go inside (-2\sqrt[3]7=\sqrt[3]{(-2)^3\times7}=\sqrt[3]{-56}); for an even index it stays outside (-5\sqrt2=-\sqrt{50}, because \sqrt{50} is never negative).

Comparing and Ordering Radicals

To compare radicals without a calculator, write each one as an entire radical with the same index. Then the larger radicand gives the larger value. This works for negative cube roots too: -80>-81, so \sqrt[3]{-80}>\sqrt[3]{-81}.
Example 8

Putting radicals in order

Without a calculator, arrange the radicals in the order asked.

(a) 3\sqrt6,\ \ 2\sqrt{13},\ \ 5\sqrt2,\ \ \sqrt{55},\ \ 4\sqrt3 from least to greatest.
Entire radicals: 3\sqrt6=\sqrt{54}, 2\sqrt{13}=\sqrt{52}, 5\sqrt2=\sqrt{50}, \sqrt{55}, 4\sqrt3=\sqrt{48}.
Least to greatest: \boldsymbol{4\sqrt3,\ \ 5\sqrt2,\ \ 2\sqrt{13},\ \ 3\sqrt6,\ \ \sqrt{55}}
(b) 2\sqrt[3]9,\ \ 3\sqrt[3]3,\ \ \sqrt[3]{70},\ \ -2\sqrt[3]{10},\ \ -3\sqrt[3]3 from greatest to least.
2\sqrt[3]9=\sqrt[3]{72}, 3\sqrt[3]3=\sqrt[3]{81}, \sqrt[3]{70}, -2\sqrt[3]{10}=\sqrt[3]{-80}, -3\sqrt[3]3=\sqrt[3]{-81}.
Greatest to least: \boldsymbol{3\sqrt[3]3,\ \ 2\sqrt[3]9,\ \ \sqrt[3]{70},\ \ -2\sqrt[3]{10},\ \ -3\sqrt[3]3}

Radicals in Context

Lengths found from areas, volumes and the Pythagorean theorem are often irrational. Give them exactly, as a radical in simplest form.

Square of area A
s=\sqrt{A}
Cube of volume V
e=\sqrt[3]{V}
Right triangle
c=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}
Distance between two points: d=\sqrt{(x_2-x_1)^2+(y_2-y_1)^2} (the Pythagorean theorem with the horizontal and vertical changes as legs).
Example 9

Floors and planters

(a) A square floor has an area of 288\ \text{m}^2. Determine the exact side length and perimeter.
s=\sqrt{288}=\sqrt{144\times2}=\boldsymbol{12\sqrt2}\ \text{m}
P=4\times12\sqrt2=\boldsymbol{48\sqrt2}\ \text{m}
(b) A cube-shaped planter holds 1\,080\ \text{cm}^3 of soil when full. Determine the exact edge length.
e=\sqrt[3]{1\,080}=\sqrt[3]{216\times5}=\boldsymbol{6\sqrt[3]5}\ \text{cm}
Example 10

Exact lengths with Pythagoras

(a) A right triangle has legs 4 cm and 8 cm. Determine the exact length of the hypotenuse.
c=\sqrt{4^2+8^2}=\sqrt{16+64}=\sqrt{80}
=\sqrt{16\times5}=\boldsymbol{4\sqrt5}\ \text{cm}
(b) Determine the exact length of segment AB, with A(-2,\ 5) and B(4,\ -1).
Horizontal change 4-(-2)=6; vertical change -1-5=-6.
AB=\sqrt{6^2+(-6)^2}=\sqrt{72}=\sqrt{36\times2}=\boldsymbol{6\sqrt2}

Key ideas

Roots. \sqrt[n]{x}: index n, radicand x. \sqrt{\ } is the principal (non-negative) root. Even index: no real root of a negative. Odd index: \sqrt[3]{-x}=-\sqrt[3]x. Perfect powers give rational roots; others are irrational.
One root sign. Same index: \sqrt[n]a\times\sqrt[n]b=\sqrt[n]{ab} and \sqrt[n]a\div\sqrt[n]b=\sqrt[n]{a\div b}. Never over + or −: \sqrt{a+b}\ne\sqrt a+\sqrt b.
Entire ↔ mixed. Out: split off the largest perfect power and take its root (\sqrt{48}=4\sqrt3). In: raise the front number to the index (5\sqrt2=\sqrt{50}). Simplest form: no perfect-power factor left inside.
Compare and apply. To order radicals, make them all entire with the same index and compare radicands. Lengths from areas, volumes and Pythagoras: exact, in simplest form.
