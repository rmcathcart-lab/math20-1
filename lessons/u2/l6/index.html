2499. The quick way: both numbers are 1 away from 50, so 49\times51=(50-1)(50+1)=50^2-1^2=2500-1. The two middle parts cancel. Same trick: 98\times102=100^2-2^2=9996.

Rationalizing Binomial Denominators with Conjugates

A denominator like \textcolor{#1f5bd6}{3-\sqrt5} has two terms, so multiplying by \sqrt5 alone won’t clear it. Its conjugate \textcolor{#c27a0e}{3+\sqrt5} will: the middle terms cancel and the product is rational.

I can…
  • write the conjugate of a binomial and explain why the product of a binomial and its conjugate is rational
  • rationalize a binomial denominator, such as \tfrac{a}{b+\sqrt c} or \tfrac{\sqrt a}{\sqrt b-\sqrt c}, by multiplying by the conjugate over itself
  • rationalize when the numerator also has two terms
  • write radicals in simplest form first, and reduce by a factor of every term at the end
  • rationalize expressions with variables and state the restrictions
  • use conjugates in mixed operations and problems

Today: conjugates, and rationalizing denominators with two terms.
1

Conjugates

Two binomials that differ only in the sign between their terms are conjugates: a+\sqrt b and a-\sqrt b, or \sqrt a+\sqrt b and \sqrt a-\sqrt b. Their product is a difference of squares (Lesson 4), so the middle terms cancel and every radical disappears: the product is rational.
(a+\sqrt b)(a-\sqrt b)=a^2-b
(\sqrt a+\sqrt b)(\sqrt a-\sqrt b)=a-b
(m\sqrt a+n\sqrt b)(m\sqrt a-n\sqrt b)=m^2a-n^2b

Multiply in a grid: the cross terms cancel

Multiply every term of one binomial by every term of the other. The two cross terms are opposites, so they add to 0.

Example 1

Conjugate pairs

Write the conjugate of each binomial, and find the product of the binomial and its conjugate.

BinomialConjugateProduct
4+\sqrt34-\sqrt316-3=13
\sqrt7-\sqrt2\sqrt7+\sqrt27-2=5
2\sqrt5+32\sqrt5-34(5)-9=11
3\sqrt2-2\sqrt63\sqrt2+2\sqrt69(2)-4(6)=18-24=-6
Flip the sign between the terms for the conjugate. Product: (\text{first})^2-(\text{second})^2, e.g. (2\sqrt5)^2-3^2=4(5)-9=\boldsymbol{11}. A product can be negative: (3\sqrt2)^2-(2\sqrt6)^2=18-24=\boldsymbol{-6}.
2

Rationalizing a Binomial Denominator

Multiplying by the radical alone does not work for a binomial: \dfrac{1}{2+\sqrt3}\cdot\dfrac{\sqrt3}{\sqrt3}=\dfrac{\sqrt3}{2\sqrt3+3} still has a radical in the denominator. Instead, multiply the numerator and the denominator by the conjugate of the denominator.

Steps
  1. Multiply the numerator and denominator by the conjugate of the denominator (a form of 1).
  2. Expand the numerator.
  3. The denominator becomes rational (a difference of squares).
  4. Simplify: write radicals in simplest form, collect like terms, reduce by a factor of every term, and keep the denominator positive.
Example 2

Clear the denominator

Rationalize the denominator and simplify.

(a) \dfrac{6}{3-\sqrt5}
\dfrac{6}{3-\sqrt5}\cdot\dfrac{3+\sqrt5}{3+\sqrt5}=\dfrac{18+6\sqrt5}{9-5}=\dfrac{18+6\sqrt5}{4}=\boldsymbol{\dfrac{9+3\sqrt5}{2}}
(b) \dfrac{10}{\sqrt7+\sqrt2}
\dfrac{10(\sqrt7-\sqrt2)}{7-2}=\dfrac{10(\sqrt7-\sqrt2)}{5}=\boldsymbol{2\sqrt7-2\sqrt2}
(c) \dfrac{\sqrt3}{2\sqrt3-3}
\dfrac{\sqrt3(2\sqrt3+3)}{12-9}=\dfrac{6+3\sqrt3}{3}=\boldsymbol{2+\sqrt3}
(d) \dfrac{4}{\sqrt6-2\sqrt2}
\dfrac{4(\sqrt6+2\sqrt2)}{6-8}=\dfrac{4(\sqrt6+2\sqrt2)}{-2}=\boldsymbol{-2\sqrt6-4\sqrt2}
!
Square the whole term. In (d), (2\sqrt2)^2=4(2)=8, not 2(2)=4. And when the denominator comes out negative, divide every term by it so the answer has no negative denominator.

Numerators with Two Terms

When the numerator is also a binomial, expand it with FOIL (a second grid). If the numerator is the same as the conjugate you multiply by, the top is a perfect square: (\sqrt5+1)(\sqrt5+1)=(\sqrt5+1)^2. Then simplify.
Example 3

Two terms on top

Rationalize the denominator and simplify.

(a) \dfrac{\sqrt5+1}{\sqrt5-1}
\dfrac{(\sqrt5+1)(\sqrt5+1)}{5-1}
=\dfrac{5+2\sqrt5+1}{4}=\dfrac{6+2\sqrt5}{4}
=\boldsymbol{\dfrac{3+\sqrt5}{2}}
(b) \dfrac{2\sqrt3-\sqrt2}{\sqrt3+\sqrt2}
\dfrac{(2\sqrt3-\sqrt2)(\sqrt3-\sqrt2)}{3-2}
=6-2\sqrt6-\sqrt6+2
=\boldsymbol{8-3\sqrt6}
(c) \dfrac{\sqrt6+3}{\sqrt6-2}
\dfrac{(\sqrt6+3)(\sqrt6+2)}{6-4}
=\dfrac{6+2\sqrt6+3\sqrt6+6}{2}
=\boldsymbol{\dfrac{12+5\sqrt6}{2}}
4

Simplifying Fully

Write every radical in simplest form before rationalizing; the conjugate is then smaller and common factors are easier to see. After rationalizing, look for a factor of every term that also divides the denominator.

A factor of every term

!
Don’t cancel just one term. \dfrac{6+2\sqrt5}{4} is not \dfrac{6+\sqrt5}{2}: dividing the denominator by 2 means dividing every term on top by 2 too, giving \dfrac{3+\sqrt5}{2}.
Example 4

Simplest form first

Simplify.

(a) \dfrac{\sqrt6}{\sqrt{18}+\sqrt{12}}
\dfrac{\sqrt6}{3\sqrt2+2\sqrt3}\cdot\dfrac{3\sqrt2-2\sqrt3}{3\sqrt2-2\sqrt3}=\dfrac{3\sqrt{12}-2\sqrt{18}}{18-12}
=\dfrac{6\sqrt3-6\sqrt2}{6}=\boldsymbol{\sqrt3-\sqrt2}
(b) \dfrac{6-\sqrt{12}}{\sqrt3-1}
\dfrac{(6-2\sqrt3)(\sqrt3+1)}{3-1}=\dfrac{6\sqrt3+6-6-2\sqrt3}{2}
=\dfrac{4\sqrt3}{2}=\boldsymbol{2\sqrt3}
5

Binomial Denominators with Variables

State the restrictions: each even-index radicand must be \ge 0, and the denominator cannot be 0. For example, \sqrt x-3=0 when x=9, so \dfrac{1}{\sqrt x-3} needs x\ge0 and x\ne9. The conjugate works the same way: (\sqrt x-3)(\sqrt x+3)=x-9.
Example 5

Variables in the denominator

Rationalize the denominator. State the restrictions on the variables.

(a) \dfrac{3}{\sqrt x+2}
x\ge0 (\sqrt x+2 is never 0)
\dfrac{3(\sqrt x-2)}{x-4}=\boldsymbol{\dfrac{3\sqrt x-6}{x-4}}
(x\ne4 is needed for this form)
(b) \dfrac{2\sqrt x}{\sqrt x-3}
x\ge0,\ x\ne9
\dfrac{2\sqrt x(\sqrt x+3)}{x-9}=\boldsymbol{\dfrac{2x+6\sqrt x}{x-9}}
(c) \dfrac{\sqrt a-\sqrt b}{\sqrt a+\sqrt b}
a\ge0,\ b\ge0,\ \text{not both }0
\dfrac{(\sqrt a-\sqrt b)^2}{a-b}=\boldsymbol{\dfrac{a-2\sqrt{ab}+b}{a-b}} (a\ne b is needed for this form)

Mixed Operations and Problems

To add or subtract fractions with radical denominators, rationalize each fraction first, then collect like terms. Use the order of operations, and multiply or divide using the rules from Lessons 4 and 5.

Treat \sqrt3 like a variable: 8\sqrt3-\sqrt3-2\sqrt3=5\sqrt3, just as 8x-x-2x=5x.

Example 6

Mixed operations

Simplify.

(a) (2\sqrt3-1)(\sqrt3+4)-\dfrac{6}{\sqrt3}
=6+8\sqrt3-\sqrt3-4-\dfrac{6\sqrt3}{3}
=2+7\sqrt3-2\sqrt3
=\boldsymbol{2+5\sqrt3}
(b) \dfrac{1}{2+\sqrt3}+\dfrac{1}{2-\sqrt3}
=\dfrac{2-\sqrt3}{4-3}+\dfrac{2+\sqrt3}{4-3}
=2-\sqrt3+2+\sqrt3
=\boldsymbol{4} (a rational number)
(c) \dfrac{3}{\sqrt5}-\dfrac{2}{\sqrt5+1}
=\dfrac{3\sqrt5}{5}-\dfrac{2(\sqrt5-1)}{4}=\dfrac{3\sqrt5}{5}-\dfrac{\sqrt5-1}{2}
=\dfrac{6\sqrt5-5\sqrt5+5}{10}=\boldsymbol{\dfrac{\sqrt5+5}{10}}

Problems: the golden ratio and a rectangle

A length or a ratio found by dividing often lands with a binomial in the denominator. The conjugate tidies it up, and sometimes reveals something surprising.

Example 7

Golden ratio and a rectangle

(a) The golden ratio is \varphi=\dfrac{1+\sqrt5}{2}. Show that \dfrac{1}{\varphi}=\varphi-1.
\dfrac{1}{\varphi}=\dfrac{2}{1+\sqrt5}\cdot\dfrac{1-\sqrt5}{1-\sqrt5}=\dfrac{2-2\sqrt5}{1-5}=\dfrac{2-2\sqrt5}{-4}=\dfrac{\sqrt5-1}{2}
\varphi-1=\dfrac{1+\sqrt5}{2}-\dfrac{2}{2}=\dfrac{\sqrt5-1}{2}. They are equal.
(b) A rectangle has an area of 11 cm² and a width of (\sqrt{15}-2) cm. Determine its exact length and perimeter.
\ell=\dfrac{11}{\sqrt{15}-2}\cdot\dfrac{\sqrt{15}+2}{\sqrt{15}+2}=\dfrac{11(\sqrt{15}+2)}{15-4}=\boldsymbol{\sqrt{15}+2} cm
P=2(\sqrt{15}+2)+2(\sqrt{15}-2)=\boldsymbol{4\sqrt{15}} cm
✓

Key ideas

Conjugates differ only in the sign between their terms. Their product is a difference of squares, (a+\sqrt b)(a-\sqrt b)=a^2-b: the cross terms cancel and the result is rational.
Rationalize a binomial denominator by multiplying the top and bottom by the conjugate of the denominator. Multiplying by the radical alone leaves a radical behind.
Simplest form: simplify radicals first, expand the top (FOIL for two terms), collect like terms, reduce by a factor of every term, and keep the denominator positive.
Variables: radicands \ge0 and the denominator \ne0 (\sqrt x-3\ne0 means x\ne9). Adding fractions: rationalize each one first, then collect like radicals.
