Adding and Subtracting Radicals

Radicals add the way like terms do: \sqrt{3} is a unit, just like x. Put every radical in simplest form first, then collect the ones with the same index and the same radicand.

I can…
  • tell like radicals (same index, same radicand) from unlike ones
  • add and subtract like radicals by combining their coefficients, and explain why \sqrt{a}+\sqrt{b}\ne\sqrt{a+b}
  • simplify each radical first so that like radicals appear, including cube roots, fourth roots and fractions in front
  • combine radicals with variable radicands and state the restrictions
  • find exact perimeters and missing lengths, solve for an unknown radical expression, and extend an arithmetic sequence of radical terms

Warm-up

Stepping Stones

A garden path is made of square stepping stones laid edge to edge in a straight line. Each stone is named by its area in m².

1
A stone has area 9 m². How long is each side?
3 m, since 3\times3=9: the side is \sqrt{9}=3.
2
Stones of area 9 and 16 are laid side by side. How long is the path?
\sqrt{9}+\sqrt{16}=3+4=\boldsymbol{7} m.
3
Is one stone of area 25 (that’s 9 + 16) just as long?
No. \sqrt{25}=5 m: 2 m short. Adding the areas does not add the sides.
!
The big one. Path A is three stones of area 2. Stone B is one stone of area 18. Which is longer? Guess first.

C, exactly equal. Stone B cuts into a 3 by 3 grid of area-2 stones (9\times2=18), so its side is three small sides: \sqrt{18}=3\sqrt{2}. Path A is \sqrt{2}+\sqrt{2}+\sqrt{2}=3\sqrt{2} too.
No calculator needed: count the \sqrt{2}’s, the way you count x’s.

1

Like Radicals

Like radicals have the same index and the same radicand. For example, 4\sqrt{7} and -\sqrt{7} are like radicals; 2\sqrt{3} and 2\sqrt{5} are not (different radicands), and \sqrt{5} and \sqrt[3]{5} are not (different indices).

Add or subtract like radicals the same way you collect like terms in algebra (4x+3x=7x): add or subtract the coefficients and keep the radical part.

Combining like radicals
m\sqrt[n]{a}+k\sqrt[n]{a}=(m+k)\sqrt[n]{a}
Only like radicals can be combined. Never add or subtract the radicands.

\sqrt{7} tiles behave like x tiles

Think of each radical as a tile. Like radicals go in the same bin; a negative tile cancels a positive one.

Never add the radicands

\sqrt{9}+\sqrt{16}=3+4=7, but \sqrt{9+16}=\sqrt{25}=5. So \sqrt{a}+\sqrt{b}\ne\sqrt{a+b}. Unlike radicals stay as separate terms, just as 2x+5y cannot be combined. Rational terms (numbers with no radical) combine only with other rational terms.

Like or not? Tap to check.

\sqrt{5} and \sqrt[3]{5}
not like: indices 2 and 3
6\sqrt{11} and 6
not like: one is a radical, one is a rational term

Combining like radicals

Simplify by combining like radicals.

(a) 6\sqrt{5}+3\sqrt{5}
(6+3)\sqrt{5}=\boldsymbol{9\sqrt{5}}
(b) 2\sqrt{13}-8\sqrt{13}+\sqrt{13}
(2-8+1)\sqrt{13}=\boldsymbol{-5\sqrt{13}}
(c) 7\sqrt[3]{6}-10\sqrt[3]{6}
(7-10)\sqrt[3]{6}=\boldsymbol{-3\sqrt[3]{6}}
(d) 5\sqrt{3}+2\sqrt{7}-\sqrt{3}+4\sqrt{7}
Group like radicals:
(5-1)\sqrt{3}+(2+4)\sqrt{7}
=\boldsymbol{4\sqrt{3}+6\sqrt{7}}
(e) 9-4\sqrt{2}+3\sqrt{2}-5
Rational terms: 9-5=4.
Radicals: -4\sqrt{2}+3\sqrt{2}=-\sqrt{2}.
Answer: \boldsymbol{4-\sqrt{2}}
(f) 3\sqrt{2}+2\sqrt[3]{2}
Different indices: not like radicals. It cannot be simplified.

Simplify First, Then Combine

Radicals that look unlike may turn into like radicals once each one is in simplest form.
Step 1
Write every radical as a mixed radical in simplest form (Lesson 1).
Step 2
Combine the like radicals.
Step 3
Leave unlike radicals as separate terms.

Why \sqrt{72}=6\sqrt{2}: cut the square

A square of area A has side \sqrt{A}. Cut it into small squares of area 2 and count how many small sides fit along one edge.

Simplify, then sort into bins

As written, the radicands look different. In simplest form, they drop into the same bin.

Example 2

Simplify first

Simplify.

(a) \sqrt{48}+\sqrt{75}
=\sqrt{16}\sqrt{3}+\sqrt{25}\sqrt{3}
=4\sqrt{3}+5\sqrt{3}=\boldsymbol{9\sqrt{3}}
(b) 3\sqrt{8}-\sqrt{50}+\sqrt{18}
=3(2\sqrt{2})-5\sqrt{2}+3\sqrt{2}
=6\sqrt{2}-5\sqrt{2}+3\sqrt{2}=\boldsymbol{4\sqrt{2}}
(c) \sqrt{20}+\sqrt{27}+\sqrt{45}-\sqrt{12}
=2\sqrt{5}+3\sqrt{3}+3\sqrt{5}-2\sqrt{3}
=\boldsymbol{5\sqrt{5}+\sqrt{3}}
(d) 2\sqrt{72}-3\sqrt{32}+\sqrt{98}
=2(6\sqrt{2})-3(4\sqrt{2})+7\sqrt{2}
=12\sqrt{2}-12\sqrt{2}+7\sqrt{2}=\boldsymbol{7\sqrt{2}}
!
Use the largest perfect-square factor. \sqrt{72}=\sqrt{4}\sqrt{18}=2\sqrt{18} is not finished: \sqrt{18} still holds a 9. Going straight to \sqrt{36}\sqrt{2}=6\sqrt{2} saves a step.

Cube roots and fourth roots

The same steps work for any index. For a cube root, look for perfect cubes; for a fourth root, perfect fourth powers. The index must match before radicals can combine.

Fractions in front

A fraction in front of a radical is a coefficient: simplify the radical, then multiply the fraction by the number that came out. \tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{48}=\tfrac{1}{2}(4\sqrt{3})=2\sqrt{3}: half of four \sqrt{3}’s is two \sqrt{3}’s.
Example 4

Fraction coefficients

Simplify.

(a) \tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{48}+\tfrac{2}{3}\sqrt{27}
=\tfrac{1}{2}(4\sqrt{3})+\tfrac{2}{3}(3\sqrt{3})
=2\sqrt{3}+2\sqrt{3}=\boldsymbol{4\sqrt{3}}
(b) \tfrac{3}{4}\sqrt{32}-\tfrac{1}{5}\sqrt{50}
=\tfrac{3}{4}(4\sqrt{2})-\tfrac{1}{5}(5\sqrt{2})
=3\sqrt{2}-\sqrt{2}=\boldsymbol{2\sqrt{2}}
(c) \tfrac{2}{3}\sqrt{18}+\tfrac{1}{4}\sqrt{8}
=\tfrac{2}{3}(3\sqrt{2})+\tfrac{1}{4}(2\sqrt{2})
=2\sqrt{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{2}=\boldsymbol{\tfrac{5}{2}\sqrt{2}}
(or \tfrac{5\sqrt{2}}{2})

Radicals with Variables

Simplify each radical as in Lesson 2, then combine like radicals. Coefficients that contain variables combine only when they are like terms: 3x\sqrt{2x}+5x\sqrt{2x}=8x\sqrt{2x}. State the restrictions: for an even index, each radicand must be \ge0. A cube root needs no restriction.
Example 5

Variable radicands

Simplify. State the restrictions on the variable.

(a) \sqrt{9a}+\sqrt{25a}-\sqrt{4a}
=3\sqrt{a}+5\sqrt{a}-2\sqrt{a}=\boldsymbol{6\sqrt{a}}, \ a\ge0
(b) \sqrt{12x^3}+x\sqrt{27x}
\sqrt{12x^3}=\sqrt{4x^2}\sqrt{3x}=2x\sqrt{3x}; \ x\sqrt{27x}=3x\sqrt{3x}
Sum: \boldsymbol{5x\sqrt{3x}}, \ x\ge0
(c) \sqrt[3]{16y^4}-y\sqrt[3]{54y}
\sqrt[3]{16y^4}=\sqrt[3]{8y^3}\sqrt[3]{2y}=2y\sqrt[3]{2y}; \ y\sqrt[3]{54y}=3y\sqrt[3]{2y}
2y\sqrt[3]{2y}-3y\sqrt[3]{2y}=\boldsymbol{-y\sqrt[3]{2y}}. No restriction (y\in\mathbb{R}).
(d) \sqrt{4x-8}+\sqrt{x-2}
Factor: \sqrt{4(x-2)}=2\sqrt{x-2}
2\sqrt{x-2}+\sqrt{x-2}=\boldsymbol{3\sqrt{x-2}}, \ x\ge2
!
Factor inside first. \sqrt{4x-8} is not 2\sqrt{x}-\sqrt{8}. Take out the common factor, 4(x-2), and then the perfect square 4 comes out as 2.

Perimeter and Missing Lengths

Exact answers are left in simplest radical form. The length of a slanted segment on a grid comes from the Pythagorean theorem: draw the right triangle under it, then
length =\sqrt{(\text{run})^2+(\text{rise})^2}
To find a missing side from a perimeter, subtract the known sides from the perimeter. Put brackets around a side that has two terms before subtracting it.
Example 6

A triangle on a grid

Triangle ABC has vertices A(-3,-2), B(1,0) and C(3,-4).

(a) Determine the length of each side in simplest form.
AB: run 4, rise 2: \sqrt{16+4}=\sqrt{20}=\boldsymbol{2\sqrt{5}}
BC: run 2, rise 4: \sqrt{4+16}=\sqrt{20}=\boldsymbol{2\sqrt{5}}
AC: run 6, rise 2: \sqrt{36+4}=\sqrt{40}=\boldsymbol{2\sqrt{10}}
(b) Determine the perimeter of \triangle ABC in simplest form.
P=2\sqrt{5}+2\sqrt{5}+2\sqrt{10}=\boldsymbol{4\sqrt{5}+2\sqrt{10}} units

A missing side: take the known sides away

Lay the whole perimeter out as tiles. Take away each known side, both of its terms. What is left is the missing side.

Example 7

A missing side

The perimeter of the triangle is 7\sqrt{5}+3\sqrt{2} cm. Determine the exact length of side x in simplest form.
Known sides: \sqrt{45}=3\sqrt{5} and \sqrt{8}+\sqrt{20}=2\sqrt{2}+2\sqrt{5}.
\begin{aligned}x&=7\sqrt{5}+3\sqrt{2}-3\sqrt{5}-(2\sqrt{2}+2\sqrt{5})\\&=7\sqrt{5}-3\sqrt{5}-2\sqrt{5}+3\sqrt{2}-2\sqrt{2}\\&=\boldsymbol{2\sqrt{5}+\sqrt{2}}\text{ cm}\end{aligned}

Unknown Radical Expressions and Sequences

An equation such as y+\sqrt{27}=2\sqrt{75} is solved like a linear equation: isolate the unknown, then simplify and combine. Terms with radicals can also form an arithmetic sequence (Unit 1): find \textcolor{#c27a0e}{d} by subtracting consecutive terms, collecting the rational parts and the radical parts separately.

Balance it

Two parts, two hops

Example 8

An unknown and a sequence

(a) If y+\sqrt{27}=2\sqrt{75}, determine y in simplest form, then simplify y-\sqrt{12}.
\begin{aligned}y&=2\sqrt{75}-\sqrt{27}\\&=2(5\sqrt{3})-3\sqrt{3}=\boldsymbol{7\sqrt{3}}\end{aligned}
y-\sqrt{12}=7\sqrt{3}-2\sqrt{3}=\boldsymbol{5\sqrt{3}}
(b) The sequence 3+\sqrt{2},\ 5+4\sqrt{2},\ 7+7\sqrt{2},\ \dots is arithmetic. Determine d, the next two terms, and t_{10}.
d=(5+4\sqrt{2})-(3+\sqrt{2})=\boldsymbol{2+3\sqrt{2}}
t_4=\boldsymbol{9+10\sqrt{2}}, \ t_5=\boldsymbol{11+13\sqrt{2}}
\begin{aligned}t_{10}&=t_1+9d=3+\sqrt{2}+9(2+3\sqrt{2})\\&=3+\sqrt{2}+18+27\sqrt{2}=\boldsymbol{21+28\sqrt{2}}\end{aligned}

Key ideas

Like radicals have the same index and the same radicand. Combine them like like terms: 4\sqrt{3}+5\sqrt{3}=9\sqrt{3}, just as 4x+5x=9x.
Never add the radicands. \sqrt{9}+\sqrt{16}=7 but \sqrt{25}=5. Unlike radicals, and rational terms, stay as separate terms.
Simplify first, then combine. Use the largest perfect square (cube, fourth power) factor: \sqrt{27}-\sqrt{12}=3\sqrt{3}-2\sqrt{3}=\sqrt{3}. A fraction in front multiplies what comes out.
Variables, lengths, unknowns. State restrictions for even indices (x\ge0). Lengths: \sqrt{\text{run}^2+\text{rise}^2}. Missing side: perimeter minus (bracketed) known sides.
