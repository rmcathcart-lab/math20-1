Add 10. Then take half of the result.

Take away the number you started with.

What number did you end up with? (I already know.)

Why does everyone get the same answer?
5, whatever number you picked. Call your number n: double it, 2n; add 10, 2n+10; take half, n+5; take away n: 5. Your number cancels out, so it never mattered. Now invent a trick of your own that always lands on 7.

Multiplying Radicals

Multiply coefficients with coefficients and radicands with radicands, then simplify. Brackets expand exactly as they do in algebra, and one special pair, the conjugates, multiplies out to a number with no radical at all.

I can…
  • multiply radicals with the same index, \textcolor{#c27a0e}{m}\sqrt[n]{\textcolor{#0f7f80}{a}}\cdot \textcolor{#c27a0e}{k}\sqrt[n]{\textcolor{#0f7f80}{b}}=\textcolor{#c27a0e}{mk}\sqrt[n]{\textcolor{#0f7f80}{ab}}, and write the answer in simplest form
  • square or cube a radical: \left(\sqrt{a}\right)^2=a and \left(\sqrt[3]{a}\right)^3=a
  • multiply radicals with variables and state the restrictions
  • distribute a radical, multiply two binomials, and square a binomial that contains radicals
  • multiply conjugates and explain why the product is rational; find exact areas
AN2 Solve problems that involve operations on radicals and radical expressions with numerical and variable radicands.
Today: multiplying radicals, expanding brackets with radicals, and conjugates. Exact answers.

Multiplying Radicals with the Same Index

Radicals with the same index are multiplied by multiplying the coefficients together and the radicands together. Then write the result in simplest form.

Key idea
\textcolor{#c27a0e}{m}\sqrt[n]{\textcolor{#0f7f80}{a}}\cdot \textcolor{#c27a0e}{k}\sqrt[n]{\textcolor{#0f7f80}{b}}=\textcolor{#c27a0e}{mk}\sqrt[n]{\textcolor{#0f7f80}{ab}}
same index n; a\ge0 and b\ge0 when n is even
\left(\sqrt{a}\right)^2=a for a\ge0
\left(\sqrt[3]{a}\right)^3=a

Two useful habits:

  • Simplifying each radical before multiplying keeps the numbers small: \sqrt{12}\cdot\sqrt{27}=2\sqrt{3}\cdot3\sqrt{3}=6\cdot3=18.
  • The rule needs the same index.
!
Same index only. \sqrt{2}\cdot\sqrt[3]{2} is not \sqrt{4} or \sqrt[3]{4}: a square root and a cube root can’t be put under one radical sign. In this course it is left as it is.
Example 1

Multiply and simplify

(a) \sqrt{6}\cdot\sqrt{10}
=\sqrt{60}=\sqrt{4}\sqrt{15}=\boldsymbol{2\sqrt{15}}
(b) \left(3\sqrt{2}\right)\left(5\sqrt{7}\right)
=3\cdot5\sqrt{2\cdot7}=\boldsymbol{15\sqrt{14}}
(c) \left(-2\sqrt{6}\right)\left(4\sqrt{15}\right)
=-8\sqrt{90}=-8\left(3\sqrt{10}\right)=\boldsymbol{-24\sqrt{10}}
(d) \sqrt{12}\cdot\sqrt{27} (two ways)
Multiply first: \sqrt{324}=\boldsymbol{18}
Simplify first: 2\sqrt{3}\cdot3\sqrt{3}=6(3)=\boldsymbol{18}
(e) \left(2\sqrt[3]{4}\right)\left(3\sqrt[3]{6}\right)
=6\sqrt[3]{24}=6\sqrt[3]{8}\sqrt[3]{3}
=6(2)\sqrt[3]{3}=\boldsymbol{12\sqrt[3]{3}}
(f) \left(\sqrt{5}\right)\left(-2\sqrt{3}\right)\left(\sqrt{15}\right)
=-2\sqrt{5\cdot3\cdot15}=-2\sqrt{225}
=-2(15)=\boldsymbol{-30}

Squaring and Cubing a Radical

A square with area a has side \sqrt{a}, so \left(\sqrt{a}\right)^2=a. With a coefficient, square both parts: \left(\textcolor{#c27a0e}{k}\sqrt{\textcolor{#0f7f80}{a}}\right)^2=\textcolor{#c27a0e}{k^2}\cdot\textcolor{#0f7f80}{a}.

Example 2

Powers of radicals

Simplify.

(a) \left(\sqrt{7}\right)^2
=\boldsymbol{7}
(b) \left(5\sqrt{3}\right)^2
=5^2\left(\sqrt{3}\right)^2
=25(3)=\boldsymbol{75}
(c) -\left(2\sqrt{6}\right)^2
=-(4)(6)
=\boldsymbol{-24}
(d) \left(2\sqrt[3]{3}\right)^3
=2^3\left(\sqrt[3]{3}\right)^3
=8(3)=\boldsymbol{24}
(e) Write 6\sqrt{15} as a product of two mixed radicals in two different ways.
Split the coefficient and the radicand: 6\sqrt{15}=\left(2\sqrt{3}\right)\left(3\sqrt{5}\right) or \left(3\sqrt{3}\right)\left(2\sqrt{5}\right) or \left(6\sqrt{5}\right)\left(\sqrt{3}\right) (any two).
2

Multiplying Radicals with Variables

The same rule works with variable radicands. Multiply, then remove perfect-square (or perfect-cube) factors, including even powers of the variable. For an even index, every radicand in the question must be \ge0, so state that restriction.
Example 3

Variable radicands

Multiply and simplify. State the restrictions on the variables.

(a) \left(3\sqrt{x}\right)\left(5\sqrt{y}\right)
=\boldsymbol{15\sqrt{xy}}, x\ge0, y\ge0
(b) \left(2\sqrt{6a}\right)\left(\sqrt{10a}\right)
=2\sqrt{60a^2}=2\sqrt{4a^2}\sqrt{15}
=2(2a)\sqrt{15}=\boldsymbol{4a\sqrt{15}}, a\ge0
(c) \left(\sqrt[3]{4m^2}\right)\left(\sqrt[3]{6m^2}\right)
=\sqrt[3]{24m^4}=\sqrt[3]{8m^3}\sqrt[3]{3m}=\boldsymbol{2m\sqrt[3]{3m}}
No restriction (cube roots).
(d) \left(\sqrt{2x^3}\right)\left(3\sqrt{8x}\right)
=3\sqrt{16x^4}=3\left(4x^2\right)=\boldsymbol{12x^2}, x\ge0

Distributing a Radical

To multiply a radical by an expression in brackets, multiply it by each term (the distributive property), then simplify each product and combine like radicals.
Example 4

Distributing

Expand and simplify. State the restrictions in (c).

(a) \sqrt{3}\left(\sqrt{6}+4\right)
=\sqrt{18}+4\sqrt{3}
=\boldsymbol{3\sqrt{2}+4\sqrt{3}}
(b) 2\sqrt{5}\left(3\sqrt{10}-\sqrt{5}+\sqrt{15}\right)
=6\sqrt{50}-2\sqrt{25}+2\sqrt{75}
=6\left(5\sqrt{2}\right)-2(5)+2\left(5\sqrt{3}\right)
=\boldsymbol{30\sqrt{2}-10+10\sqrt{3}}
(c) \sqrt{x}\left(\sqrt{x}+3\sqrt{xy}\right)
=\left(\sqrt{x}\right)^2+3\sqrt{x^2y}=\boldsymbol{x+3x\sqrt{y}}, x\ge0, y\ge0
4

Multiplying Binomials

Multiply each term of the first binomial by each term of the second (“FOIL”), simplify each product, then combine like terms. To square a binomial, write it as a product of two equal binomials first.
Example 5

Binomial × binomial

Expand and simplify.

(a) \left(3+\sqrt{2}\right)\left(5-2\sqrt{2}\right)
=15-6\sqrt{2}+5\sqrt{2}-2\left(\sqrt{2}\right)^2
=15-\sqrt{2}-4=\boldsymbol{11-\sqrt{2}}
(b) \left(2\sqrt{3}+\sqrt{5}\right)\left(\sqrt{3}-4\sqrt{5}\right)
=2(3)-8\sqrt{15}+\sqrt{15}-4(5)
=6-7\sqrt{15}-20=\boldsymbol{-14-7\sqrt{15}}
(c) \left(\sqrt{6}-\sqrt{2}\right)\left(\sqrt{3}+2\sqrt{2}\right)
=\sqrt{18}+2\sqrt{12}-\sqrt{6}-2\sqrt{4}
=\boldsymbol{3\sqrt{2}+4\sqrt{3}-\sqrt{6}-4} (no like radicals left)
4

Squaring a Binomial

Key idea
(a+b)^2=a^2+2ab+b^2
(a-b)^2=a^2-2ab+b^2
Never (a+b)^2=a^2+b^2: that forgets the two middle terms.
Example 6

Squares of binomials

Expand and simplify. State the restrictions in (c).

(a) \left(\sqrt{5}+3\right)^2
=\left(\sqrt{5}+3\right)\left(\sqrt{5}+3\right)
=5+3\sqrt{5}+3\sqrt{5}+9
=\boldsymbol{14+6\sqrt{5}}
(b) \left(2\sqrt{7}-\sqrt{3}\right)^2
=\left(2\sqrt{7}\right)^2-2\left(2\sqrt{7}\right)\left(\sqrt{3}\right)+\left(\sqrt{3}\right)^2
=28-4\sqrt{21}+3
=\boldsymbol{31-4\sqrt{21}}
(c) \left(\sqrt{x}-2\sqrt{y}\right)^2
=x-2\sqrt{x}\left(2\sqrt{y}\right)+4y
=\boldsymbol{x-4\sqrt{xy}+4y},
x\ge0, y\ge0
5

Conjugates

The conjugate of a\sqrt{b}+c\sqrt{d} is a\sqrt{b}-c\sqrt{d}: the same two terms, the opposite sign between them. When conjugates are multiplied, the two middle terms are opposites and cancel, and squaring each term removes the radicals.
\left(a\sqrt{b}+c\sqrt{d}\right)\left(a\sqrt{b}-c\sqrt{d}\right)=a^2b-c^2d
The product of conjugates is rational.

A number like \sqrt{7} (the square root of a whole number that is not a perfect square) is irrational: its decimal never ends or repeats. Conjugates show that the product of two irrational numbers can be rational. Conjugates are used in Lesson 6 to remove radicals from a denominator.

Example 7

Conjugates

Write the conjugate of each expression, then multiply the expression by its conjugate. State the restrictions in (d).

(a) \sqrt{7}+2
Conjugate: \sqrt{7}-2
\left(\sqrt{7}+2\right)\left(\sqrt{7}-2\right)=7-2\sqrt{7}+2\sqrt{7}-4=\boldsymbol{3}
(b) 3\sqrt{2}-\sqrt{5}
Conjugate: 3\sqrt{2}+\sqrt{5}
Product: \left(3\sqrt{2}\right)^2-\left(\sqrt{5}\right)^2=18-5=\boldsymbol{13}
(c) 4+2\sqrt{6}
Conjugate: 4-2\sqrt{6}
Product: 4^2-\left(2\sqrt{6}\right)^2=16-24=\boldsymbol{-8}
(d) \sqrt{a}+3\sqrt{b}
Conjugate: \sqrt{a}-3\sqrt{b}
Product: \boldsymbol{a-9b}, a\ge0, b\ge0
6

Area Problems

Area formulas work the same way with radical side lengths: rectangle A=lw, square A=s^2, triangle A=\tfrac{1}{2}bh. Give the answer exactly, in simplest form.
Example 8

Exact areas

Determine the area of each figure in simplest form. Dimensions are in centimetres.

(a) Rectangle
A=\sqrt{3}\left(3\sqrt{6}+\sqrt{2}\right)
=3\sqrt{18}+\sqrt{6}
=\boldsymbol{9\sqrt{2}+\sqrt{6}} cm²
(b) Square
A=\left(2\sqrt{5}-1\right)^2
=20-4\sqrt{5}+1
=\boldsymbol{21-4\sqrt{5}} cm²
(c) Triangle
A=\tfrac{1}{2}\left(4\sqrt{3}+2\right)\left(4\sqrt{3}-2\right)
=\tfrac{1}{2}(48-4)
=\boldsymbol{22} cm² (conjugates)
✓

Key ideas

Same index: multiply across. Coefficients × coefficients, radicands × radicands: m\sqrt[n]{a}\cdot k\sqrt[n]{b}=mk\sqrt[n]{ab}. Then pull out perfect squares (or cubes). Simplifying first keeps numbers small.
Squares undo roots. \left(\sqrt{a}\right)^2=a, \left(k\sqrt{a}\right)^2=k^2a. With variables, even powers come out; state x\ge0 for an even index (no restriction for cube roots).
Every term × every term. Distribute, or fill the four cells of the area model, simplify each product, then combine like radicals. (a+b)^2=a^2+2ab+b^2, never a^2+b^2.
Conjugates. a\sqrt{b}+c\sqrt{d} and a\sqrt{b}-c\sqrt{d}: the middle terms cancel, so the product a^2b-c^2d is rational.
