Two of these are true. One is a lie. Which one is the lie?

A💇Hair grows about 1.25 cm a month. In 2 years it grows about 30 cm.
B🐷Put $5 in a piggy bank every week. After 4 years it holds more than $1000.

Arithmetic Sequences in Context

Rebuilding a whole sequence from just two of its terms, then putting arithmetic sequences to work: money that grows by the same amount, values that decay by the same amount, and the straight line hiding behind every arithmetic sequence.

I can…
  • find d and t_1 from two terms that are not consecutive, by counting the jumps or by solving a system
  • model arithmetic growth (simple interest, a fee plus a rate) and arithmetic decay (depreciation, cooling) with a general term
  • decide what t_1 stands for (at the start or after one period) and match the position to the time asked for
  • solve “when does it first pass…” questions with an inequality, round to the right natural number and check both sides
  • see an arithmetic sequence as a linear function: slope d, domain the natural numbers

Two Terms That Are Not Consecutive

If two terms t_p and t_q are known (with p<q), you can still find \textcolor{#c27a0e}{d}. Going from position p to position q takes q-p steps, and each step adds \textcolor{#c27a0e}{d}:
\textcolor{#c27a0e}{d}=\dfrac{t_q-t_p}{q-p}(change in the terms \div change in the positions)
Then substitute either known term into t_n=\textcolor{#0e7c7b}{t_1}+(n-1)\textcolor{#c27a0e}{d} to find \textcolor{#0e7c7b}{t_1}.

Split the gap into equal jumps

Two tiles are known. Count the jumps between them, share the change equally, then turn the tiles into points.

!
Count jumps, not terms. From t_4 to t_{11} there are 8 terms but only 11-4=7 jumps. Dividing by 8 is the most common slip.

A second method: a system of equations

Write both known terms with the general term formula. This gives a system of two linear equations in \textcolor{#0e7c7b}{t_1} and \textcolor{#c27a0e}{d}. Subtract one equation from the other to eliminate \textcolor{#0e7c7b}{t_1}.
Example 1

Two ways to find d and t_1

An arithmetic sequence has t_4=19 and t_{11}=54.

(a) Determine d by counting steps.
From t_4 to t_{11} is 11-4=7 steps.
d=\dfrac{54-19}{7}=\dfrac{35}{7}=\boldsymbol{5}
(b) Determine t_1 and the general term.
19=t_1+3(5)\ \Rightarrow\ t_1=\boldsymbol{4}
t_n=4+(n-1)(5)=\boldsymbol{5n-1}
(c) Solve the problem again using a system of equations.
t_4:\ \ t_1+3d=19\qquad t_{11}:\ \ t_1+10d=54
Subtract: 7d=35\ \Rightarrow\ d=\boldsymbol{5}. Then t_1+3(5)=19\ \Rightarrow\ t_1=\boldsymbol{4}.
Example 2

A negative, non-integer difference

An arithmetic sequence has t_6=-8 and t_{20}=-43. Determine d, t_1, the general term and t_{50}.

Show your work.
d=\dfrac{-43-(-8)}{20-6}=\dfrac{-35}{14}=\boldsymbol{-2.5}
-8=t_1+5(-2.5)\ \Rightarrow\ t_1=\boldsymbol{4.5}
t_n=4.5+(n-1)(-2.5)=\boldsymbol{7-2.5n}\qquad t_{50}=7-2.5(50)=\boldsymbol{-118}
Negative terms on the TI-84. Use the (−) key for a negative number and brackets around the top and the bottom: ((−)43−(−)8)÷(20−6)ENTER gives −2.5.

Two terms in a story: rows of seats

In context, the positions are often row numbers, years or hours. The same jump counting works, and “how many rows?” is “which position?”.

Example 3

The amphitheatre

Each row of an outdoor amphitheatre has the same number of extra seats as the row in front of it. Row 6 has 64 seats and row 16 has 94 seats.

(a) How many seats are in row 1?
d=\dfrac{94-64}{16-6}=3
64=t_1+5(3)\ \Rightarrow\ t_1=\boldsymbol{49} seats
(b) The back row has 133 seats. How many rows are there?
133=49+3(n-1)
84=3(n-1)\ \Rightarrow\ n=\boldsymbol{29} rows

Arithmetic Growth

A quantity shows arithmetic growth when it increases by the same amount every period: a fixed raise each year, a fixed deposit each week, a set fee plus an hourly rate, and simple interest. With simple interest, interest is paid only on the original amount invested (the principal), so the interest is the same every year:
interest per year = principal \times annual rate

Before writing a sequence, decide what \textcolor{#0e7c7b}{t_1} stands for:

\textcolor{#0e7c7b}{t_1} = value at the start (time 0)
The value after k periods is t_{k+1}.
\textcolor{#0e7c7b}{t_1} = value after the first period
The value after k periods is t_k.

“When does it first pass…?”

“When does it first pass…” leads to an inequality. Solve it, round to the natural number that fits the question (not the usual rounding rule), and check the terms on both sides.

Solve the inequality in one line. For 90n+2400>4000: (4000−2400)÷90ENTER gives 17.77777778, so n>17.8.
Example 4

Simple interest

Marco invests $2400 at 3.75% simple interest per year.

(a) How much interest does he earn each year? Write the values at the end of years 1, 2 and 3.
2400\times0.0375=\boldsymbol{\$90} per year
$2490, $2580, $2670
(b) Let t_1 be the value at the end of year 1. Write the general term and find the value after 15 years.
t_n=2490+90(n-1)=\boldsymbol{90n+2400}
After 15 years: t_{15}=90(15)+2400=\boldsymbol{\$3750}
(c) Redo (b) with t_1=2400, the value when he invests.
2400,\ 2490,\ 2580,\ \dots: after 15 years is t_{16}.
t_{16}=2400+15(90)=\boldsymbol{\$3750} (same answer, different position)
(d) After how many full years is the value first more than $4000?
90n+2400>4000\ \Rightarrow\ n>17.8
After 18 years ($4020; after 17 years it is $3930).

A fixed fee plus a rate

When each job costs the same amount more than the last, \textcolor{#c27a0e}{d} is the rate. The fixed fee is what is left when you take one rate off \textcolor{#0e7c7b}{t_1}: the cost of a “0-hour job”.

Example 5

The landscaping company

A landscaping company charges a fixed equipment fee plus an hourly rate. A 1-hour job costs $115, a 2-hour job costs $160 and a 3-hour job costs $205.

(a) State t_1 and d. What does d represent?
t_1=\boldsymbol{115}, d=\boldsymbol{45}
d is the hourly rate, $45 per hour.
(b) Determine the fixed fee and the general term.
Fee =115-45=\boldsymbol{\$70}
t_n=115+45(n-1)=\boldsymbol{45n+70}
(c) What does an 8-hour job cost?
t_8=45(8)+70=\boldsymbol{\$430}
(d) A customer has $600. What is the longest job, in whole hours, they can pay for?
45n+70\le600\ \Rightarrow\ n\le11.8
11 hours ($565; 12 hours would be $610).

Arithmetic Decay

A quantity shows arithmetic decay when it decreases by the same amount every period, so \textcolor{#c27a0e}{d} is negative. Examples: equipment losing the same dollar value each year (straight-line depreciation), air temperature falling as altitude increases, and a candle burning down. The same care applies: state what \textcolor{#0e7c7b}{t_1} means, and match the position to the time asked for.

Years are positions too

When t_1 is a year, every other year has a position: count from t_1.

Example 6

The food truck

A food truck was bought new in 2018. It loses the same amount of value every year. In 2021 it was worth $96 800, and in 2026 it was worth $63 800. Let t_1 be its value in 2018.

(a) Determine the annual depreciation.
2021 is t_4 and 2026 is t_9.
d=\dfrac{63\,800-96\,800}{9-4}=-6600
It loses $6600 per year.
(b) Determine the purchase price.
96\,800=t_1+3(-6600)
t_1=96\,800+19\,800=\boldsymbol{\$116\,600}
(c) In what year will it first be worth less than $15 000?
t_n=116\,600-6600(n-1)=123\,200-6600n<15\,000\ \Rightarrow\ n>16.4, so n=17.
t_{17} is the year 2018+16= 2034: worth $11 000. (In 2033, t_{16}=\$17\,600.)

Up, up and cooler: readings every 100 m

The positions don’t have to be times. Here they are readings, one every 100 m, starting with reading 1 at the ground.

Example 7

The weather balloon

A weather balloon is launched from a station where the air temperature is 21 °C. It records the temperature every 100 m as it rises. The temperature drops 0.65 °C for every 100 m. Let t_1=21, the reading at the ground.

(a) Write the first three readings and the general term.
21,\ 20.35,\ 19.7,\ \dots
t_n=21-0.65(n-1)=\boldsymbol{21.65-0.65n}
(b) What is the temperature 2500 m above the station?
2500 m is 25 steps of 100 m, so n=26.
t_{26}=21.65-0.65(26)=\boldsymbol{4.75} °C
(c) At what height is the first reading below -10 °C?
21.65-0.65n<-10\ \Rightarrow\ n>48.7, so n=49: t_{49}=-10.2 °C.
Reading 49 is 48\times100= 4800 m above the station.

Arithmetic Sequences and Linear Functions

Plot the points (n,\,t_n) of an arithmetic sequence and they lie on a line: each step right by 1 goes up (or down) by \textcolor{#c27a0e}{d}, so the slope is \textcolor{#c27a0e}{d}. Expanding the general term shows the same thing:
t_n=\textcolor{#0e7c7b}{t_1}+(n-1)\textcolor{#c27a0e}{d}=\textcolor{#c27a0e}{d}\,n+(\textcolor{#0e7c7b}{t_1}-\textcolor{#c27a0e}{d})the form t_n=\textcolor{#c27a0e}{m}n+\textcolor{#7b4fc4}{b} with \textcolor{#c27a0e}{m=d} and \textcolor{#7b4fc4}{b=t_1-d}
The value \textcolor{#7b4fc4}{b} is where the line crosses the vertical axis, one step before t_1. It is not a term: the domain is the natural numbers, so there is no point at n=0 and the points are not joined. Going the other way, any linear function y=mx+b with its domain restricted to the natural numbers gives an arithmetic sequence with d=m.
!
b is not t_1. In t_n=4n+5, the first term is t_1=4(1)+5=9, not 5. The 5 is the height of the line at n=0, where there is no term.
Example 8

Reading a sequence off its graph

The graph shows the first four terms of an arithmetic sequence.

(a) State t_1 and d, and write the general term in the form t_n=mn+b.
t_1=\boldsymbol{9}, d=13-9=\boldsymbol{4}
t_n=9+4(n-1)=\boldsymbol{4n+5}
(b) What do the slope and the vertical intercept of the line through the points represent? Determine t_{25}.
Slope 4=d. Intercept 5=t_1-d (not a term, since n=0 is not in the domain).
t_{25}=4(25)+5=\boldsymbol{105}
(c) The function f(x)=30-2x has its domain restricted to the natural numbers. List the first four values of the range and show that they form an arithmetic sequence.
f(1)=28,\ f(2)=26,\ f(3)=24,\ f(4)=22. Each value is 2 less than the one before: arithmetic, d=\boldsymbol{-2} (the slope).
✓

Key ideas

Two terms are enough. d=\dfrac{t_q-t_p}{q-p}: the change in the terms over the number of jumps. Then substitute a known term to get t_1 (or solve the system and subtract).
Decide what t_1 means. At the start (time 0): after k periods is t_{k+1}. After the first period: after k periods is t_k.
Growth and decay. The same change every period: d>0 grows (simple interest, fee plus rate), d<0 decays (depreciation, cooling). “First passes” → inequality → round to fit the question → check both sides.
It’s a line, in dots. t_n=dn+(t_1-d): slope d, intercept t_1-d (not a term). The domain is n=1,2,3,\dots, so the points are not joined.
