Arithmetic Sequences

Sequences that change by the same amount every step. Two numbers, the first term t_1 and the common difference d, tell you every term: the 5th, the 30th, even the 500th.

I can…
  • find the common difference d and decide whether a sequence is arithmetic
  • explain why it takes n-1 jumps to get from t_1 to t_n, and write the general term t_n=t_1+(n-1)d
  • use the general term to find a term, or to decide which term a number is (or whether it is a term at all)
  • find the number of terms, the first term t_1 or the common difference d
  • insert arithmetic means between two numbers
  • solve for an unknown when three expressions are consecutive terms of an arithmetic sequence
RF9 Analyze arithmetic sequences and series to solve problems.
Today: arithmetic sequences: the common difference, the general term, and solving for any one of t_n, t_1, n or d.
Warm-up

Fence Posts and Clock Chimes

A fence has a post at both ends and a post every 3 m. A rail joins each post to the next one.

1
The fence is 12 m long. How many posts does it need?
5 posts, not 4. 12\div3=4 counts the gaps; the posts are one more: at 0, 3, 6, 9 and 12 m.
2
A longer fence has 10 posts. How many rails? How long is it?
9 rails (one fewer than the posts), so it is 9\times3=27 m long.
!
The big one. A clock tower takes 5 seconds to ring 6 chimes (from the first chime to the last, evenly spaced). How long does it take to ring 12 chimes? Guess first.
A 10 s
B 11 s
C 12 s
D 14 s
B, 11 seconds. 6 chimes have only 5 gaps, so each gap is 1 s. 12 chimes have 11 gaps: 11 s.
A doubles the time, but doubling the chimes does not double the gaps. Chimes are posts; the seconds are the rails between them. Today: the terms of a sequence are the posts, and the jumps between them are always one fewer.

Ask your neighbour: you climb from floor 1 to floor 10. How many flights of stairs is that?

1

The Common Difference

In an arithmetic sequence, each term after the first is found by adding the same number to the term before it. That number is the common difference, d. To find d, subtract any term from the term that comes right after it.
\textcolor{#c27a0e}{d}=t_2-t_1=t_3-t_2=t_4-t_3=\dots=t_n-t_{n-1}

If d>0 the terms increase; if d<0 they decrease. The terms may be fractions, decimals or algebraic expressions.

!
Check every difference you can. One matching pair is not enough to call a sequence arithmetic: 1,3,5,9 starts +2,+2 and then jumps +4.
Example 1

Arithmetic or not?

Decide whether each sequence is arithmetic. If it is, state d and the next two terms.

(a) 13,\ 21,\ 29,\ 37,\ \dots
Differences: 8,\ 8,\ 8.
Arithmetic, \boldsymbol{d=8}.
Next: \boldsymbol{45,\ 53}
(b) 6,\ 1.5,\ -3,\ -7.5,\ \dots
Differences: -4.5,\ -4.5,\ -4.5.
Arithmetic, \boldsymbol{d=-4.5}.
Next: \boldsymbol{-12,\ -16.5}
(c) \frac{1}{3},\ \frac{5}{6},\ \frac{4}{3},\ \frac{11}{6},\ \dots
In sixths: \frac{2}{6},\ \frac{5}{6},\ \frac{8}{6},\ \frac{11}{6}.
Arithmetic, \boldsymbol{d=\frac{1}{2}}.
Next: \boldsymbol{\frac{14}{6}=\frac{7}{3},\ \frac{17}{6}}
(d) 3,\ 6,\ 12,\ 24,\ \dots
Differences: 3,\ 6,\ 12 (not equal).
Not arithmetic.
(e) 4a+b,\ \ 7a-b,\ \ 10a-3b,\ \dots
(7a-b)-(4a+b)=3a-2b
(10a-3b)-(7a-b)=3a-2b
Arithmetic, \boldsymbol{d=3a-2b}.
Next: \boldsymbol{13a-5b,\ \ 16a-7b}
(f) 2,\ 5,\ 9,\ 14,\ \dots
Differences: 3,\ 4,\ 5 (not equal).
Not arithmetic.
2

The General Term

Build the terms from t_1. Each step to the right adds one more d:
\textcolor{#0e7c86}{t_1},\quad t_2=\textcolor{#0e7c86}{t_1}+\textcolor{#c27a0e}{d},\quad t_3=\textcolor{#0e7c86}{t_1}+2\textcolor{#c27a0e}{d},\quad t_4=\textcolor{#0e7c86}{t_1}+3\textcolor{#c27a0e}{d},\quad\dots
The number of d’s is always one less than the position, because it takes n-1 steps to get from t_1 to t_n.

Why n-1 and not n?

The terms are the fence posts. The jumps are the rails between them, so there is always one fewer jump than terms.

General term of an arithmetic sequence
t_n=\textcolor{#0e7c86}{t_1}+(\textcolor{#1f5bd6}{n}-1)\textcolor{#c27a0e}{d}
t_n the term in position n
\textcolor{#0e7c86}{t_1} the first term (also written a)
\textcolor{#c27a0e}{d} the common difference
\textcolor{#1f5bd6}{n} the position, a natural number 1,2,3,\dots

Writing the general term

To write the general term of a given sequence, substitute t_1 and d, then expand and simplify.

Think In t_n=48-7n, where can you see the common difference? And what is the 48: is it a term of 41,34,27,\dots?
The number in front of n is d=-7: each step adds -7. The 48 is not a term: it is what you would get one step before t_1 (41+7=48), at the position n=0, which does not exist.
Example 2

Using the general term

Consider the arithmetic sequence 41,\ 34,\ 27,\ 20,\ \dots

(a) Determine the general term in simplest form.
t_1=41,\quad d=34-41=-7
t_n=41+(n-1)(-7)=41-7n+7
\boldsymbol{t_n=48-7n}
(b) Determine t_{30}.
t_{30}=48-7(30)=48-210=\boldsymbol{-162}
(c) Which term is equal to -295?
48-7n=-295\ \Rightarrow\ -7n=-343\ \Rightarrow\ n=49
-295=\boldsymbol{t_{49}}
(d) Is -300 a term of the sequence?
48-7n=-300\ \Rightarrow\ -7n=-348\ \Rightarrow\ n\approx49.7
Not a natural number, so -300 is not a term.

Which term is it? Is it a term at all?

Set the general term equal to the number and solve for n. The number is a term only if n comes out as a natural number (1,2,3,\dots).

Solve for n in one line. From 48-7n=-295: n=(-295-48)\div(-7). Use (−) for a negative number: ((−)295−48)÷(−)7ENTER gives 49. Do the same for -300 and look at the decimal.

Knowing a term in the middle

If you know any term and d, you can go both ways: add d to step right, subtract d to step left.

Example 3

Going both ways

(a) An arithmetic sequence has t_4=11 and d=-3. Write the first six terms.
Go left by subtracting d (adding 3): t_3=14,\ t_2=17,\ t_1=20. Go right by adding d: t_5=8,\ t_6=5.
The sequence is \boldsymbol{20,\ 17,\ 14,\ 11,\ 8,\ 5}.
(b) Fill in the missing terms of the arithmetic sequence with d=2.5.
-3.5-11.546.5
Right: 4+2.5=6.5. Left: 4-2.5=1.5,\ \ 1.5-2.5=-1,\ \ -1-2.5=-3.5.
\boldsymbol{-3.5,\ -1,\ 1.5,\ 4,\ 6.5}
3

The Number of Terms

A finite sequence ends with a last term, and that last term is t_n. Substitute t_1, d and the last term into t_n=t_1+(n-1)d, then solve for n. The answer must be a natural number.
To count the multiples of a number in an interval, find the smallest and the largest multiple in the interval. They are t_1 and t_n, and d is the number itself. “Inclusive” means the endpoints count; “between” means they do not.
Jumps, then add 1. n=(t_n-t_1)\div d+1: for 8,2,-4,\dots,-166 press ((−)166−8)÷(−)6+1ENTER. For multiples of 9, divide the endpoints by 9 to find the first and last multiples inside.
Example 4

How many terms?

(a) How many terms are in the sequence 8,\ 2,\ -4,\ \dots,\ -166?
t_1=8,\ d=-6,\ t_n=-166
-166=8+(n-1)(-6)
-174=-6(n-1)\ \Rightarrow\ n-1=29\ \Rightarrow\ n=30
There are 30 terms.
(b) How many multiples of 9 are there between 100 and 600?
100\div9\approx11.1, so the first multiple is 9\times12=108. 600\div9\approx66.7, so the last is 9\times66=594.
594=108+(n-1)(9)\ \Rightarrow\ 486=9(n-1)\ \Rightarrow\ n-1=54\ \Rightarrow\ n=55
There are 55 multiples of 9. (Check: multiples number 12 to 66 is 66-12+1=55.)

A pattern you can count: toothpick squares

Each new square shares a side with the one before it, so it needs fewer new toothpicks than the first one did.

Example 5

Toothpicks

Toothpicks are laid out to make a row of squares.

(a) Write the number of toothpicks for 1, 2, 3 and 4 squares, and the general term.
4,\ 7,\ 10,\ 13: arithmetic, t_1=4,\ d=3
t_n=4+(n-1)(3)=\boldsymbol{3n+1}
(b) How many toothpicks make a row of 40 squares?
t_{40}=3(40)+1=\boldsymbol{121} toothpicks
(c) There are 200 toothpicks in a box. What is the longest row of squares that can be made, and how many toothpicks are left over?
3n+1=200\ \Rightarrow\ n=66.\overline{3}. Round down: 66 squares.
t_{66}=3(66)+1=199, so 1 toothpick is left over.
4

Finding t_1 or d

The formula t_n=\textcolor{#0e7c86}{t_1}+(\textcolor{#1f5bd6}{n}-1)\textcolor{#c27a0e}{d} links four quantities: t_n, t_1, n and d. If you know any three of them, substitute and solve for the fourth.

In pictures: between two known terms there are a known number of equal jumps. Share the gap equally to find d, or walk the jumps back to find t_1.

Example 6

Three known, find the fourth

(a) An arithmetic sequence has d=6 and t_{15}=71. Determine t_1.
71=t_1+(15-1)(6)
71=t_1+84
\boldsymbol{t_1=-13}
(b) An arithmetic sequence has t_1=4 and t_{13}=-38. Determine d and the general term.
-38=4+(13-1)d
-42=12d\ \Rightarrow\ \boldsymbol{d=-3.5}
t_n=4+(n-1)(-3.5)=\boldsymbol{7.5-3.5n}
(c) An arithmetic sequence has 16 terms. The first term is 12 and the last term is 87. Determine d.
t_{16}=87:
87=12+15d\ \Rightarrow\ 75=15d\ \Rightarrow\ \boldsymbol{d=5}
5

Arithmetic Means

The terms between two non-consecutive terms of an arithmetic sequence are called arithmetic means. In 4,\ \mathbf{10},\ \mathbf{16},\ 22, the arithmetic means between 4 and 22 are 10 and 16.

To insert k arithmetic means between two numbers, use the two numbers as t_1 and the last term. The sequence then has k+2 terms. Find d, then build the means. One arithmetic mean between two numbers is simply their average.

Example 7

Inserting means

(a) Insert four arithmetic means between 7 and -18.
4 means + 2 ends = 6 terms: t_1=7,\ t_6=-18
-18=7+5d\ \Rightarrow\ d=-5
7,\ \mathbf{2},\ \mathbf{-3},\ \mathbf{-8},\ \mathbf{-13},\ -18
The means are 2,\ -3,\ -8,\ -13.
(b) Insert one arithmetic mean between -9 and 26.
3 terms: t_1=-9,\ t_3=26
26=-9+2d\ \Rightarrow\ d=17.5
Mean: -9+17.5=\boldsymbol{8.5}
(Same as the average: \frac{-9+26}{2}=8.5.)
Think Why is one arithmetic mean always the average of the two numbers?
With one mean there are two equal jumps, so the mean sits exactly halfway between the two numbers. Halfway between two numbers is their average.
6

Unknown Terms in an Arithmetic Sequence

If three expressions are consecutive terms of an arithmetic sequence, the two differences are equal:
t_2-t_1=t_3-t_2 or, the same thing, t_2=\dfrac{t_1+t_3}{2} (the middle term is the mean of its neighbours)

Set the differences equal, solve for the variable, then substitute to find the terms.

Example 8

Three expressions

The expressions x-4, 2x+1 and 5x-2 are consecutive terms of an arithmetic sequence.

(a) Determine the value of x.
(2x+1)-(x-4)=(5x-2)-(2x+1)
x+5=3x-3
8=2x\ \Rightarrow\ \boldsymbol{x=4}
(b) State the three terms, the general term and t_{20}.
Terms: 4-4=0,\ \ 2(4)+1=9,\ \ 5(4)-2=18
t_1=0,\ d=9:\ \ t_n=0+(n-1)(9)=\boldsymbol{9n-9}
t_{20}=9(20)-9=\boldsymbol{171}
✓

Key ideas

Common difference. d=t_2-t_1, the same for every pair of neighbours. Check them all; one matching pair is not enough.
General term. t_n=\textcolor{#0e7c86}{t_1}+(n-1)\textcolor{#c27a0e}{d}: from t_1 to t_n is n-1 jumps (posts and rails). Expand and simplify, e.g. 48-7n.
Know three, find the fourth. Substitute and solve for t_n, t_1, n or d. A position n must be a natural number, or the number is not a term.
Means and unknowns. k means make k+2 terms and k+1 jumps. For three expressions in a row, set the two differences equal.
