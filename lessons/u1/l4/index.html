Arithmetic Series

A sequence lists the terms; a series adds them up. Write the sum forwards and backwards and every column has the same total, so even a hundred terms add up in moments.

I can…
  • tell a series from a sequence, and find partial sums S_1,\ S_2,\ S_3,\ \dots
  • add an arithmetic series by pairing (writing it forwards and backwards)
  • use both sum formulas: S_n=\dfrac{n}{2}\left[2\textcolor{#0f7f80}{t_1}+(n-1)\textcolor{#c27a0e}{d}\right] and S_n=\dfrac{n}{2}(\textcolor{#0f7f80}{t_1}+\textcolor{#1f5bd6}{t_n})
  • work backwards from a sum to find n (a quadratic), t_1 or d, and solve word problems
  • find terms from a sum formula with t_n=S_n-S_{n-1}

Sequences and Series

A series is the sum of the terms of a sequence. A sequence is written with commas; a series is written with plus signs. The arithmetic sequence 4,\ 9,\ 14,\ 19 gives the arithmetic series 4+9+14+19.
sequence4,\ 9,\ 14,\ 19
series4+9+14+19=46

\textcolor{#7a1f3d}{S_n} is the sum of the first n terms: S_1=t_1, S_2=t_1+t_2, S_3=t_1+t_2+t_3, and so on. These running totals are called partial sums.

Example 1

A series and its partial sums

Consider the arithmetic sequence 7,\ 12,\ 17,\ 22,\ 27,\ 32,\ \dots

(a) Write the series of the first six terms.
7+12+17+22+27+32
(b) Complete the table of partial sums.
n123456
S_n719365885117
Keep adding the next term: 7,\ 7+12=19,\ 19+17=36,\ 36+22=58,\ 58+27=85,\ 85+32=117
(c) Calculate S_4-S_3. Which term is it?
S_4-S_3=58-36=\boldsymbol{22}, which is \boldsymbol{t_4}.

Adding by Pairing

A long arithmetic series can be added quickly by writing it forwards and backwards and adding the two lines. Each column adds to the same total, \textcolor{#0f7f80}{t_1}+\textcolor{#1f5bd6}{t_n}, and there are n columns:
2S_n=n(\textcolor{#0f7f80}{t_1}+\textcolor{#1f5bd6}{t_n})
This is the trick the young Carl Friedrich Gauss is said to have used to add 1+2+3+\dots+100 in moments.

Forwards and backwards

The same idea with blocks

Build each term as a column of blocks. Two copies of the staircase fit together into a rectangle.

Think Why does every column have the same total? Would pairing still work for 1+2+4+8+16?
Moving one column right, the top number goes up by d and the bottom number goes down by d, so the total stays the same. In 1+2+4+8+16 there is no common difference: the columns are 17,\ 10,\ 8,\ 10,\ 17, not all equal, so pairing fails.
Example 2

Pairing

Use pairing to find the sum 5+10+15+\dots+295+300.

There are 300\div5=60 terms.
\begin{aligned}S&=5+10+15+\dots+295+300\\ S&=300+295+290+\dots+10+5\\ 2S&=305+305+305+\dots+305+305\quad(60\text{ columns})\end{aligned}
2S=60(305)=18\,300, so S=\boldsymbol{9\,150}.
Math HistoryBraunschweig · c. 1786

The schoolboy and the slate

The story goes that Büttner, a teacher in Braunschweig (Germany), gave his class a long sum to keep them busy. Nine-year-old Carl Friedrich Gauss wrote one number on his slate, put it down and said, “There it lies.” Only his answer was right.

The oldest telling (1856) only says “an arithmetic series.” Later books made it 1+2+\dots+100. In 1937, E. T. Bell used a nastier one:

81\,297+81\,495+81\,693+\dots+100\,899
Each step is 198. How many terms are there, and what is the sum?
100\,899=81\,297+198(n-1)
n-1=99, so 100 terms.
S_{100}=50(182\,196)=\boldsymbol{9\,109\,800}

The Sum Formulas

Pairing works for every arithmetic series. Replacing \textcolor{#1f5bd6}{t_n} with \textcolor{#0f7f80}{t_1}+(n-1)\textcolor{#c27a0e}{d} gives a second version of the formula.

Sum of an arithmetic series
S_n=\dfrac{n}{2}\left[2\textcolor{#0f7f80}{t_1}+(n-1)\textcolor{#c27a0e}{d}\right]
when you know \textcolor{#c27a0e}{d}
or
S_n=\dfrac{n}{2}(\textcolor{#0f7f80}{t_1}+\textcolor{#1f5bd6}{t_n})
when you know the last term \textcolor{#1f5bd6}{t_n}
S_n = sum of the first n terms · \textcolor{#0f7f80}{t_1} = first term · \textcolor{#c27a0e}{d} = common difference · n = number of terms · \textcolor{#1f5bd6}{t_n} = last term

Use the first formula when d is known and the second when the last term is known. If the last term is given but n is not, find n first with t_n=t_1+(n-1)d.

Using the formulas

Determine each sum.

(a) The first 25 terms of 7+11+15+\dots
t_1=7,\ d=4,\ n=25
S_{25}=\tfrac{25}{2}\left[2(7)+24(4)\right]
=\tfrac{25}{2}(110)=\boldsymbol{1\,375}
(b) The first 30 terms of -20+(-17.5)+(-15)+\dots
t_1=-20,\ d=2.5,\ n=30
S_{30}=\tfrac{30}{2}\left[2(-20)+29(2.5)\right]
=15(32.5)=\boldsymbol{487.5}
(c) S_{18} when t_1=-12 and t_{18}=73
S_{18}=\tfrac{18}{2}(-12+73)
=9(61)=\boldsymbol{549}
One line on the calculator. Brackets keep the order right: 25÷2×(2×7+24×4)=. For a negative first term use (−), not −.
Example 4

Last term known, n unknown

Determine the sum 52+45+38+\dots+(-39).

t_1=52,\ d=-7,\ t_n=-39. Find n first:
-39=52+(n-1)(-7)\Rightarrow -91=-7(n-1)\Rightarrow n-1=13\Rightarrow n=14
S_{14}=\tfrac{14}{2}\left(52+(-39)\right)=7(13)=\boldsymbol{91}

Working Backwards: Finding n, t_1 or d

Substitute everything you know into a sum formula and solve for the unknown.
  • Finding t_1 or d gives a linear equation.
  • Finding n gives a quadratic equation. Expand, move every term to one side, divide out any common factor, then factor. n must be a natural number, so reject a negative or fractional root. You can also make a table of values (with a calculator) of S_n for n=1,\ 2,\ 3,\ \dots and look for the sum.

Finding n

Example 5

How many terms?

How many terms of the series 6+10+14+\dots must be added to give a sum of 336?

t_1=6,\ d=4,\ S_n=336
336=\tfrac{n}{2}\left[2(6)+(n-1)(4)\right]=\tfrac{n}{2}(4n+8)=n(2n+4)
2n^2+4n-336=0
n^2+2n-168=0 (divide by 2)
(n+14)(n-12)=0\Rightarrow n=12 (n=-14 is rejected: n must be a natural number)
12 terms must be added. (Check: t_{12}=50, S_{12}=6(6+50)=336.)
Check your n. Put it back into the formula: 12÷2×(2×6+11×4)= should give the sum, 336.
Example 6

Finding t_1 or d

(a) An arithmetic series has S_{20}=650 and d=3. Determine t_1.
650=\tfrac{20}{2}\left[2t_1+19(3)\right]
650=10(2t_1+57)
65=2t_1+57\Rightarrow t_1=\boldsymbol{4}
(b) An arithmetic series has 15 terms. The first term is 8 and the sum is 645. Determine the last term and d.
645=\tfrac{15}{2}(8+t_{15})
86=8+t_{15}\Rightarrow t_{15}=\boldsymbol{78}
78=8+14d\Rightarrow d=\boldsymbol{5}

The can display

Example 7

A can display

Cans in a store display are stacked in rows. The bottom row has 30 cans and each row has 2 fewer cans than the row below it. The top row has 6 cans.

(a) How many rows are in the display?
t_1=30,\ d=-2,\ t_n=6
6=30+(n-1)(-2)\Rightarrow n-1=12
n=\boldsymbol{13} rows
(b) How many cans are in the display?
S_{13}=\tfrac{13}{2}(30+6)=13(18)=\boldsymbol{234} cans
Example 8

Training distances

A runner’s weekly training distances form an arithmetic sequence. She runs 18 km in week 4 and 39 km in week 11.

(a) Determine d and t_1.
From week 4 to week 11 is 7 steps:
7d=39-18=21\Rightarrow d=\boldsymbol{3} km
t_1=18-3(3)=\boldsymbol{9} km
(b) Determine her total distance over the first 16 weeks.
t_{16}=9+15(3)=54
S_{16}=\tfrac{16}{2}(9+54)=8(63)=\boldsymbol{504} km

Terms from a Sum Formula

Sometimes a formula for S_n is given instead of the terms. Each partial sum is the previous one plus one more term, so subtracting consecutive sums gives a term.

t_1=S_1
and
t_n=S_n-S_{n-1}\quad\text{for } n\ge2

This also works when the sums S_n come from a table or a graph of points (n,\ S_n).

Example 9

From S_n to the terms

An arithmetic series has S_n=3n^2+2n.

(a) Determine the first four terms of the sequence.
S_1=5,\ S_2=16,\ S_3=33,\ S_4=56
t_1=5,\ t_2=16-5=11,
t_3=33-16=17,\ t_4=56-33=23
The sequence is \boldsymbol{5,\ 11,\ 17,\ 23,\ \dots} (d=6)
(b) Determine t_{20}.
S_{20}=3(400)+40=1\,240
S_{19}=3(361)+38=1\,121
t_{20}=1\,240-1\,121=\boldsymbol{119}
(Check: 5+19(6)=119.)
Big partial sums. 3×20x²+2×20= gives S_{20}; change 20 to 19 for S_{19}.

Key ideas

Sequence vs series. A sequence lists terms with commas; a series adds them. S_n is the sum of the first n terms (a partial sum).
Pair it up. Forwards + backwards: n columns, each t_1+t_n. So S_n=\dfrac{n}{2}(t_1+t_n), or with d: S_n=\dfrac{n}{2}\left[2t_1+(n-1)d\right].
Work backwards. Substitute what you know. t_1 or d: a linear equation. n: a quadratic; factor it and keep only the natural-number root.
Term or total? “Last row” is t_n; “altogether” is S_n. From a sum formula: t_1=S_1 and t_n=S_n-S_{n-1}.
