Infinite Geometric Series

Adding forever and still getting a number. When the common ratio is small enough, the partial sums settle on one value: the sum to infinity S_\infty=\dfrac{\textcolor{#0f7c80}{t_1}}{1-\textcolor{#c27a0e}{r}}.

I can…
  • find partial sums and tell whether an infinite series is convergent or divergent
  • decide from r alone: the series converges only when -1<r<1
  • calculate the sum to infinity S_\infty=\dfrac{t_1}{1-r}, and work backwards to find t_1 or r
  • write a repeating decimal as a fraction using an infinite geometric series
  • find a total over all time, such as a bouncing ball’s total distance
  • find the values of x that make a series with a variable ratio converge

Warm-up

The Last Piece

Ava, Ben and Cy share a square chocolate bar. Each round, the piece that is left is cut into 4 equal pieces: each friend takes one, and the 4th piece is left for the next round.

1
After round 1, what fraction of the bar does Ava have? How much is left?
Ava has \tfrac14 of the bar, and \tfrac14 is left.
2
After round 2, how much does Ava have now?
The leftover \tfrac14 is cut in 4, so each new piece is \tfrac1{16}. Ava: \tfrac14+\tfrac1{16}=\tfrac5{16}. Left: \tfrac1{16}.
3
How much is left after round 3? After round 10?
Round 3: \tfrac1{64}. Each round leaves a quarter of the last leftover, so round 10 leaves \left(\tfrac14\right)^{10}: about one millionth of the bar.
!
The big one. They keep sharing forever. In the end, how much of the bar does Ava get? Guess first.
A \tfrac14
B \tfrac{5}{16}
C \tfrac13
D infinitely much
C, exactly \tfrac13. Every round is fair, so the three friends always have equal amounts, and the leftover shrinks to nothing. The whole bar ends up split three ways:
\tfrac14+\tfrac1{16}+\tfrac1{64}+\dots=\tfrac13
Infinitely many pieces, but a finite total. Today: how to find totals like this.

Challenge your neighbour: 4 friends share by cutting the leftover into 5 pieces each round. What does each one get?

1

Partial Sums: Convergent or Divergent

An infinite series has no last term: 6+2+\tfrac23+\dots We cannot add infinitely many terms one at a time, but we can add the first n terms. These sums S_1,\ S_2,\ S_3,\ \dots are the partial sums of the series.
Convergent
The partial sums get closer and closer to one fixed number. That number is the sum to infinity, written \textcolor{#12805a}{S_\infty}.
Divergent
The partial sums do not settle on one number (they grow without limit, or keep jumping). The series has no sum.

Fill the square

Shade half of a square, then half of what is left, then half of that… Watch the partial sums on the number line.

Example 1

Two series, two endings

Two infinite geometric series are given. Series A: 6+2+\tfrac23+\tfrac29+\dots Series B: 5+10+20+40+\dots

(a) State r for each series, then complete the table of partial sums (to 4 decimal places where needed).
Series A: r= \tfrac13  Series B: r= 2
n123456
S_n for Series A688.66678.88898.96308.9877
S_n for Series B5153575155315
A: r=\dfrac{2}{6}=\boldsymbol{\tfrac13}. B: r=\dfrac{10}{5}=\boldsymbol{2}. Each partial sum is the one before plus the next term: S_3=8+\tfrac23\approx 8.6667, S_4=S_3+\tfrac29\approx8.8889, …
(b) Plot the partial sums of Series A. What value do they approach?
They get closer and closer to 9 (but never pass it).
(c) Classify each series as convergent or divergent. Give S_\infty where it exists.
Series A is convergent: the terms shrink so fast that the partial sums level off at 9. \boldsymbol{S_\infty=9}.
Series B is divergent: the partial sums grow without limit. There is no S_\infty.

Settle down, or run away?

Plot the points (n,\ S_n). A convergent series levels off at a line; a divergent one runs off the grid or never settles.

The Sum to Infinity of a Geometric Series

Whether a geometric series converges depends on what happens to r^n as n gets large:

rr^5r^{20}r^{50}as n grows, r^n …
0.50.03130.0000\dots0.0000\dotsgets closer and closer to 0
-0.5-0.03130.0000\dots0.0000\dotsgets closer and closer to 0
1.57.59383\,325.26about 638 milliongrows without limit
-1.5-7.59383\,325.26about 638 milliongrows in size, sign keeps switching

Write the Lesson 7 formula as S_n=\dfrac{t_1(1-r^n)}{1-r} (multiply its top and bottom by -1). When -1<r<1, r^n shrinks to 0, so S_n gets closer and closer to \dfrac{t_1(1-0)}{1-r}.

Infinite geometric series
If -1<r<1, the series is convergent and
S_\infty=\dfrac{\textcolor{#0f7c80}{t_1}}{1-\textcolor{#c27a0e}{r}}
If r\le -1 or r\ge 1, the series is divergent and has no sum to infinity. (For r=1 the partial sums t_1,\ 2t_1,\ 3t_1,\ \dots grow; for r=-1 they jump between t_1 and 0.)
!
Check r first. The formula will happily give a number for r=2 (\tfrac{5}{1-2}=-5), but that number means nothing: the series diverges. Only use S_\infty once you know -1<r<1.

The gate: is r between −1 and 1?

Example 2

Convergent or divergent?

Determine r and decide whether each series is convergent or divergent. Calculate S_\infty where it exists.

(a) 45+15+5+\dots
r=\tfrac{15}{45}=\tfrac13: convergent.
S_\infty=\dfrac{45}{1-\frac13}=\dfrac{45}{\frac23}=\boldsymbol{67.5}
(b) 20-16+12.8-\dots
r=\tfrac{-16}{20}=-0.8: convergent.
S_\infty=\dfrac{20}{1-(-0.8)}=\dfrac{20}{1.8}\approx\boldsymbol{11.11}
(c) 2+5+12.5+\dots
r=\tfrac52=2.5\ge1: divergent. No sum to infinity.
(d) 4-4+4-4+\dots
r=-1: divergent. (Partial sums: 4,\ 0,\ 4,\ 0,\ \dots)
(e) \tfrac34+\tfrac12+\tfrac13+\dots
r=\tfrac12\div\tfrac34=\tfrac23: convergent.
S_\infty=\dfrac{\frac34}{\frac13}=\boldsymbol{\dfrac94}
(f) 0.006+0.06+0.6+\dots
r=10: divergent, even though the first terms are tiny.
Example 3

How close is close?

Consider the series 40+30+22.5+\dots

(a) Determine S_\infty.
r=0.75. S_\infty=\dfrac{40}{1-0.75}=\dfrac{40}{0.25}=\boldsymbol{160}
(b) Determine S_{10} to the nearest hundredth.
S_{10}=\dfrac{40(1-0.75^{10})}{1-0.75}=160(1-0.0563\dots)\approx\boldsymbol{150.99}
(c) Using a table of values, determine the smallest number of terms whose sum is within 1 of S_\infty.
S_n=160(1-0.75^n), so S_\infty-S_n=160(0.75)^n. Table of 160(0.75)^n: n=17 gives 1.20; n=18 gives 0.90.
The first 18 terms are needed (S_{18}\approx159.10).

3

Finding t_1 or r

Substitute what you know into S_\infty=\dfrac{t_1}{1-r} and solve. Any value of r you find must satisfy -1<r<1, or the series has no sum.
Example 4

Working backwards

(a) S_\infty=50 and r=0.3. Determine t_1.
50=\dfrac{t_1}{1-0.3}=\dfrac{t_1}{0.7}
t_1=50(0.7)=\boldsymbol{35}
(b) t_1=12 and S_\infty=8. Determine r.
8=\dfrac{12}{1-r}\ \Rightarrow\ 8(1-r)=12\ \Rightarrow\ 1-r=1.5
\boldsymbol{r=-0.5} (between -1 and 1, so valid).
(c) In an infinite geometric series, t_1=64 and t_3=16. Determine S_\infty for each possible series.
t_3=t_1r^2:\ 64r^2=16\ \Rightarrow\ r^2=\tfrac14\ \Rightarrow\ r=\pm\tfrac12
r=\tfrac12:\ S_\infty=\dfrac{64}{\frac12}=\boldsymbol{128}
r=-\tfrac12:\ S_\infty=\dfrac{64}{\frac32}=\boldsymbol{\dfrac{128}{3}}\approx42.67
(d) Is there an infinite geometric series with t_1=10 and S_\infty=4? Explain.
4=\dfrac{10}{1-r}\ \Rightarrow\ 1-r=2.5\ \Rightarrow\ r=-1.5
But r=-1.5 is not between -1 and 1, so the series would diverge. No such series exists.
4

Repeating Decimals

A repeating decimal is an infinite geometric series. Split it into its repeating blocks: each block is the one before it moved one block-length to the right, so r=0.1 for a 1-digit block, r=0.01 for a 2-digit block, and so on. Then S_\infty gives the decimal as a fraction. If some digits do not repeat, keep them separate and add them at the end.
Example 5

Decimals into fractions

Write each repeating decimal as an infinite geometric series, then as a fraction in lowest terms.

(a) 0.\overline{4}
0.4+0.04+0.004+\dots
t_1=0.4,\ r=0.1
S_\infty=\dfrac{0.4}{1-0.1}=\dfrac{0.4}{0.9}=\boldsymbol{\dfrac49}
(b) 0.\overline{63}
0.63+0.0063+0.000063+\dots
t_1=0.63,\ r=0.01
S_\infty=\dfrac{0.63}{0.99}=\dfrac{63}{99}=\boldsymbol{\dfrac{7}{11}}
(c) 1.2\overline{7}
1.2+(0.07+0.007+\dots)
t_1=0.07,\ r=0.1
S_\infty=\dfrac{0.07}{0.9}=\dfrac{7}{90}
1.2\overline{7}=\dfrac{108}{90}+\dfrac{7}{90}=\boldsymbol{\dfrac{23}{18}}
5

Applications

When a quantity keeps shrinking by the same percent (a bounce height, weekly sales, yearly output), the total over all time is S_\infty. In a real situation the process eventually stops, so S_\infty is the largest the total could ever be.
For a bouncing ball, the ball travels down and up. Add two series: the drops (starting with the original height) and the rises (starting with the first rebound height).
Example 6

The bouncing ball

A ball is dropped from a height of 9 m. Each time it bounces, it rises to 60% of the height it fell from. Assume it keeps bouncing forever.

(a) Write the series for the distances travelled down, and for the distances travelled up.
Down: 9+5.4+3.24+\dots (t_1=9,\ r=0.6)
Up: 5.4+3.24+1.944+\dots (t_1=5.4,\ r=0.6)
(b) Determine the total vertical distance the ball travels.
Down: S_\infty=\dfrac{9}{1-0.6}=22.5 m. Up: S_\infty=\dfrac{5.4}{1-0.6}=13.5 m.
Total: 22.5+13.5=\boldsymbol{36} m.
!
Don’t forget the trip up. The ball rises to 5.4 m and then falls 5.4 m again: every rebound height is travelled twice. Only the first 9 m is travelled once.
Example 7

Downloads that fade

A new phone app is downloaded 12 000 times in its first week. Each week after that, the number of downloads is 85% of the week before.

(a) How many downloads are there in week 5?
t_1=12\,000,\ r=0.85
t_5=12\,000(0.85)^4\approx\boldsymbol{6\,264} downloads
(b) How many downloads are there in the first 10 weeks, to the nearest whole number?
S_{10}=\dfrac{12\,000(1-0.85^{10})}{1-0.85}\approx\boldsymbol{64\,250} downloads
(c) If this pattern went on forever, what is the greatest total number of downloads the app could reach?
S_\infty=\dfrac{12\,000}{1-0.85}=\dfrac{12\,000}{0.15}=\boldsymbol{80\,000} downloads
(d) Why might the real total differ from (c)?
The 85% pattern is only a model: an update, an ad or the app being removed would change it. Also, downloads come in whole numbers, so the tiny late terms are really 0.
Check (a) and (b). 12000×.85^4ENTER for week 5 (the exponent is n-1=4), then the sum formula with brackets for S_{10}.
6

Series with a Variable Ratio

When the terms contain a variable, find r as an expression. The series converges only for the values of the variable that make -1<r<1: solve this double inequality by doing the same thing to all three parts.
Example 8

Which values of x work?

Consider the infinite geometric series 3+\dfrac{3x}{2}+\dfrac{3x^2}{4}+\dots

(a) Determine r and the values of x for which the series converges.
r=\dfrac{3x}{2}\div3=\dfrac{x}{2}
-1<\dfrac{x}{2}<1\ \Rightarrow\ \boldsymbol{-2<x<2}
(b) Write an expression for S_\infty and evaluate it when x=1.
S_\infty=\dfrac{3}{1-\frac{x}{2}}=\boldsymbol{\dfrac{6}{2-x}}
x=1:\ S_\infty=\dfrac61=\boldsymbol{6} (series 3+1.5+0.75+\dots)
(c) Determine the value of x for which S_\infty=4.
\dfrac{6}{2-x}=4\ \Rightarrow\ 2-x=1.5\ \Rightarrow\ \boldsymbol{x=0.5}. Check: -2<0.5<2, so the series converges (r=0.25).
✓

Key ideas

Partial sums tell the story. If S_1,\ S_2,\ S_3,\ \dots settle on one number, the series is convergent and that number is S_\infty. If they grow or keep jumping, it is divergent.
The gate is -1<r<1. Then r^n\to0 and S_\infty=\dfrac{t_1}{1-r}. For r\le-1 or r\ge1 there is no sum, whatever the formula says.
Work backwards by substituting into S_\infty=\dfrac{t_1}{1-r}. A repeating decimal is a series with r=0.1, 0.01, …: 0.\overline{63}=\tfrac{0.63}{0.99}=\tfrac7{11}.
Totals over all time. A bouncing ball goes down and up: add two series. With a variable ratio, solve -1<r<1 for x, doing the same thing to all three parts.
