Toothpick Houses

A row of houses is built from toothpicks. Neighbouring houses share a wall. Here are 3 houses.

1
How many toothpicks make the 3-house row?
13. The first house takes 5; each of the other two adds 4.
2
Each new house needs how many more toothpicks? Why not 5?
4 more: a floor, a wall and two roof picks. Its left wall is already there.
3
How many toothpicks for 10 houses?
41: 5 for the first house, then 9\times4=36 more.

The big one. A box holds exactly 100 toothpicks. Can you build one row of houses that uses all 100? Guess first.
A Yes, 20 houses
B Yes, 24 houses
C Yes, 25 houses

Sequences and Terms

A sequence is an ordered list of numbers. Each number is a term. We write \textcolor{#0e7c86}{t_1} for the first term, t_2 for the second term, and t_n for the term in position n (the general term). The position n is always a natural number: 1,\ 2,\ 3,\ \dots
5
t_1
9
t_2
13
t_3
17
t_4
…
position n goes in the little number; the term t_n is the value in that spot. Here t_3=13.

Two ways to describe a sequence

A rule from term to term
“start at 5 and add 4”
Gets the next term from the previous one: 5,\ 9,\ 13,\ 17,\ \dots
A formula for the general term
t_n=4n+1
Gives any term straight from its position: t_{3}=4(3)+1=13.

The sequence machine

Feed in a position n; the formula turns it into the term t_n. Each term drops into the row and onto a graph as the point (n,\ t_n).

Rule or formula: which is faster?

2

Arithmetic, Geometric, or Neither

Arithmetic
Each term is the previous term plus a constant, the common difference d.
5,\ 9,\ 13,\ 17,\ \dots has \textcolor{#c27a0e}{d=4}
Geometric
Each term is the previous term times a constant, the common ratio r.
3,\ 6,\ 12,\ 24,\ \dots has \textcolor{#c27a0e}{r=2}
Neither
Many sequences are neither: the square numbers 1,\ 4,\ 9,\ 16,\ \dots, the primes 2,\ 3,\ 5,\ 7,\ 11,\ \dots, and the Fibonacci sequence 1,\ 1,\ 2,\ 3,\ 5,\ 8,\ \dots (each term is the sum of the two before it).
To test a sequence, subtract each term from the next (are the differences all equal?) and divide each term by the one before it (are the ratios all equal?).

The hop test: subtract, then divide

!
Order matters. Always do next − previous and next ÷ previous. A falling sequence like 40,\ 33,\ 26,\ \dots has a negative difference, d=33-40=-7, not 7.
Example 2

Arithmetic, geometric or neither?

Classify each sequence as arithmetic, geometric or neither. Give d or r when there is one.

(a) 40,\ 33,\ 26,\ 19,\ \dots
Differences: -7,\ -7,\ -7.
Arithmetic, d=\boldsymbol{-7}.
(b) 2,\ -6,\ 18,\ -54,\ \dots
Ratios: \tfrac{-6}{2}=-3, \tfrac{18}{-6}=-3, \tfrac{-54}{18}=-3.
Geometric, r=\boldsymbol{-3}.
(c) 1,\ 3,\ 6,\ 10,\ 15,\ \dots
Differences 2,\ 3,\ 4,\ 5 (not equal); ratios 3,\ 2,\ \dots (not equal).
Neither.
(d) 64,\ 16,\ 4,\ 1,\ \dots
Ratios: all \tfrac{1}{4}.
Geometric, r=\boldsymbol{\tfrac{1}{4}}.
(e) \tfrac{1}{2},\ \tfrac{5}{4},\ 2,\ \tfrac{11}{4},\ \dots
Differences: all \tfrac{3}{4}.
Arithmetic, d=\boldsymbol{\tfrac{3}{4}}.
(f) 7,\ 7,\ 7,\ 7,\ \dots
Both: d=\boldsymbol{0} and r=\boldsymbol{1}. (A constant sequence is arithmetic and geometric.)

Patterns that are neither (but still patterns)

“Neither” doesn’t mean “no pattern”. These grow by a rule, just not by adding or multiplying the same number every time.

Finite and Infinite Sequences

Finite
4,\ 7,\ 10,\ \dots,\ 31
A finite sequence has a last term. The dots in the middle stand for the terms we skipped writing.
Infinite
4,\ 7,\ 10,\ \dots
An infinite sequence goes on forever: three dots at the end and no last term.

Using a Formula for the General Term

When a formula for t_n is given, substitute the position n to get the term. To decide whether a number is a term, set the formula equal to it and solve for n: the number is a term only if n is a natural number (1,\ 2,\ 3,\ \dots).
Find a term: substitute
n\ \to\ t_n
Put the position in for n and work it out.
Is it a term? Solve
t_n\ \to\ n
Set t_n equal to the number, solve for n, then ask: is n a counting number?

Example 3

Using a general term

A sequence has general term t_n=5n-3.

(a) Write the first four terms.
t_1=5(1)-3=2, t_2=7, t_3=12, t_4=17.
The sequence is \boldsymbol{2,\ 7,\ 12,\ 17,\ \dots}
(b) Determine t_{30}.
t_{30}=5(30)-3=\boldsymbol{147}
(c) Is 92 a term of the sequence? If so, which one?
5n-3=92\ \Rightarrow\ 5n=95\ \Rightarrow\ n=19.
Yes: 92=\boldsymbol{t_{19}}.
(d) Is 100 a term of the sequence?
5n-3=100\ \Rightarrow\ 5n=103\ \Rightarrow\ n=20.6.
Not a natural number, so 100 is not a term.
Check on the TI-84. (b) 5×30−3ENTER and (d) (100+3)÷5ENTER (undo the -3, then the \times5).
Example 4

A general term with an exponent

The general term of a sequence is t_n=3(2)^{n-1}. Write the first five terms and classify the sequence.

First five terms, and the classification
t_1=3(2)^0=3, t_2=3(2)^1=6, t_3=12, t_4=24, t_5=48.
Each term is the previous term times 2, so the sequence is geometric with r=\boldsymbol{2}.
!
The exponent belongs to the 2 only. Do the power first, then multiply by 3: t_5=3(2)^4=3(16)=48. Multiplying first, 6^4=1296, is wrong.
t_5 on the TI-84. The calculator does the power first, as it should: 3×2^4ENTER shows 48. (For t_n the power is n-1, so t_5 uses 4.)
5

A Sequence Is a Function

A sequence is a function whose domain is the natural numbers (the positions n) and whose range is the set of terms. Its graph is a set of separate points (n,\ t_n): they are not joined, because there is no term at n=1.5.
Arithmetic → linear
It adds the same amount each step, so its points lie on a line: a linear function.
Geometric → non-linear
With r\ne1 it multiplies each step, so its points curve: a non-linear (exponential) function.
Think The constant sequence 7,\ 7,\ 7,\ 7,\ \dots from Example 2(f) is geometric with r=1. Are its points linear or non-linear?
Linear: the points (1,7),\ (2,7),\ (3,7),\ \dots lie on a horizontal line. It is also arithmetic with d=0, which is why the rule says r\ne1.
Example 5

Linear or non-linear?

Plot the first five terms of each sequence. State whether each is linear or non-linear.

(a) 2,\ 5,\ 8,\ 11,\ 14
The points lie on a line: linear (arithmetic, d=3).
(b) 1,\ 2,\ 4,\ 8,\ 16
The points curve upward: non-linear (geometric, r=2).
✓

Key ideas

Terms and positions. t_n is the term in position n; n is always 1,\ 2,\ 3,\ \dots A formula for t_n gives any term directly.
Subtract, then divide. Equal differences: arithmetic (d). Equal ratios: geometric (r). Neither equal: neither. A constant sequence is both.
Is it a term? Set t_n equal to the number and solve. It is a term only if n is a natural number. A finite sequence has a last term; an infinite one doesn’t.
A sequence is a function. Graph separate points (n,\ t_n), never joined. Arithmetic points lie on a line (linear); geometric points curve (non-linear).
