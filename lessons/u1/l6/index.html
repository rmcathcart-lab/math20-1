The Sneaky Sale

A pair of sneakers costs $100.

The store raises the price by 50%. A month later, the new price goes on sale for 50% off.

What do the sneakers cost now?

?
Work it out, then convince the person beside you.
$75, not $100! Up 50%: the price becomes $150. Then 50% off takes half of $150, which is $75 off, not $50. The second percent is taken from a bigger number. (Sale first, then the raise? $50, then $75 again.)

Growth and Decay Factors

When a quantity changes by the same percent every period (every year, hour, bounce, …), each value is the previous value times the same number. The values form a geometric sequence, and the common ratio \textcolor{#c27a0e}{r} is called the growth factor. When 0<r<1 the quantity shrinks, and we call it geometric decay (r is then also called the decay factor).
Increase by p\% each period
r=1+\dfrac{p}{100}
up 6%: r=1.06
Decrease by p\% each period
r=1-\dfrac{p}{100}
down 6%: r=0.94
Other words
Doubles: r=2 · Triples: r=3 · Halves: r=\tfrac{1}{2}
Keeps a fraction f of its value (“rebounds to \tfrac{3}{5} of its height”): r=f
!
Read carefully. “Loses \tfrac{1}{4} of its value” means it keeps \tfrac{3}{4}, so r=\tfrac{3}{4}. “Rebounds to 80%” means r=0.8, but “loses 80% of its height” means r=0.2.
Example 1

State the growth factor

State the growth factor r in each situation.

(a) A herd of elk grows by 7% each year.
r=1+0.07=\boldsymbol{1.07}
(b) A laptop loses 28% of its value each year.
r=1-0.28=\boldsymbol{0.72}
(c) The views of a video triple every day.
r=\boldsymbol{3}
(d) A swing’s arc gets shorter by one-eighth with each swing.
It keeps \tfrac{7}{8}: r=\boldsymbol{\tfrac{7}{8}=0.875}
(e) A ball rebounds to 65% of its previous height.
r=\boldsymbol{0.65}
(f) Wages rise by 2.75% per year.
r=1+0.0275=\boldsymbol{1.0275}

Building a Growth or Decay Sequence

Multiply by \textcolor{#c27a0e}{r} to get each next value. Keep the unrounded values in your calculator and round only the answers you write down. Once you know \textcolor{#a33357}{t_1} and \textcolor{#c27a0e}{r}, any term comes from t_n=\textcolor{#a33357}{t_1}\textcolor{#c27a0e}{r}^{\,n-1}.

A community garden program had 450 members when it started in January 2022. Its membership grows by 12% each year.

Multiply by 1.12 each year: 450\to504\to564.48\to632.2176, so 504, 564, 632.
(b) Show that the sequence is geometric and state r.
\dfrac{504}{450}=1.12,\quad\dfrac{564.48}{504}=1.12,\ \dots
Same ratio each year: geometric, \boldsymbol{r=1.12}.
(c) If t_1 is the membership in January 2022, which term gives the membership in January 2032?
2022 is t_1, 2023 is t_2, …, so 2032 (10 years later) is \boldsymbol{t_{11}}.
(d) Estimate the membership in January 2032.
t_{11}=450(1.12)^{10}\approx1\,397.6
About 1 398 members.

Where Does the Sequence Start?

Problems often ask for the value “after n years” or “after the nth bounce”. There are two correct ways to set up the sequence; the danger is mixing them up. Let \textcolor{#1f5bd6}{P} be the starting amount (at time 0).

Start at time 0
\textcolor{#a33357}{t_1}=P. Then t_2 is the value after 1 period, so the value after n periods is \boldsymbol{t_{n+1}}.
Start after one period
\textcolor{#a33357}{t_1}=Pr. Then the value after n periods is \boldsymbol{t_n}.
Both give the same result:   value after n periods =\textcolor{#1f5bd6}{P}\textcolor{#c27a0e}{r}^{\,n}   (P = starting amount at time 0, r = growth factor)
Example 3

The bouncing ball, two ways

A ball is dropped from a height of 16 m. Each bounce reaches 75% of the height of the bounce before. Determine the height of the 7th bounce, to the nearest centimetre, in two ways.

(a) Let t_1=16, the height it is dropped from. Which term is the height of the 7th bounce? Evaluate it.
t_1=16 is the drop (no bounces yet), t_2 is the 1st bounce, …, so the 7th bounce is t_8.
t_8=16(0.75)^7\approx2.1357
The 7th bounce is about 2.14 m high.
(b) Let t_1 be the height of the first bounce. State t_1, then evaluate the term that gives the 7th bounce.
t_1=16(0.75)=12 m. Now the 7th bounce is t_7.
t_7=12(0.75)^6\approx2.1357
About 2.14 m, the same answer.
(c) Write a formula for the height h of the ball after x bounces.
\boldsymbol{h=16(0.75)^x}   (P=16 at time 0, multiplied by 0.75 once per bounce)
Same number both ways. 16×.75^7ENTER and 12×.75^6ENTER both give 2.1357…

Doubling, Half-Life and Other Periods

The change does not have to happen once a year. If a quantity doubles every 20 minutes, the period is 20 minutes, and the number of periods is the total time divided by 20 minutes. The half-life of a radioactive substance is the time it takes for half of it to decay, so r=\tfrac{1}{2} for each half-life.
Number of periods n=\dfrac{\text{total time}}{\text{length of one period}},    amount =Pr^{\,n}
Example 4

Doubling and half-life

(a) A culture starts with 300 bacteria and the number doubles every 20 minutes. How many bacteria are there after 4 hours?
4 h =240 min, so n=\dfrac{240}{20}=12 doublings.
300(2)^{12}=300(4096)=\boldsymbol{1\,228\,800} bacteria.
(b) A hospital receives 96 mg of a medical isotope whose half-life is 6 hours. How much is left after 2 days?
2 days =48 h, so n=\dfrac{48}{6}=8 half-lives.
96\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{8}=\dfrac{96}{256}=\boldsymbol{0.375} mg.
Half is 0.5. 300×2^12ENTER and 96×.5^8ENTER.
5

Compound Interest

When interest is compounded annually at a rate i (as a decimal), the interest each year is added to the balance, and the next year’s interest is paid on that new balance. Each year the value is multiplied by 1+i, so the values form a geometric sequence with \textcolor{#c27a0e}{r=1+i}.
Compound interest:   A=P(1+i)^n
P = principal (amount invested) · i = interest rate per period (decimal) · n = number of periods · A = final amount

If interest is compounded more often, use the rate per period and count periods: 6% per year compounded monthly gives i=\tfrac{0.06}{12}=0.005, and 3 years is n=36 months.

Example 5

Lena’s GIC

Lena invests $6 500 in a GIC that pays 3.8% per year, compounded annually.

(a) State the growth factor.
r=1+0.038=\boldsymbol{1.038}
(b) Write a product (do not evaluate) for the value after 1 year and after 2 years.
After 1 year: 6\,500(1.038).
After 2 years: 6\,500(1.038)(1.038)=6\,500(1.038)^2.
(c) Let t_1 be the value after 1 year. Write t_n and show it equals 6\,500(1.038)^n.
t_n=t_1r^{n-1}=6\,500(1.038)(1.038)^{n-1}
=6\,500(1.038)^{1+n-1}=6\,500(1.038)^n
(d) Determine the value after 9 years, to the nearest cent.
t_9=6\,500(1.038)^9\approx9\,092.63
The GIC is worth $9 092.63.
Money: round at the end. 6500×1.038^9ENTER gives 9 092.6304…, so $9 092.63.

Working backwards: finding the rate

When the rate is the unknown, divide to get the growth factor’s power on its own, then undo the power with a root: the nth root undoes the nth power.

Example 6

Find the interest rate

An investment of $2 500 grows to $3 400 in 7 years with interest compounded annually. Determine the annual interest rate, to the nearest tenth of a percent.

2\,500(1+i)^7=3\,400\ \Rightarrow\ (1+i)^7=\dfrac{3\,400}{2\,500}=1.36
Take the 7th root: 1+i=\sqrt[7]{1.36}\approx1.0449
i\approx0.0449: the rate is about 4.5% per year.
The 7th root. Type the index first: 72ndˣ√1.36ENTER. Or use the power \tfrac{1}{7}: 1.36^(1÷7)ENTER. Both give 1.0449…

Finding the Number of Periods with a Table

To find when a growing or decaying quantity passes a target, we need the exponent n. Logarithms are not part of this course, so use a table of values: evaluate Pr^{\,n} on your calculator for n=1,2,3,\dots (the TI-84’s table, 2nd TABLE, does this for you). Find the first whole number of periods that passes the target, and check the value just before it.
Build the table on the TI-84. Y= Y_1=500\times0.7^X (the X,T,θ,n key types X, the number of passes), 2ndTBLSET TblStart = 0, ΔTbl = 1, then 2ndTABLE. Scroll with ▼ to the first row below the target, and check the row just before it. Without the table: 500ENTER, then ×.7ENTER and ENTER again and again (each ENTER is one more period: the calculator writes Ans*.7 for you), or one row at a time: 500×.7^8ENTER.
Example 7

Filtering the water

A quantity of water holds 500 mg of a contaminant. Each pass through a filter removes 30% of the contaminant that is left. How many passes are needed to bring it below 40 mg?

Each pass keeps 70%, so r=0.7. After n passes: 500(0.7)^n.
Table: n=6: 58.82 mg;   n=7: 41.18 mg;   n=8: 28.82 mg.
After 7 passes it is still above 40 mg; 8 passes are needed.

When the rate changes after the first year

Some things lose a big chunk in the first year, then less each year after. Work out the first year on its own, then start the sequence after year 1.

Key ideas

Growth factor. Up p\%: r=1+\tfrac{p}{100}. Down p\%: r=1-\tfrac{p}{100}. Doubles: 2, halves: \tfrac{1}{2}. “Loses \tfrac14” keeps \tfrac34. r>1 grows, 0<r<1 decays.
Where it starts. t_1=P (time 0): after n periods is t_{n+1}. t_1=Pr (after 1 period): after n periods is t_n. Either way the value is Pr^{\,n}.
Count the periods. n=\tfrac{\text{total time}}{\text{one period}} (same units!). Half-life: r=\tfrac12. Compound interest: A=P(1+i)^n, with i and n per period. Find a rate with the nth root.
When does it pass a target? Try values of n (a table). The answer is the first whole n past the target; check the one just before it.
