The Big Throw

You throw a ball straight up as hard as you can.

It comes back down into your hand 4 seconds later.

Estimate: how high did the ball go?

?
Higher than our school? Write down a number of metres.
About 20 m: as high as a six-storey building! Most people guess far lower.
The ball takes 2 s up and 2 s down (the same time each way). Falling from the top for 2 s, it drops about 4.9\times2^2\approx19.6 m.
And 8 seconds in the air? Not twice as high: about four times as high (78 m). The height grows with the square of the time. Galileo worked that out around 1604.

Maximum and Minimum Problems: Graphical Approach

A quadratic model describes a real situation: a ball in the air, a business's revenue, the area of a shape. Every question about the situation is a feature of the graph: the vertex gives the greatest or least value, the y-intercept the start, a zero the landing, and a line y = k the times it reaches a height. A graphing calculator finds them all.

Reading a Quadratic Model

A quadratic model is a quadratic function that describes a real situation, such as the height of a ball, the revenue of a business or the area of a shape. Models are often written in function notation: in h(t)=-5t^2+10t+15, the input is the time t (seconds) and the output h(t) is the height (metres), so h(2) means “the height after 2 seconds”. Each question about the situation matches a feature of the graph.
Key idea
The question asks for…Use this feature of the graph
the greatest or least value (maximum height, maximum revenue, minimum cost)the y-coordinate of the vertex
when or where the maximum or minimum happensthe x-coordinate of the vertex
the starting value (at launch, at time 0)the y-intercept
when an object lands or hits the groundthe positive zero (x-intercept)
when the output equals a given value kthe intersection with the line y=k
the output for a given inputsubstitute (evaluate)

In a context, the domain and range are only the values that make sense. A ball's model is used from launch (t=0) to landing, so the domain stops at the landing time and the range runs from 0 up to the maximum height (not from the launch height: the ball falls past it to the ground).

Example 1

A ball thrown from a building

A ball is thrown from the top of a building. Its height in metres after t seconds is h(t)=-5t^2+10t+15, graphed at the right. Use the graph to answer.

(a) From what height was the ball thrown?
The y-intercept: 15 m.
(b) What is the maximum height, and when is it reached?
The vertex is (1,\ 20): a maximum height of 20 m after 1 s.
(c) When does the ball hit the ground?
The positive zero: after 3 s.
(d) At what other time is the ball at its launch height?
By symmetry about the axis t=1: at t=2 s
(h(2)=-20+20+15=15).
(e) State the domain and range of the model in this context.
Domain \{t\mid0\le t\le3,\ t\in\mathbb{R}\}; range \{h\mid0\le h\le20,\ h\in\mathbb{R}\}.

Using a Graphing Calculator

When the vertex or the intercepts are not on grid lines, a graphing calculator finds them to as many decimal places as you need.

TI-84 Plus CE (the Casio has the same tools under G-Solv)
1Press Y= and enter the model with X as the input variable (for h(t)=-4.9t^2+32t+2.5, enter -4.9X^2+32X+2.5).
2Set the WINDOW from the context: start X and Y at 0, and make Xmax and Ymax a little larger than the times and values you expect. Press GRAPH.
3Press 2ndTRACE (CALC) and choose 1:value (the output for an input), 2:zero, 3:minimum, 4:maximum or 5:intersect (after entering Y_2=k for a given value). For zero, minimum and maximum, move to a left bound and a right bound on either side of the point, press ENTER for each, then ENTER again for the guess.
4Round only the final answer, as the question asks. 2ndGRAPH (TABLE) lists outputs for whole-number inputs.

Always sketch the graph you see, with the axes labelled (name and units) and the important points marked.

On your own calculator. The maximum: bounds at X=2 and X=5, guess 3. The times at 30 m: Y_2=30, then 5:intersect twice, with a guess near each crossing (1, then 5).
Example 2

A model rocket

A model rocket is launched from a platform. Its height in metres after t seconds is h(t)=-4.9t^2+32t+2.5. Use a graphing calculator, and answer to the nearest hundredth.

(a) Sketch the part of the graph that fits the context.
(b) How high is the rocket after 2 s?
2nd [CALC], 1:value, X=2: h(2)=46.90 m.
(c) What is its maximum height, and when is it reached?
2nd [CALC], 4:maximum: (3.27,\ 54.74).
A maximum height of 54.74 m after 3.27 s.
(d) How high is the platform?
h(0)=2.5: the y-intercept, 2.50 m.
(e) How long is the rocket in the air?
2nd [CALC], 2:zero, with bounds on either side of the positive t-intercept: t\approx6.61 s.
(f) When is the rocket 30 m above the ground?
Enter Y_2=30, then 2nd [CALC], 5:intersect at each crossing: t\approx1.02 s (going up) and t\approx5.51 s (coming down).
(g) State the domain and range of the function in this context.
Domain \{t\mid0\le t\le6.61,\ t\in\mathbb{R}\}; range \{h\mid0\le h\le54.74,\ h\in\mathbb{R}\}.
Example 3

A power line: a minimum

A power line hangs between two poles 120 m apart. Its height in metres above the ground, x metres from the left pole, is h(x)=0.004x^2-0.48x+25, 0\le x\le120.

(a) How high is the line where it is attached to each pole?
h(0)=25 m at the left pole.
h(120)=0.004(14\,400)-57.6+25=25 m at the right pole.
(b) What is the lowest height of the line, and how far from the left pole is it?
The parabola opens up (a>0), so the vertex is a minimum.
2nd [CALC], 3:minimum: (60,\ 10.6).
The lowest point is 10.6 m high, 60 m from the left pole.
(c) At what distances from the left pole is the line 15 m high? Answer to the nearest tenth of a metre.
Y_2=15, intersect twice:
x\approx26.8 m and x\approx93.2 m.
(d) State the range of the function in this context.
\{h\mid10.6\le h\le25,\ h\in\mathbb{R}\}

Revenue Models

Revenue is the money taken in: \text{revenue}=(\text{price per item})\times(\text{number of items sold}). When a business raises its price, it usually sells fewer items, so the revenue can go up or down. If x is the number of price changes, both the price and the number sold are linear expressions in x, and their product is a quadratic.
Key idea
Let x be the number of price changes.
Price =(\text{current price})\pm(\text{change})\,x,   Number sold =(\text{current number})\mp(\text{change in sales})\,x.
R(x)=(\text{price})(\text{number sold}), and the vertex of its graph gives the maximum revenue.

If the price can only change in whole steps (“each $2 increase”), x must be a whole number. When the vertex is not at a whole number, compare the revenue at the whole numbers on either side of it (the TABLE does this quickly).

Example 4

Boat-tour tickets

A tour company sells 600 boat-tour tickets a week at $45 each. A survey shows that for every $2 increase in the price, 15 fewer tickets would be sold. The price changes only in steps of $2.

(a) Let x be the number of $2 increases. Write expressions for the price and the number of tickets sold, and the revenue function R(x).
Price: 45+2x dollars
Tickets: 600-15x
R(x)=(45+2x)(600-15x)
(b) Graph R(x) and find its maximum. Why is that not the final answer?
Window 0\le X\le40, 0\le Y\le35\,000. 4:maximum: (8.75,\ 29\,296.88).
x counts whole $2 steps, so x=8.75 is not allowed.
(c) Use the table to choose the best whole number of increases. State the best price, the number of tickets sold, and the maximum weekly revenue.
TABLE: R(8)=(61)(480)=\$29\,280;   R(9)=(63)(465)=\$29\,295.
Best: 9 increases. Price 45+18=\$63, 465 tickets, revenue $29 295.
(d) How much more is this than the current weekly revenue?
Now: R(0)=45(600)=\$27\,000.
29\,295-27\,000=\$2295 more per week.
The TABLE for (c). Enter Y_1=(45+2X)(600-15X), set 2ndWINDOW (TBLSET) to start at 0 with steps of 1, then 2ndGRAPH (TABLE) and scroll down to X=8 and X=9.

Models from Data and from Geometry

Many quadratic models come from measured data: a calculator draws the “curve of best fit” (quadratic regression, a later course) and the model is then used like any other. A model only describes the situation over the inputs it was built from; it can be poor far outside them. Area problems often lead to a quadratic in one length, and then the domain is the set of lengths that make every dimension positive.
Example 5

Fuel use and speed

A driver records the fuel her car uses at different steady speeds.

Speed, v (km/h)406080100120
Fuel use, E (L per 100 km)6.35.04.75.67.1

The data are modelled by E(v)=0.0012v^2-0.18v+11.5 for 40\le v\le130.

The data (dots) and the model (curve)
(a) At what speed does the car use the least fuel, and how much does it use?
The parabola opens up; 3:minimum gives (75,\ 4.75).
The least fuel use is 4.75 L per 100 km, at 75 km/h.
(b) Compare the fuel use at 50 km/h and at 110 km/h.
E(50)=5.5 L per 100 km; E(110)\approx6.22 L per 100 km.
The car uses more fuel at 110 km/h.
(c) At what speeds does the car use 6 L per 100 km? Answer to the nearest km/h.
Y_2=6, intersect: v\approx43 km/h and v\approx107 km/h.

Bending a rain gutter

A strip of metal 30 cm wide is bent up x cm on each side. Bend too little and the gutter is shallow; bend too much and the base disappears. The area of the cross-section changes with x, and its graph is a parabola.

Example 6

The rain gutter

A rain gutter is made from a strip of metal 30 cm wide by bending up x cm on each side, as shown. The area of the cross-section, in square centimetres, is A(x)=x(30-2x).

(a) State the domain of A(x) in this context.
Both x and the base 30-2x must be positive: \{x\mid0<x<15,\ x\in\mathbb{R}\}.
x x 30-2x
(b) What value of x gives the greatest cross-section area? What is that area?
Window 0\le X\le15, 0\le Y\le130. 4:maximum: (7.5,\ 112.5).
Bend up 7.5 cm on each side; the area is 112.5\text{ cm}^2 (base 15 cm).
(c) For which values of x is the area 100\text{ cm}^2?
Y_2=100, intersect: x=5 or x=10.
Check: 5(20)=100 and 10(10)=100.

Key ideas

Question → feature. Greatest or least value: the vertex (y is the value, x is when or where). Start: the y-intercept. Landing: the positive zero. Output equals k: intersect with y=k.
The calculator. Y=, a WINDOW from the context, GRAPH, then 2ndTRACE: 1:value, 2:zero, 3:minimum, 4:maximum, 5:intersect. Sketch what you see, and round only at the end.
Domain and range in context. Only the values that make sense: a ball's domain stops at landing, and its range runs from 0 (the ground) up to the maximum height.
Revenue. R(x)=(\text{price})(\text{number sold}), with x the number of price changes. Whole steps only: if the vertex isn't at a whole number, compare the whole numbers on either side in the TABLE.
