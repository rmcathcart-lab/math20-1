The Matchstick Grids

One square takes 4 matchsticks. A 2 × 2 grid of squares takes 12, and a 3 × 3 grid takes 24.

How many matchsticks do you need to build a 10 × 10 grid?

?
Make a guess first. Then look for a quick way to count.
220 matchsticks, not 4\times100=400: squares side by side share their matchsticks.
Count the lines instead: a 10 × 10 grid has 11 rows of 10 matchsticks going across (110) and 11 columns of 10 going down (110). 110+110=220.
The same count for an n\times n grid gives 2n(n+1): 4, 12, 24, 40, 60, … Each grid needs 4 more extra matchsticks than the jump before (+8, +12, +16, +20, …).

Quadratic Functions and Their Graphs

A quadratic function can be written as y=ax^2+bx+c (or f(x)=ax^2+bx+c), where a, b and c are real numbers and a\ne0. This is its general form. Its graph is a U-shaped curve called a parabola. In function notation, f(x) is the y-value for a given x: f(3) is the value of y when x=3.
  • The axis of symmetry is the vertical line that splits the parabola into two mirror-image halves. Its equation is x= a number. Because of the symmetry, it is halfway between the two x-intercepts.
  • The vertex is the point where the axis of symmetry meets the parabola: the turning point.
  • If a>0 the parabola opens up; the vertex is its lowest point and its y-coordinate is the minimum value of the function. If a<0 it opens down; the vertex is its highest point and its y-coordinate is the maximum value.
  • The y-intercept is where the graph crosses the y-axis (x=0). For y=ax^2+bx+c it is c. The x-intercepts are where the graph meets the x-axis (y=0).

Roots, Zeros and x-Intercepts

Three words describe the same numbers from three points of view.

Rootof an equationA root (or solution) of an equation is a value of x that makes the equation true.
Zeroof a functionA zero of a function f is a value of x that makes f(x)=0.
x-interceptof a graphAn x-intercept of a graph is where the graph meets the x-axis. The point is (r,\ 0); the number r is also called the x-intercept.
Key idea
For a function y=f(x):  the zeros of f = the x-intercepts of the graph of y=f(x) = the roots of the equation f(x)=0.
Each factor (x-r) of f(x) gives the zero r:  f(x)=(x-3)(x+5) has zeros 3 and -5.
Example 2

One number, three names

(a) For the linear function f(x)=3x-12, determine the root of 3x-12=0, the zero of f and the x-intercept of its graph.
3x-12=0\Rightarrow x=4.  Root: 4.  Zero: 4 (since f(4)=0).  x-intercept: 4, the point (4,\ 0). All the same number.
(b) The graph of f(x)=x^2+2x-8 is shown. Factor x^2+2x-8, then fill in the blanks.
The function f has zeros -4, 2.
The equation x^2+2x-8=0 has roots -4, 2.
The graph has x-intercepts -4, 2 and y-intercept -8.
x^2+2x-8=(x+4)(x-2)
(c) How do the factors of x^2+2x-8 show the zeros?
The factor (x+4) is 0 when x=-4 and (x-2) is 0 when x=2: each factor gives one zero, by the zero product property.
Math HistoryBaghdad · about 820 CE

Why is a solution called a “root”?

Around 820 CE, at the House of Wisdom in Baghdad, al-Khwarizmi wrote the book that gave us the word algebra, entirely in words. He called the unknown number jidhr, Arabic for the root of a plant; its square was māl, “wealth”.

In 1145 Robert of Chester translated the book into Latin and wrote radix, Latin for “root” (the same word gives us radical and radish). By 1728 an English encyclopedia said it plainly: “The Root of an Equation, is the Value of the unknown Quantity in the Equation.”

One of al-Khwarizmi's examples, “a square equals forty roots minus four squares”, becomes 5x^2=40x. Find its roots with today's method.
5x^2-40x=0, so 5x(x-8)=0: x=0 or x=8. (Dividing both sides by x would lose the root 0: set the equation equal to 0 and factor.)

Finding Zeros and Roots by Factoring

To find the zeros of f algebraically
1Set f(x)=0 and move every term to one side.
2Factor fully (a common factor first).
3Set each factor that contains x equal to 0.
4Solve each one.
To find the y-intercept: substitute x=0, i.e. calculate f(0).

The same method works for a product of more than two factors, such as x(x-1)(x+6): each factor with an x gives a zero. (Except for a linear equation, never factor before the other side is 0.)

How Many Real Roots?

A parabola can cross the x-axis twice, touch it once at its vertex, or miss it completely.

Key idea
Two x-interceptstwo distinct real roots (two zeros)
One x-intercept(the vertex is on the x-axis): one real root, a double root
No x-interceptsno real roots (no zeros)

You can often tell from a sketch: a parabola that opens up with its vertex above the x-axis (or opens down with its vertex below it) never meets the axis.

y=x^2-4x+4

y=x^2-4x+6

(b) Without graphing, state how many x-intercepts each parabola has.

(ii) It opens up; its vertex is (-1,\ -4).

Finding Zeros with a Graphing Calculator

Zeros on a TI-84
Enter the function in Y=, then GRAPH (adjust the WINDOW until every x-intercept shows). Press 2ndTRACE (CALC) 2:zero; move to the left of one x-intercept and press ENTER (left bound), move to its right and press ENTER (right bound), then ENTER again (guess). Repeat for each x-intercept. (Casio: G-SOLV, ROOT.)

A decimal such as 1.3333\dots is probably the fraction \frac{4}{3} (on a TI-84, MATH1 ►Frac converts it). A zero r gives a factor (x-r), and a zero \frac{m}{n} gives a factor (nx-m); expand to check that the leading coefficient matches. When the zeros are not rational, the calculator gives decimals: round as the question asks.

Example 6(a) on the calculator. Y_1=6X^2+7X-20, ZOOM6, then 2ndTRACE2 around each intercept: left bound -4, right bound -1, guess -2; then 1, 2, guess 1.5.
Roots of an equation, another way: intersect. For x^2-5x=14 (Example 3(a)), graph each side, Y_1=X^2-5X and Y_2=14, and use 2ndTRACE 5:intersect (ENTERENTER, then a guess near each crossing). The x-coordinates of the crossings are the roots, 7 and -2. (Casio: G-SOLV, INTSECT.)

Writing a Quadratic Function from Its Zeros

If a quadratic function has zeros r and s, it has factors (x-r) and (x-s). Every function y=a(x-r)(x-s), a\ne0, has these zeros: changing a stretches or flips the parabola but keeps its x-intercepts. One more point on the graph fixes a.

Factored form
y=a(x-r)(x-s) has zeros r and s.  To find a, substitute the coordinates of another point on the graph and solve.
A double zero r (the graph touches the x-axis at r) gives y=a(x-r)^2.

Example 8

A signal flare

A signal flare is fired upward from the top of a cliff. Its height above the sea after t seconds is h(t)=-5t^2+25t+30 metres.

(a) Determine the zeros of h. Which zero answers “when does the flare reach the sea?”
-5t^2+25t+30=0
-5(t^2-5t-6)=0
-5(t-6)(t+1)=0
Zeros: 6 and -1. Reject t=-1 (before the flare was fired). It reaches the sea after 6 s.
(b) Find h(0) and explain what it means.
h(0)=30: the y-intercept. The flare is fired from 30 m above the sea (the cliff is 30 m high).

Key ideas

The parabola. y=ax^2+bx+c, a\ne0. a>0: opens up, the vertex gives the minimum; a<0: opens down, the maximum. The axis of symmetry is halfway between the x-intercepts; the y-intercept is c.
Three names, one number. The zeros of f = the x-intercepts of its graph = the roots of f(x)=0. To find them: set f(x)=0, factor fully, set each factor with x equal to 0. The y-intercept is f(0).
Two, one or none. Two x-intercepts: two real roots. The vertex on the axis: one (double) root. Opens up with the vertex above the axis, or down with it below: no real roots. A calculator's zero finds them as decimals.
From the zeros. Zeros r and s: y=a(x-r)(x-s); a double zero: y=a(x-r)^2. Substitute one more point to find a, then expand for general form.
