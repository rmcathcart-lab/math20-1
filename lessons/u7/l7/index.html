The Three Cards

Three cards go in a hat: one is red on both sides, one is white on both sides, and one is red on one side, white on the other. I pull one out without looking and lay it on the table. The side facing up is red.

I bet you $1 that the other side is red too. If it’s white, you win $1. Is that a fair bet?

?
Vote first: fair or unfair? Then convince your neighbour.
Unfair: I win 2 times out of 3. Count the red faces, not the cards. There are 3 red faces that could be facing up: 2 of them belong to the red–red card (its other side is red) and only 1 to the red–white card. So the other side is red with probability \frac23.
Most people say “it’s one of two cards, so 50–50”, but the red–red card is twice as likely to land red side up. Try it with three paper cards and 30 draws.

Reciprocal Functions of Quadratic Functions

The reciprocal rules from Lesson 6 work for any function. A parabola just gives the graph of y=\dfrac{1}{f(x)} more features: two, one or no vertical asymptotes, and a turning point where the parabola has its vertex.

I can…
  • graph y=\dfrac{1}{f(x)} from a table of values of a quadratic f
  • tell how many vertical asymptotes the reciprocal has from the zeros of f (or the discriminant)
  • find the turning point \left(p,\ \frac1q\right) and state the domain and range of y=\dfrac{1}{f(x)}
  • sketch y=\dfrac{1}{f(x)} from the graph of a parabola, and work backwards from the reciprocal graph to f
  • convert points between y=f(x) and y=\dfrac{1}{f(x)} and match functions to reciprocal graphs

The Reciprocal Rules, Applied to a Parabola

The rules from Lesson 6 work for any function. A parabola just gives the reciprocal graph more features.

Key idea: from y=f(x) to y=\dfrac{1}{f(x)}
  • each point (x,\ y) with y\ne0 becomes \left(x,\ \frac1y\right); the y-intercept (0,\ c) becomes \left(0,\ \frac1c\right)
  • each zero of f gives a vertical asymptote x=a of the reciprocal graph
  • \frac{1}{f(x)} is never 0; as f(x) grows large (positive or negative), \frac{1}{f(x)} approaches 0: the horizontal asymptote is y=0
  • where f(x)=1 or f(x)=-1, the reciprocal is the same number: these are the invariant points (on the lines y=\pm1)
  • \frac{1}{f(x)} has the same sign as f(x): the reciprocal graph is above the x-axis exactly where f is
  • where f increases, \frac1f decreases, and where f decreases, \frac1f increases (on each interval between asymptotes)
Check a sketch on the calculator. Enter the quadratic as Y1 and Y2 = 1/Y1 (VARS►11 types Y1), and use 2ndTABLE to read values. Example 1: Y1 = X² − 2X − 3.

How Many Vertical Asymptotes?

The vertical asymptotes of y=\dfrac{1}{f(x)} are at the zeros of f. A quadratic function has two, one or no real zeros, so its reciprocal has two, one or no vertical asymptotes. Find the zeros by factoring or the quadratic formula, or count them with the discriminant b^2-4ac (Unit 5).

f(x)=\frac12x^2-2
f(x)=\frac12x^2
f(x)=\frac12x^2+1
Key idea: zeros of f → vertical asymptotes of y=\dfrac{1}{f(x)} (f dashed, its reciprocal solid, above)
  • Two zeros (b^2-4ac>0): two vertical asymptotes; three branches, and the middle one has a turning point.
  • One zero (b^2-4ac=0, the vertex is on the x-axis): one vertical asymptote; two branches on the same side of the x-axis.
  • No zeros (b^2-4ac<0): no vertical asymptote; one unbroken curve, entirely above or entirely below the x-axis.

The Turning Point, the Domain and the Range

On an interval where f(x) keeps the same sign, \dfrac{1}{f(x)} is largest where f(x) is smallest, and smallest where f(x) is largest. So the vertex of the parabola becomes the turning point of the reciprocal graph, with its type switched.

Key idea: the turning point
If the vertex of f(x)=a(x-p)^2+q is (p,\ q) with q\ne0, the graph of y=\dfrac{1}{f(x)} has a turning point at \left(p,\ \frac1q\right):
  • a>0 (minimum of f) → a maximum point of the reciprocal graph; a<0 (maximum of f) → a minimum point.
  • If q=0 the vertex is a zero: there is a vertical asymptote x=p and no turning point. If q=1 or q=-1 the vertex is an invariant point.
Key idea: domain and range
Domain: every real number except the zeros of f.   Range: take the reciprocal of each part of the range of f (never y=0).
Range of y=\dfrac{1}{f(x)}q>0q<0q=0
a>0 (opens up)\left\{y\mid 0<y\le\frac1q,\ y\in\mathbb{R}\right\}\left\{y\mid y\le\frac1q\text{ or }y>0,\ y\in\mathbb{R}\right\}\{y\mid y>0,\ y\in\mathbb{R}\}
a<0 (opens down)\left\{y\mid y<0\text{ or }y\ge\frac1q,\ y\in\mathbb{R}\right\}\left\{y\mid \frac1q\le y<0,\ y\in\mathbb{R}\right\}\{y\mid y<0,\ y\in\mathbb{R}\}

You do not need to memorize the table. For example, if f has range y\ge-2: the values -2\le f(x)<0 have reciprocals \dfrac{1}{f(x)}\le-\dfrac12, and the values f(x)>0 have reciprocals \dfrac{1}{f(x)}>0.

Sketching y=\dfrac{1}{f(x)} for a Quadratic

Steps
  1. Graph y=f(x) and draw the lines y=1 and y=-1.
  2. Draw a dashed vertical asymptote at each zero; the horizontal asymptote is y=0.
  3. Mark the invariant points (where f meets y=\pm1), the reciprocal y-intercept \left(0,\ \frac1c\right) and the turning point \left(p,\ \frac1q\right).
  4. Draw each branch on the same side of the x-axis as f: it heads to \pm\infty beside an asymptote, and toward y=0 where f(x) is large.

Working Backwards from y=\dfrac{1}{f(x)}

When the reciprocal graph is given and f is quadratic, reverse the steps: each vertical asymptote x=a gives a zero a of f, a turning point (p,\ k) gives the vertex \left(p,\ \frac1k\right), and a y-intercept \frac1c gives f(0)=c. Write f(x)=a(x-p)^2+q and use a zero or another point to find a.

Points, Matching and Reasoning

A point (x,\ y) is on y=f(x) exactly when \left(x,\ \frac1y\right) is on y=\dfrac{1}{f(x)}, so the conversion works both ways. To find where the reciprocal equals k, solve f(x)=\dfrac1k.

Example 7

Points both ways; y=x^2 and y=\dfrac{1}{x^2}

(a) The point (3,\ 8) is on y=f(x). State the matching point on y=\dfrac{1}{f(x)}.
\left(3,\ \frac18\right)
(b) The point (-2,\ -0.25) is on y=\dfrac{1}{f(x)}. State the matching point on y=f(x).
\left(-2,\ \frac{1}{-0.25}\right)=(-2,\ -4)
(c) Let f(x)=x^2+x. The point \left(a,\ \frac16\right) is on y=\dfrac{1}{f(x)}. Find the possible values of a.
f(a)=6: a^2+a-6=0, (a+3)(a-2)=0, a=-3 or a=2
(d) Sketch y=x^2 and y=\dfrac{1}{x^2} on the grid. State the asymptotes, the invariant points and the range of y=\dfrac{1}{x^2}. Explain why its graph is never below the x-axis.
Asymptotes x=0 and y=0. Invariant points: x^2=1 gives (-1,\ 1) and (1,\ 1) (x^2=-1 has no solution).
Range \{y\mid y>0,\ y\in\mathbb{R}\}. x^2\ge0, and its reciprocal has the same sign, so \frac{1}{x^2}>0.

Key ideas

Point by point. (x,\ y)\to\left(x,\ \frac1y\right). Zeros of f → vertical asymptotes; y=0 is the horizontal asymptote; f=\pm1 → invariant points; same sign as f.
Two, one or none. Two zeros (b^2-4ac>0): two vertical asymptotes, three branches. One zero (=0): one asymptote, two branches. No zeros (<0): one unbroken curve.
Turning point \left(p,\ \frac1q\right). The vertex (p,\ q) of f becomes a turning point of the other type. Domain: all x except the zeros. Range: the reciprocal of each part of the range of f.
Backwards. Asymptotes → zeros of f; turning point (p,\ k) → vertex \left(p,\ \frac1k\right); y-intercept \frac1c → f(0)=c. Then find a.
