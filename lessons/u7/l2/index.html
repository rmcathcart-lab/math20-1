The Distance Game

I’m thinking of a whole number from 1 to 100. You guess, and I tell you only how far your guess is from my number. Not “higher” or “lower”: just the distance.

What is the smallest number of guesses that will always tell you my number for sure?

?
Play it with a partner first. Where would you make your first guess?
Just one! Guess 1 (or 100). If I say “36 away”, my number can only be 37: there is nothing below 1, so only one number is 36 away.
A guess in the middle is a trap: from 50, “13 away” could be 37 or 63, one on each side. (With “higher or lower” you would need up to 7 guesses. Here, one guess tells you the number, and the second one wins.)

Graphing Absolute Value Functions of Linear Functions

Put absolute value bars around a whole function and its graph can never go below the x-axis. The graph of y=|f(x)| is the graph of y=f(x) with the part below the axis flipped up; for a line, that makes a V.

I can…
  • graph y=|x| and state its vertex, domain and range
  • graph y=|f(x)| from a table or the graph of y=f(x) by reflecting the part below the x-axis, and name the invariant points
  • state the vertex, y-intercept, domain and range of y=|ax+b| and sketch it without a table
  • write y=|ax+b| in piecewise form
  • reason about matching points on y=f(x) and y=|f(x)|, and solve a distance problem with the graph

1

The Graph of y=|x|

To graph the absolute value function y=|x|, compare its table with the table of y=x.

x-3-2-10123
y=x-3-2-10123
y=|x|3210123

For x\ge0 the y-values are the same. For x<0 the y-values of y=x are negative and those of y=|x| are their opposites, so those points are reflected in the x-axis.

The graph of y=|x| is a V shape with its vertex at (0,\ 0). It is made of two half-lines, the two pieces of the definition: y=x for x\ge0 and y=-x for x<0.
Domain: \{x\mid x\in\mathbb{R}\}    Range: \{y\mid y\ge0,\ y\in\mathbb{R}\}

Graphing y=|f(x)| from the Graph of y=f(x)

Example 1 shows the rule for any function y=f(x).

Key idea: to graph y=|f(x)| from y=f(x)
  • where the graph of y=f(x) is on or above the x-axis (f(x)\ge0), keep it;
  • where it is below the x-axis (f(x)<0), reflect it in the x-axis: (x,\ y)\to(x,\ -y).
The graph of y=|f(x)| is never below the x-axis. Points on the x-axis do not move: the x-intercepts of y=f(x) are the invariant points.

A non-horizontal line y=ax+b crosses the x-axis once, so the graph of y=|ax+b| is a V shape whose vertex is that x-intercept.

3

Characteristics of y=|ax+b|

Key idea: for y=|ax+b|, a\ne0
  • vertex (the x-intercept and invariant point): solve ax+b=0, giving \left(-\dfrac ba,\ 0\right)
  • y-intercept: (0,\ |b|)
  • domain \{x\mid x\in\mathbb{R}\};  range \{y\mid y\ge0,\ y\in\mathbb{R}\}
  • the two arms have slopes a and -a; the graph is symmetric about the vertical line through the vertex.

Compared with the line y=ax+b: the domain and the x-intercept stay the same, the range changes from all real numbers to y\ge0, and the y-intercept b becomes |b|.

Piecewise Form of y=|ax+b|

Apply the definition of absolute value to the whole expression ax+b.

|ax+b|=\begin{cases}\ \ ax+b, & \text{if } ax+b\ge0\\ -(ax+b), & \text{if } ax+b<0\end{cases}
Solve ax+b\ge0 for x to see where each piece applies. Dividing by a negative number reverses the inequality sign.

The boundary is the x-coordinate of the vertex. At the vertex both pieces equal 0, so the equal sign can go with either piece; we put it with the first. On the graph, the piece ax+b is the original line (above the axis) and the piece -(ax+b) is the reflected arm.

Example 5

Write it in piecewise form

Write each function in piecewise form.

(a) y=|x+5|
x+5\ge0\Rightarrow x\ge-5
y=\begin{cases}x+5, & \text{if } x\ge-5\\ -x-5, & \text{if } x<-5\end{cases}
(b) y=|2x-7|
2x-7\ge0\Rightarrow x\ge\frac72
y=\begin{cases}2x-7, & \text{if } x\ge\frac72\\ -2x+7, & \text{if } x<\frac72\end{cases}
(c) y=|6-3x|
6-3x\ge0\Rightarrow-3x\ge-6\Rightarrow x\le2 (sign reversed)
y=\begin{cases}6-3x, & \text{if } x\le2\\ 3x-6, & \text{if } x>2\end{cases}
(d) y=\left|-\frac12x+1\right|
-\frac12x+1\ge0\Rightarrow-\frac12x\ge-1\Rightarrow x\le2
y=\begin{cases}-\frac12x+1, & \text{if } x\le2\\ \frac12x-1, & \text{if } x>2\end{cases}
Example 6

True or false?

True or false? Explain.

(a) |x-4|=x-4 if x\ge4
True: for x\ge4, x-4\ge0, so the bars change nothing.
(b) |3x+6|=3x+6 if x<-2
False: for x<-2, 3x+6<0, so |3x+6|=-3x-6.
(Test x=-3: \lvert -3\rvert=3, but 3(-3)+6=-3.)
(c) |5-x|=x-5 if x>5
True: for x>5, 5-x<0, so |5-x|=-(5-x)=x-5.
(d) |2x-3|=-2x+3 if x\le\frac32
True: for x\le\frac32, 2x-3\le0, so |2x-3|=-(2x-3)=-2x+3.
5

Reasoning with y=|f(x)|

  • A point (x,\ y) on y=f(x) becomes the point (x,\ |y|) on y=|f(x)|.
  • If (x,\ k) with k>0 is on y=|f(x)|, then f(x)=k or f(x)=-k: the point on y=f(x) is (x,\ k) or (x,\ -k).
  • For a linear function, the same V comes from two lines: y=f(x) and its reflection y=-f(x).
  • |f(x)| is never negative, so an equation such as |f(x)|=-4 has no solution and |f(x)|<0 is never true.
Example 7

Points, and working backwards

(a) The point (-4,\ -7) is on the graph of y=f(x). State the matching point on y=|f(x)|.
(-4,\ \lvert -7\rvert)=(-4,\ 7)
(b) The point (2,\ 5) is on the graph of y=|g(x)|. State the possible points on y=g(x).
(2,\ 5) or (2,\ -5)
(d) Explain why the equation |f(x)|=-4 has no solution.
An absolute value is never negative, so |f(x)| can never equal -4. The graph of y=|f(x)| never reaches the line y=-4.
(c) The graph of y=|f(x)|, where f is linear, is shown. Sketch the two possible graphs of y=f(x) and write their equations. Then write y=|f(x)| in the form y=|ax+b|.
The right arm passes through (2,\ 0) and (4,\ 3): slope \frac32, so one line is y=\frac32x-3.
Its reflection in the x-axis is y=-\frac32x+3 (through (0,\ 3) and (2,\ 0)).
y=\left|\frac32x-3\right| (or, the same function, y=\left|-\frac32x+3\right|).
