The Skipping Stone

Maya skips a stone across a pond that is 20 m wide. Its first hop is 4 m long, and every hop after that is ¾ as long as the hop before it.

Pretend the stone never sinks: it keeps hopping forever.

Does the stone ever reach the other side of the pond?

?
Guess first: yes or no? Then add up a few hops.
No, never! The hops are 4+3+2.25+1.6875+\dots metres: a geometric series with r=\frac34.
Its sum is S_\infty=\dfrac{4}{1-\frac34}=16 m, so even after infinitely many hops the stone gets closer and closer to 16 m and stays 4 m short of the far side.
The twist: infinitely many hops can still add up to a short distance. (The first 10 hops already cover about 15 m; the next million barely add another metre.)

Graphing Absolute Value Functions of Quadratic Functions

The rule from Lesson 2 works for parabolas too: keep what is on or above the x-axis and flip up what is below it. A parabola that dips below the axis turns into a W (or an M with its arms bent up), and where the parabola sits decides how much of it changes.

From y=f(x) to y=|f(x)| for a Parabola

The rule from Lesson 2 works for every function: keep the part of the graph of y=f(x) that is on or above the x-axis, and reflect the part below the x-axis in the x-axis, (x,\ y)\to(x,\ -y). Each y-value of y=|f(x)| is the absolute value of the y-value of y=f(x) at the same x.

For a parabola that crosses the x-axis twice, the part below the axis is a single arc: the arc between the zeros when the parabola opens up, and the two arms outside the zeros when it opens down. After the reflection the graph has a sharp corner at each x-intercept, and the vertex of that arc is reflected too.

Key idea: to sketch y=|f(x)| for a quadratic function f
  1. Sketch y=f(x) (vertex, x-intercepts, y-intercept).
  2. Keep every part on or above the x-axis.
  3. Reflect every part below the x-axis in the x-axis: the y-values change sign, the x-values stay.
  4. The x-intercepts are the invariant points; a vertex (p,\ q) below the axis moves to (p,\ -q).

Sketching y=|f(x)| from a Graph or an Equation

Where the parabola sits decides how much of it changes:

  • it crosses the x-axis: only the part below the axis is reflected, and the graph has two invariant points;
  • it is entirely above the x-axis: nothing changes, |f(x)|=f(x);
  • it is entirely below the x-axis: the whole parabola is reflected, |f(x)|=-f(x), and there are no invariant points.

3

Invariant Points, Domain and Range

Reflecting in the x-axis does not change any x-value, so the domain of y=|f(x)| is the same as the domain of y=f(x): all real numbers for a quadratic function. The range changes, because no y-value of y=|f(x)| is negative.

Key idea: for a quadratic function f(x)=a(x-p)^2+q
Domain of y=|f(x)|: \{x\mid x\in\mathbb{R}\}   ·   y-intercept: |f(0)|

When an arc between the zeros is reflected, its vertex (p,\ -q) is the highest point of that arc but not of the whole graph, since the outer arms keep rising. The graph has no maximum value.

Piecewise Form of y=|f(x)|

Without absolute value bars, |f(x)|=f(x) where f(x)\ge0 and |f(x)|=-f(x) where f(x)<0. For a parabola with zeros r<s, the sign of f(x) changes only at the zeros, so find the zeros first and use the direction of opening (or a test value) to see where f(x)<0.

Key idea
If f opens up with zeros r<s:   |f(x)|=\begin{cases}\ \ f(x), & \text{if } x\le r\text{ or }x\ge s\\ -f(x), & \text{if } r<x<s\end{cases}
If f opens down, the pieces swap: |f(x)|=f(x) for r\le x\le s and -f(x) for x<r or x>s.
If f(x)\ge0 for every x, one piece is enough: |f(x)|=f(x). If f(x)<0 for every x, |f(x)|=-f(x).

The piecewise form can also be written in three pieces (left arm, middle arc, right arm). At a zero both pieces give 0, so it does not matter which piece includes it.

Example 5

Piecewise form

Write each function in piecewise form, without absolute value symbols.

(a) y=|x^2-x-12|
(x-4)(x+3)=0: zeros -3 and 4; opens up, so f(x)<0 between them.
y=\begin{cases} x^2-x-12, & \text{if } x\le-3\text{ or }x\ge4\\ -x^2+x+12, & \text{if } -3<x<4\end{cases}
(b) y=|16-4x^2|
4(2-x)(2+x)=0: zeros \pm2; opens down, so f(x)<0 outside them.
y=\begin{cases} 16-4x^2, & \text{if } -2\le x\le2\\ 4x^2-16, & \text{if } x<-2\text{ or }x>2\end{cases}
(c) y=|x^2+4x+7|
x^2+4x+7=(x+2)^2+3\ge3 for every x (vertex (-2,\ 3), opens up).
One piece: y=x^2+4x+7, x\in\mathbb{R}.
(d) Use (a) to evaluate y at x=1 and at x=5. Check with the bars.
x=1 is between -3 and 4: y=-1+1+12=12.  Check |1-1-12|=12.
x=5\ge4: y=25-5-12=8.  Check |8|=8.
Example 6

True or false?

True or false? Explain.

(a) |x^2-1|=1-x^2 if -1\le x\le1
True: for -1\le x\le1, x^2\le1, so x^2-1\le0 and |x^2-1|=-(x^2-1).
(b) |x^2-25|=x^2-25 if x>-5
False: x=0>-5 gives \left|-25\right|=25, but 0-25=-25.
It is true only for x\le-5 or x\ge5.
(c) |(x-6)^2|=(x-6)^2 for every real x
True: a square is never negative.
(d) \left|-x^2+4\right|=|x^2-4| for every real x
True: -x^2+4=-(x^2-4), and opposites have the same absolute value. The two graphs are identical.
5

Working Backwards and Solving Problems

The graph of y=|f(x)| does not show which parts of y=f(x) were below the axis. If f is quadratic, there are two possibilities: f(x) or its reflection -f(x), because both have the same absolute value. A point (x,\ k) on y=|f(x)| with k>0 comes from (x,\ k) or (x,\ -k) on y=f(x).

Example 7

Which f was it?

(a) The graph of y=|f(x)| is shown, where f is a quadratic function. Sketch the two possible graphs of y=f(x) and write the equation of each in factored form.
Invariant points (-2,\ 0) and (4,\ 0); the middle arc has its vertex at (1,\ 9).
If the middle arc was reflected, f opens up with vertex (1,\ -9): f(x)=a(x+2)(x-4) and f(1)=a(3)(-3)=-9, so a=1: f(x)=(x+2)(x-4).
Otherwise f opens down with vertex (1,\ 9): f(x)=-(x+2)(x-4).

Key ideas

Flip what is below. For y=|f(x)|: keep every part on or above the x-axis, reflect every part below it, (x,\ y)\to(x,\ -y). The x-intercepts are the invariant points; a vertex (p,\ q) below the axis moves to (p,\ -q).
Three cases. Crosses the axis: invariant points at the zeros, range y\ge0. Entirely above: no change, range y\ge q. Entirely below: the whole parabola flips, range y\ge-q. Domain \{x\mid x\in\mathbb{R}\}; y-intercept |f(0)|.
Piecewise form. The zeros cut the domain: f(x) where f(x)\ge0, -f(x) where f(x)<0. Opens up: -f(x) between the zeros; opens down: outside them. No zeros: one piece.
Working backwards. The graph of y=|f(x)| comes from f(x) or -f(x): two possible quadratics. A range that starts above 0 means the parabola never reaches the x-axis.
