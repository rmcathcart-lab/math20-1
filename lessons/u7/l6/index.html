The Gas Upgrade

Your family can upgrade ONE vehicle. The old farm truck would go from 5 km per litre to 10 km per litre. The hybrid car would go from 20 km per litre to 40 km per litre. Each one drives 1000 km a month.

Which upgrade saves more gas each month?

?
Make a guess first. Then work it out.
The truck, by a lot! Truck: 1000\div5=200 L a month becomes 1000\div10=100 L, a saving of 100 L. Hybrid: 1000\div20=50 L becomes 1000\div40=25 L, a saving of only 25 L.
Both numbers doubled, but the gas used is 1000\div(\text{km per litre}): doubling a small number saves far more than doubling a big one. That is one reason Canada rates cars in litres per 100 km instead.

1

Reciprocals and the Graph of y=\dfrac{1}{x}

The reciprocal of a non-zero number a is \dfrac{1}{a}. A number and its reciprocal have the same sign; the reciprocal of a large number is close to 0, the reciprocal of a number close to 0 is large, 1 and -1 are their own reciprocals, and 0 has no reciprocal.

The reciprocal function of y=f(x) is y=\dfrac{1}{f(x)}. For example, the reciprocal of f(x)=x is y=\dfrac{1}{x}, and the reciprocal of f(x)=2x+5 is y=\dfrac{1}{2x+5}.

Each value flips: 4\to\tfrac{1}{4}, \tfrac{1}{4}\to4. \tfrac{1}{0} is undefined.
(b) The line y=x is drawn. Sketch y=\dfrac{1}{x} on the same grid.
Two branches: one in the first quadrant, one in the third.
(c) State the points where the two graphs meet, the asymptotes of y=\dfrac{1}{x}, and its domain and range.
They meet at (1,\ 1) and (-1,\ -1), where x=\tfrac{1}{x}.
The graph never touches the y-axis (x=0 is not allowed) and gets closer and closer to it: the vertical asymptote is x=0.
As x gets large (positive or negative), \tfrac{1}{x} gets close to 0 but is never 0: the horizontal asymptote is y=0.
Domain \{x\mid x\ne0,\ x\in\mathbb{R}\}; range \{y\mid y\ne0,\ y\in\mathbb{R}\}.

Comparing y=f(x) and y=\dfrac{1}{f(x)}

Each y-value of y=\dfrac{1}{f(x)} is the reciprocal of the y-value of y=f(x) at the same x, so a point (x,\ y) on y=f(x) becomes \left(x,\ \dfrac{1}{y}\right) (when y\ne0).

These examples show what happens for any function.

Properties of y=\dfrac{1}{f(x)}
  • Where f(x)=0, the reciprocal is undefined: a vertical asymptote (for a line, at its x-intercept).
  • Where f(x)=1 or f(x)=-1, the reciprocal is the same number: the invariant points lie on the lines y=1 and y=-1.
  • \dfrac{1}{f(x)} has the same sign as f(x): both graphs are above the x-axis, or both are below, at each x.
  • Where f(x) increases, \dfrac{1}{f(x)} decreases, and the other way around.
  • Where |f(x)| is large, \dfrac{1}{f(x)} is near 0: the horizontal asymptote is y=0. A reciprocal is never 0, so the graph has no x-intercept.
  • Where f(x) is near 0, \left|\dfrac{1}{f(x)}\right| is large: the graph runs along the vertical asymptote.
  • The y-intercept of y=\dfrac{1}{f(x)} is the reciprocal of the y-intercept of y=f(x); a point where f(x)=2 becomes a point where y=\tfrac{1}{2}, and so on.
3

Sketching y=\dfrac{1}{f(x)} from the Graph of a Line

To sketch y=\dfrac{1}{f(x)} from the line y=f(x)
1Draw the vertical asymptote (dashed) through the x-intercept, and the horizontal asymptote y=0.
2Mark the invariant points where the line meets y=1 and y=-1.
3Mark a few more points, such as the reciprocal of the y-intercept, or y=\tfrac{1}{2} where f(x)=2.
4Draw two smooth branches, one on each side of the vertical asymptote, on the same side of the x-axis as the line, approaching both asymptotes.

The graph of the reciprocal of a non-horizontal line always has two branches in diagonally opposite regions, like y=\dfrac{1}{x}.

Example 6

Points both ways

(a) The graph of y=f(x) passes through (5,\ -4). Which point with x=5 is on the graph of y=\dfrac{1}{f(x)}?
\frac{1}{-4}=-\frac{1}{4}: the point \left(5,\ -\frac{1}{4}\right).
(b) The graph of y=\dfrac{1}{f(x)} passes through (-3,\ 0.2). Which point with x=-3 is on the graph of y=f(x)?
f(-3)=\frac{1}{0.2}=5: the point (-3,\ 5).
(c) For f(x)=\frac{1}{2}x+1, determine the point on y=\dfrac{1}{f(x)} where y=4.
\frac{1}{f(x)}=4\Rightarrow f(x)=\frac{1}{4}
\frac{1}{2}x+1=\frac{1}{4}\Rightarrow\frac{1}{2}x=-\frac{3}{4}\Rightarrow x=-\frac{3}{2}
The point is \left(-\frac{3}{2},\ 4\right).
(d) Can the graph of y=\dfrac{1}{f(x)} ever have y=0? Explain.
No. A fraction \frac{1}{f(x)} with numerator 1 is never 0, however large f(x) is. That is why y=0 is an asymptote and 0 is not in the range.

Working Backwards and Matching

The features go both ways: a vertical asymptote x=a of y=\dfrac{1}{f(x)} is a zero of f, an invariant point is on both graphs, and a point \left(x,\ \dfrac{1}{y}\right) on the reciprocal gives (x,\ y) on the line. Two points determine the line y=f(x).

Example 7

From the reciprocal back to the line

The graph of y=\dfrac{1}{f(x)}, where f(x) is a linear function, is shown. It has the vertical asymptote x=1.5 and passes through the invariant point (2,\ 1).

(a) Sketch the graph of y=f(x) on the grid.
The zero of f is 1.5, so (1.5,\ 0) is on the line; (2,\ 1) is on it too. Draw the line through these points (it also passes through (1,\ -1)).
(b) Determine the equation of f(x) in the form f(x)=ax+b, and the y-intercept of y=\dfrac{1}{f(x)}.
Slope a=\frac{1-0}{2-1.5}=2;  0=2(1.5)+b\Rightarrow b=-3
f(x)=2x-3
y-intercept of the reciprocal: \frac{1}{-3}=-\frac{1}{3} (matches the graph)

Key ideas

Partner points. (x,\ y) on the line becomes \left(x,\ \tfrac{1}{y}\right): same sign, big ↔ small, and \pm1 stay put (the invariant points, on y=\pm1).
Asymptotes. A vertical asymptote at the zero of f (the line’s x-intercept), and the horizontal asymptote y=0: the reciprocal is never 0.
Sketching. Asymptotes, invariant points on y=\pm1, a few more points, then two branches on the same side of the x-axis as the line.
From the equation. For y=\tfrac{1}{ax+b}: ax+b=0 gives the asymptote, ax+b=\pm1 the invariant points, \tfrac{1}{b} the y-intercept; range \{y\mid y\ne0\}.
