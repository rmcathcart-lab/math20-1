Geometric Sequences

Last lesson every step added the same amount. Today every step multiplies by the same number, the common ratio r. Repeated multiplying is a power, so the general term has an exponent: t_n=\textcolor{#a33357}{t_1}\textcolor{#c27a0e}{r}^{\,n-1}.

I can…
  • tell a geometric sequence from an arithmetic one and find r=\tfrac{t_n}{t_{n-1}}
  • write the general term t_n=t_1r^{n-1} and use it to find any term (and explain why 5(3)^{n-1}\ne15^{n-1})
  • describe the curved graph of a geometric sequence (growing, shrinking toward 0, or flipping sides)
  • find the number of terms by writing both sides as powers of the same base
  • find r and t_1 from two terms (an odd power gives one r, an even power gives \pm r) and insert geometric means
  • use b^2=ac when expressions are consecutive terms
RF10 Analyze geometric sequences and series to solve problems.
Today: geometric sequences, their general term t_n=t_1r^{n-1} and their graphs.

The Common Ratio

In a geometric sequence each term is the previous term multiplied by the same non-zero number, the common ratio r. Find r by dividing any term by the term before it.
Common ratio
r=\dfrac{t_n}{t_{n-1}}\quad(n\ge2)
e.g. r=\dfrac{t_2}{t_1}=\dfrac{t_3}{t_2}

At least three terms are needed to decide the type of sequence: two terms such as 4,\ 8,\ \dots fit an arithmetic sequence (d=4) and a geometric sequence (r=2). A negative r makes the signs alternate.

Think Why must the common ratio be non-zero? What would happen to the sequence if r=0?
Every term after t_1 would be 0 (5,\ 0,\ 0,\ 0,\ \dots), and you could not divide by the 0 terms to check the ratio.

The General Term

Starting from t_1, each step multiplies by r once more:

\textcolor{#a33357}{t_1},\qquad t_2=t_1\textcolor{#c27a0e}{r},\qquad t_3=t_1\textcolor{#c27a0e}{r^2},\qquad t_4=t_1\textcolor{#c27a0e}{r^3},\qquad\dots

The exponent on r is always one less than the position.

General term of a geometric sequence
t_n=\textcolor{#a33357}{t_1}\,\textcolor{#c27a0e}{r}^{\,n-1}
t_n = the term in position n,  t_1 = first term,  r = common ratio,  n = position (a natural number)
!
Leave it as t_1r^{n-1}. Do not multiply t_1 by r first: 5(3)^{n-1} is not 15^{n-1}, because the exponent applies only to the 3. When t_1 is a power of r, the exponent laws combine them into a single power.
On the calculator, type t_1r^{n-1} just as it is written; the calculator does the power first. Put a negative or fraction r in brackets: 1250×((−)1÷5)^7ENTER, then MATH1ENTER (►Frac) turns the decimal into a fraction.
Example 2

The general term, then one term

Write the general term of each sequence, then determine the indicated term.

(a) 7,\ 14,\ 28,\ \dots;  t_{12}
t_n=7(2)^{n-1}
t_{12}=7(2)^{11}=7(2\,048)=\boldsymbol{14\,336}
(b) 1\,250,\ -250,\ 50,\ \dots;  t_8
r=-\frac15,  t_n=1\,250\left(-\frac15\right)^{n-1}
t_8=1\,250\left(-\frac15\right)^{7}=-\frac{1\,250}{78\,125}=\boldsymbol{-\frac{2}{125}}  (-0.016)
(c) \frac29,\ \frac23,\ 2,\ \dots;  t_9
r=3,  t_n=\frac29(3)^{n-1}
t_9=\frac29(3)^8=\frac29(6\,561)=\boldsymbol{1\,458}
(d) 4,\ 8,\ 16,\ \dots; write t_n as a single power of 2.
t_n=4(2)^{n-1}=2^2\cdot2^{n-1}=\boldsymbol{2^{n+1}}
Check: n=1 gives 2^2=4 ✓

The graph of a geometric sequence

A geometric sequence is a function of n with an exponent: its graph is non-linear. When r>1 the points curve up steeply; when 0<r<1 they curve down towards zero; when r<0 they jump from one side of the n-axis to the other. The points (n,\ t_n) are separate dots: never join them.

Example 3

Plot the terms

Plot the first terms of each sequence and describe the graph.

(a) t_n=64(0.5)^{n-1},  n=1 to 6
64,\ 32,\ 16,\ 8,\ 4,\ 2. The points curve down and level off towards 0: non-linear.
(b) t_n=(-2)^{n-1},  n=1 to 5
1,\ -2,\ 4,\ -8,\ 16. The points alternate above and below the n-axis and move further away: non-linear.

Finding the Number of Terms

To find the number of terms, set t_n equal to the last term and solve for n. Divide both sides by t_1, then write both sides as powers of the same base and compare exponents. (A table of values of t_1r^{n-1} for n=1,\ 2,\ 3,\ \dots also works: on the TI-84, 2nd TABLE.)
Check your n. Put it back into t_1r^{n-1}: the calculator should give the last term. For Example 4(b), n=8: 1215×((−)1÷3)^7ENTER then MATH1ENTER (►Frac) shows -5/9.
Or find n with a table of values. For Example 4(a), Y= Y_1=3\times2^{X-1}, 2ndTBLSET TblStart = 5, ΔTbl = 1, then 2ndTABLE: 6144 is at X=12.
Example 4

How many terms?

Determine the number of terms in each sequence.

(a) 3,\ 6,\ 12,\ \dots,\ 6\,144
t_1=3, r=2
3(2)^{n-1}=6\,144
2^{n-1}=2\,048=2^{11}
n-1=11\Rightarrow n=12: 12 terms
(b) 1\,215,\ -405,\ 135,\ \dots,\ -\frac59
t_1=1\,215, r=-\frac13
1\,215\left(-\frac13\right)^{n-1}=-\frac59
\left(-\frac13\right)^{n-1}=-\frac{5}{9\cdot1\,215}=-\frac{1}{2\,187}=\left(-\frac13\right)^{7}
n-1=7\Rightarrow n=8: 8 terms

Two Terms Known: Finding t_1 and r

If two terms are known, write each with the general term and divide the equations: t_1 cancels and a power of r is left. Then substitute r back to find t_1.
r^3=-8
An odd power has one real root: r=-2.
r^2=9
An even power has two: r=\pm3. Both values give a valid sequence, so there are two answers.
Example 5

An odd power

In a geometric sequence, t_3=12 and t_6=-96. Determine r, t_1 and the general term.
t_1r^5=-96 and t_1r^2=12. Divide: \dfrac{t_1r^5}{t_1r^2}=\dfrac{-96}{12}\Rightarrow r^3=-8\Rightarrow \boldsymbol{r=-2}
t_1(-2)^2=12\Rightarrow 4t_1=12\Rightarrow \boldsymbol{t_1=3}
\boldsymbol{t_n=3(-2)^{n-1}} (the sequence is 3,\ -6,\ 12,\ -24,\ 48,\ -96,\ \dots)
Example 6

An even power: two sequences

In a geometric sequence, t_2=12 and t_4=108. Explain why there are two possible sequences, and determine t_1 and t_5 for each.
\dfrac{t_1r^3}{t_1r}=\dfrac{108}{12}\Rightarrow r^2=9\Rightarrow r=3 or r=-3. Both values work, so there are two sequences.
r=3:  t_1=12\div3=\boldsymbol{4}, sequence 4,\ 12,\ 36,\ 108,\ \dots,  t_5=4(3)^4=\boldsymbol{324}
r=-3:  t_1=12\div(-3)=\boldsymbol{-4}, sequence -4,\ 12,\ -36,\ 108,\ \dots,  t_5=-4(-3)^4=\boldsymbol{-324}
Brackets matter for an even power. -4(-3)^4 needs (−)4×((−)3)^4ENTER = −324. Without the brackets the calculator works out -(3^4) and the sign comes out wrong.
5

Geometric Means

The terms between two non-consecutive terms of a geometric sequence are called geometric means. To insert k means between two numbers, treat the numbers as t_1 and the last term of a sequence of k+2 terms, find r, then multiply.
Example 7

Insert the means

(a) Insert three geometric means between 5 and 405.
5 terms: t_1=5, t_5=405
5r^4=405\Rightarrow r^4=81\Rightarrow r=\pm3
r=3:  5,\ \underline{15},\ \underline{45},\ \underline{135},\ 405
r=-3:  5,\ \underline{-15},\ \underline{45},\ \underline{-135},\ 405
(b) Insert two geometric means between 4 and -500.
4 terms: t_1=4, t_4=-500
4r^3=-500\Rightarrow r^3=-125\Rightarrow r=-5
4,\ \underline{-20},\ \underline{100},\ -500
6

Expressions in a Geometric Sequence

If a, b, c are consecutive terms of a geometric sequence, the ratios are equal:

\dfrac{b}{a}=\dfrac{c}{b} which cross-multiplies to b^2=ac

Set up this equation with the expressions, solve, and check that no term is zero.

Example 8

Find x

Each set of expressions gives three consecutive terms of a geometric sequence. Determine x, the terms, and r.

(a) x+1,\ \ x+4,\ \ x+10
(x+4)^2=(x+1)(x+10)
x^2+8x+16=x^2+11x+10
6=3x\Rightarrow \boldsymbol{x=2}
Terms 3,\ 6,\ 12;  r=2
(b) x,\ \ x+4,\ \ 9x
(x+4)^2=x(9x)
x^2+8x+16=9x^2\Rightarrow 8x^2-8x-16=0
x^2-x-2=0\Rightarrow(x-2)(x+1)=0
x=2:  2,\ 6,\ 18,  r=3
x=-1:  -1,\ 3,\ -9,  r=-3
✓

Key ideas

Divide to find r. r=\dfrac{t_n}{t_{n-1}}, the same for every pair. Check with three terms; a negative r makes the signs alternate.
General term t_n=t_1r^{n-1}. The exponent is one less than the position. Leave it as t_1r^{n-1}: 5(3)^{n-1}\ne15^{n-1}. The graph is curved dots: never a line.
Number of terms. Set t_1r^{n-1} equal to the last term, divide by t_1, write both sides as powers of the same base, and compare exponents.
Two terms, means, expressions. Divide the two terms to get a power of r: odd power → one r, even power → \pm r. k means make k+2 terms. For a,\ b,\ c: b^2=ac.
