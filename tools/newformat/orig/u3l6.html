The Cosine Law

The sine law needs an angle and the side opposite it. When you know two sides and the angle between them (SAS) or all three sides (SSS), there is no such pair. Today: one formula that works for both, built from Pythagoras.

I can…
  • tell when the sine law can’t start: SAS (the angle is between the sides) and SSS
  • explain where a^2=b^2+c^2-2bc\cos A comes from, and why it is Pythagoras with a correction
  • find the third side from two sides and the contained angle, multiplying before subtracting
  • find an angle from three sides, and read a negative cosine as an obtuse angle
  • solve a whole triangle, and solve problems with bearings and parallelograms

Warm-up

The Snapping Crocodile

Clyde the crocodile’s jaws are hinged at the back of his mouth. His lower jaw is 30 cm long and his upper jaw is 40 cm long. How far apart are the tips of his jaws?

1
Clyde’s mouth is closed (0°). How far apart are the tips?
10 cm. The jaws lie on top of each other, and the upper jaw sticks out 40-30=10 cm past the lower one.
2
He opens to 90°. Now how far apart are the tips?
50 cm. A right triangle: \sqrt{30^2+40^2}=\sqrt{2500}=50.
!
The big one. He opens to 60°. How far apart are the tips now? Guess first.
A 50 cm
B 40 cm
C about 36 cm
D 30 cm
C, about 36 cm. It is not a right triangle, so 50 cm is wrong. Split it: the 30 cm jaw’s tip is 30\sin60^\circ\approx26.0 cm above the upper jaw, at 30\cos60^\circ=15 cm along it, leaving 40-15=25 cm. So the gap is \sqrt{26.0^2+25^2}\approx36.1 cm.
Pythagoras only works at 90°. Today: a formula that works at any angle.

Challenge your neighbour: at what angle is the gap exactly 40 cm, as long as the upper jaw? (You’ll be able to find it by the end of today.)

1

When the Sine Law Cannot Start

The sine law needs a known pair: an angle and the side opposite it. Two cases have no known pair:
  • SAS: two sides and the contained angle (the angle between them);
  • SSS: all three sides.
These triangles can be solved by splitting them into right triangles, or in one step with the cosine law.
Example 1

Can the sine law start?

Decide whether the sine law can be used as the first step. If not, name the case (SAS or SSS).

(a) \angle A, \angle C, c
Yes (\angle C, c)
(b) b, c, \angle A
No: SAS
(c) a, b, c
No: SSS
(d) \angle B, b, a (b>a)
Yes (\angle B, b)
Example 2

Right triangles only

In \triangle ABC, \angle A=42^\circ, AC=9.0 cm and AB=13.0 cm. The altitude CD has length h. Determine BC to the nearest tenth using right triangles only.

\triangle ACD: h=9.0\sin42^\circ\approx6.022 cm and AD=9.0\cos42^\circ\approx6.688 cm.
DB=13.0-6.688\approx6.312 cm
\triangle BCD (Pythagoras): BC=\sqrt{h^2+DB^2}=\sqrt{6.022^2+6.312^2}\approx\boldsymbol{8.7} cm
2

The Cosine Law

Repeating Example 2 with letters gives a formula. Put A at the left end of side c, and let AD=x, so DB=c-x.

Example 3

Complete the proof

In \triangle ACD:  \cos A=\dfrac{x}{b}, so x= b\cos A, and h^2+x^2= b^2.
In \triangle BCD:  a^2=h^2+(c-x)^2
Expand:  a^2=h^2+c^2-2cx+x^2
Regroup:  a^2=(h^2+x^2)+c^2-2cx
Substitute:  a^2= b^2 +\,c^2-2c\,\big(b\cos A\big)
So  a^2=b^2+c^2-2bc\cos A
The blanks: x=b\cos A; h^2+x^2=b^2; then b^2 and b\cos A are substituted.
The Cosine Law. In any \triangle ABC:
\textcolor{#a33357}{a}^2=\textcolor{#1f5bd6}{b}^2+\textcolor{#1f5bd6}{c}^2-2\textcolor{#1f5bd6}{bc}\cos \textcolor{#a33357}{A}\textcolor{#a33357}{b}^2=\textcolor{#1f5bd6}{a}^2+\textcolor{#1f5bd6}{c}^2-2\textcolor{#1f5bd6}{ac}\cos \textcolor{#a33357}{B}\textcolor{#a33357}{c}^2=\textcolor{#1f5bd6}{a}^2+\textcolor{#1f5bd6}{b}^2-2\textcolor{#1f5bd6}{ab}\cos \textcolor{#a33357}{C}
The side on the left is opposite the angle on the right; the other two sides are the ones that form that angle. If the angle is 90^\circ, then \cos90^\circ=0 and the law becomes the Pythagorean theorem.

Pythagoras with a correction

Hold two sides fixed and open the angle between them like a hinge. At 90^\circ the squares on the sides obey Pythagoras exactly. Close the hinge and \textcolor{#a33357}{c^2} falls short of \textcolor{#1f5bd6}{a^2+b^2}; open it past 90^\circ and \textcolor{#a33357}{c^2} is more. The correction \textcolor{#c27a0e}{-2ab\cos C} is exactly the difference.

3

Finding a Side (SAS)

Substitute the two sides and the contained angle, then take the square root. Enter the whole expression at once, for example \sqrt{9^2+13^2-2(9)(13)\cos42^\circ}. When the angle is obtuse, \cos A<0, so the last term is added and the third side is longer.
!
Multiply before you subtract. Do not subtract 2bc from b^2+c^2 before multiplying by \cos A: in b^2+c^2-2bc\cos A the multiplication 2bc\cos A comes first. Working Example 4(a) as (81+169-234)\cos42^\circ gives about 3.4 cm, which is impossible.
Think Two sides are 6 and 8. Without calculating: is the third side longer or shorter than 10 when the angle between them is 100^\circ? When it is 70^\circ?
At 90^\circ it would be exactly \sqrt{36+64}=10. At 100^\circ, \cos100^\circ<0: the correction is added, so longer. At 70^\circ, \cos70^\circ>0: it is subtracted, so shorter.

4

Finding an Angle (SSS)

Solve the cosine law for the cosine of the angle.

\cos \textcolor{#a33357}{A}=\dfrac{b^2+c^2-\textcolor{#a33357}{a}^2}{2bc}\cos \textcolor{#a33357}{B}=\dfrac{a^2+c^2-\textcolor{#a33357}{b}^2}{2ac}\cos \textcolor{#a33357}{C}=\dfrac{a^2+b^2-\textcolor{#a33357}{c}^2}{2ab}
  • The side subtracted in the numerator is the side opposite the angle you want.
  • \cos^{-1} gives an angle from 0^\circ to 180^\circ. A negative cosine means the angle is obtuse. There is never a second answer, so the cosine law is the safe way to find an angle that might be obtuse.
  • The largest angle is opposite the longest side, and the smallest angle is opposite the shortest side.

One cosine, one angle

Why there is never a second answer: as an angle opens from 0^\circ to 180^\circ, its cosine slides steadily from 1 down to -1, so every cosine belongs to exactly one angle. The sine goes up and comes back down, so most sines belong to two.

Think Can two angles of the same triangle both have negative cosines?
No. A negative cosine means an obtuse angle, and two obtuse angles already add to more than 180^\circ. A triangle has at most one negative cosine.

5

Solving a SAS Triangle Completely

Solving SAS (two sides and the contained angle)
  1. Cosine law for the third side. Now you have a known pair.
  2. Sine law for the smaller unknown angle, the one opposite the shorter given side. It must be acute, so \sin^{-1} gives the right answer.
  3. Angle sum for the last angle: 180^\circ minus the other two.
Keep unrounded values in the calculator until the final answers.

Problems in Context

Sketch the situation and look for two sides with the angle between them (SAS) or three sides (SSS). A bearing such as N25^\circE means: face north, then turn 25^\circ toward east.
Example 7

Two boats

Two boats leave a harbour H at the same time. One travels 14 km on a bearing of N25^\circE. The other travels 19 km on a bearing of S60^\circE. How far apart are the boats, to the nearest tenth of a kilometre?

Angle between the paths: from north to the first boat is 25^\circ; from north to the second is 180^\circ-60^\circ=120^\circ. So \angle H=120^\circ-25^\circ=95^\circ.
d^2=14^2+19^2-2(14)(19)\cos95^\circ\approx603.37
d\approx\boldsymbol{24.6} km
Example 8

The diagonals of a parallelogram

Parallelogram ABCD has AB=14.0 cm, AD=9.0 cm and \angle DAB=62^\circ. Determine the lengths of both diagonals to the nearest tenth.

BD is opposite \angle A=62^\circ in \triangle ABD:
BD^2=14.0^2+9.0^2-2(14.0)(9.0)\cos62^\circ, so BD\approx\boldsymbol{12.6} cm.
Adjacent angles of a parallelogram add to 180^\circ, so \angle ABC=118^\circ, and BC=AD=9.0:
AC^2=14.0^2+9.0^2-2(14.0)(9.0)\cos118^\circ, so AC\approx\boldsymbol{19.9} cm.
✓

Key ideas

When to use it. No known pair (an angle with its opposite side): SAS (the angle is between the two sides) or SSS. Then the sine law can’t start, but the cosine law can.
The law. a^2=b^2+c^2-2bc\cos A: Pythagoras with a correction. The side on the left is opposite the angle at the end; at 90^\circ the correction is 0.
Finding a side (SAS). Substitute, enter it all at once, take the square root. Multiply 2bc\cos A before subtracting. An obtuse angle makes the last term positive.
Finding an angle (SSS). \cos A=\dfrac{b^2+c^2-a^2}{2bc}. Negative cosine: obtuse, with no second answer. The largest angle is opposite the longest side.
