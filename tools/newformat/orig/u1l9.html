Side by side

Same start, two rules. Watch the terms, the dots (n,\,t_n) and the running sums.

How to choose

  1. Add or multiply? A fixed amount each step is arithmetic. A fixed percent or a fixed multiple (doubling, halving, “rises to 70%”) is geometric.
  2. One value or a total? One value (“in year 8”, “row 20”, “height of the 5th bounce”) uses t_n. A total (“altogether”, “in the first 10 weeks”, “total distance”) uses S_n. A total that goes on forever uses S_\infty.
  3. Count the terms carefully. If t_1 is the starting value (time 0), the value after n years is t_{n+1}=t_1r^{n}. If t_1 is the value after year 1, the value after n years is t_n. To count terms from first to last: n=\dfrac{t_n-t_1}{d}+1.
  4. Finding n. From t_n (arithmetic): a linear equation. From S_n (arithmetic): a quadratic; factor it, or use a table of values. Geometric: use a table of values (trial).
  5. Given a formula for S_n: t_1=S_1 and t_n=S_n-S_{n-1}.
  6. Bouncing ball: add the series of drops and the series of rises (the rises start with the first rebound).
2

Choosing the Formula

Read the story for two clues. Clue 1: is the same amount added each time (arithmetic), or the same percent or multiple (geometric)? Clue 2: does it ask for one value (t_n), a total (S_n), or a total forever (S_\infty)? The two answers point to exactly one formula.

Where does the count start?

Growth and decay questions hide a counting trap. If t_1 is the value at time 0, then “after 6 years” is 7 terms in: t_7.

Example 1

Arithmetic or geometric? Term or sum?

For each situation, decide whether it is arithmetic or geometric, and whether it asks for a term or a sum. Then answer it.

(a) A theatre has 24 seats in the first row, and each row has 3 more seats than the row in front of it. How many seats are in the first 20 rows?
Arithmetic (d=3), a sum.
S_{20}=\dfrac{20}{2}\left[2(24)+19(3)\right]=10(105)=\boldsymbol{1050} seats
(b) A car bought for $32 000 loses 15% of its value each year. What is it worth 6 years after it was bought?
Geometric (r=0.85), a term. t_1=32\,000 is the value at time 0, so after 6 years we want t_7.
t_7=32\,000(0.85)^6\approx\boldsymbol{\$12\,068.78}
(c) A message is sent to 3 people in round 1. In each round after that, everyone who just got it sends it to 3 new people. How many people get the message in the first 8 rounds?
Geometric (t_1=3, r=3), a sum.
S_8=\dfrac{3(3^8-1)}{3-1}=\dfrac{3(6560)}{2}=\boldsymbol{9840} people
(d) A pendulum’s first swing is 50 cm long and each swing after that is 90% as long as the one before. What total distance does it travel if it swings forever?
Geometric (r=0.9), an infinite sum.
S_\infty=\dfrac{50}{1-0.9}=\boldsymbol{500} cm
Powers on the TI-84. Use the ^ key, and put brackets around 3^8-1: 3×(3^8−1)÷2ENTER.

Working with Terms

Two terms that are not next to each other? Count the jumps between them. In an arithmetic sequence the gap is (number of jumps) \times\,d; in a geometric one the later term is the earlier term \times\,r^{\text{jumps}}. Then step back to t_1.
Example 2

Two arithmetic terms

In an arithmetic sequence, t_5=17 and t_{12}=45.

(a) Determine d, t_1 and the general term.
From t_5 to t_{12} is 7 steps: 7d=45-17=28\Rightarrow \boldsymbol{d=4}.
t_1=17-4(4)=\boldsymbol{1}.
t_n=1+(n-1)(4)=\boldsymbol{4n-3}
(b) Determine S_{30}. Is 205 a term of the sequence?
S_{30}=\dfrac{30}{2}\left[2(1)+29(4)\right]=15(118)=\boldsymbol{1770}
4n-3=205\Rightarrow n=52. Yes, 205=t_{52}.
Example 3

Two geometric terms

In a geometric sequence, t_2=-12 and t_5=324. Determine r, t_1, the general term and S_8.

From t_2 to t_5 is 3 steps: -12r^3=324\Rightarrow r^3=-27\Rightarrow \boldsymbol{r=-3}.
t_1=-12\div(-3)=\boldsymbol{4}, so \boldsymbol{t_n=4(-3)^{n-1}}.
S_8=\dfrac{4\left[(-3)^8-1\right]}{-3-1}=\dfrac{4(6560)}{-4}=\boldsymbol{-6560}
!
Brackets on a negative ratio. (-3)^8=6561, but -3^8=-6561. On the calculator use (−) inside brackets: ((−)3)^8.

Three terms in a row

For a,\ b,\ c in a row: arithmetic means the two differences match, b-a=c-b. Geometric means the two ratios match, \dfrac{b}{a}=\dfrac{c}{b}, so b^2=ac.

Example 4

Expressions as terms

(a) Determine k so that k+4, 3k and 4k+2 are consecutive terms of an arithmetic sequence. State the terms.
3k-(k+4)=(4k+2)-3k
2k-4=k+2\Rightarrow \boldsymbol{k=6}
Terms: \boldsymbol{10,\ 18,\ 26} (d=8)
(b) Determine x so that x-2, x+2 and x+10 are consecutive terms of a geometric sequence. State the terms and r.
(x+2)^2=(x-2)(x+10)
x^2+4x+4=x^2+8x-20\Rightarrow 24=4x\Rightarrow \boldsymbol{x=6}
Terms: \boldsymbol{4,\ 8,\ 16} (r=2)

Terms from a formula for S_n

S_n is a running total, so each new total is the old total plus one more term: t_1=S_1 and t_n=S_n-S_{n-1}.

Example 5

From S_n back to the terms

The sum of the first n terms of a sequence is S_n=2n^2+3n. Determine t_1, t_2 and t_{10}, and identify the sequence.

t_1=S_1=\boldsymbol{5}.   t_2=S_2-S_1=14-5=\boldsymbol{9}.   t_{10}=S_{10}-S_9=230-189=\boldsymbol{41}.
t_2-t_1=4 and t_{10}=5+9(4)=41: arithmetic with d=4, \boldsymbol{t_n=4n+1}.
4

Finding the Number of Terms

Set the formula equal to the value you know and solve for n. Arithmetic t_n: a linear equation. Arithmetic S_n: a quadratic (factor it; n cannot be negative). Geometric: n is an exponent, so make a table of values and find where it reaches the target.
Example 6

How many terms?

(a) How many terms of 3+5+7+\dots add to 255?
\dfrac{n}{2}\left[2(3)+(n-1)(2)\right]=255
\dfrac{n}{2}(2n+4)=255\Rightarrow n(n+2)=255
n^2+2n-255=0\Rightarrow(n+17)(n-15)=0
\boldsymbol{n=15} (n cannot be negative)
(b) How many terms of 5+10+20+\dots add to 5115?
\dfrac{5(2^n-1)}{2-1}=5115\Rightarrow 2^n-1=1023
2^n=1024. Table of 2^n: 2^{10}=1024.
\boldsymbol{n=10}
Example 7

A growing town

A town has a population of 8400 and is growing by 3.5% per year.

(a) Predict the population 5 years from now.
r=1.035, and 8400 is the population at time 0.
8400(1.035)^5\approx\boldsymbol{9977} people
(b) After how many full years will the population first be over 12 000?
Table of 8400(1.035)^n: n=10 gives about 11\,849; n=11 gives about 12\,264.
After 11 years.

5

Series in Context: the Bouncing Ball

A bouncing ball travels down and up. The drops make one geometric series (starting with the first drop); the rises make another (starting with the first rebound). Count each list carefully, add each series, then add the two totals.
Example 8

The bouncing ball

A ball is dropped from a height of 15 m. After each bounce it rises to 70% of the height it fell from.

(a) How high, to the nearest hundredth of a metre, does it rise after the 4th bounce?
Rises: 10.5,\ 7.35,\ \dots, so t_n=15(0.7)^n.
15(0.7)^4\approx\boldsymbol{3.60} m
(b) What total vertical distance has it travelled when it hits the ground for the 5th time?
5 drops and 4 rises.
Down: S_5=\dfrac{15(1-0.7^5)}{0.3}\approx41.5965
Up: S_4=\dfrac{10.5(1-0.7^4)}{0.3}\approx26.5965
Total \approx\boldsymbol{68.19} m
(c) What total vertical distance does it travel if it bounces forever?
Down: \dfrac{15}{0.3}=50 m.   Up: \dfrac{10.5}{0.3}=35 m.   Total: \boldsymbol{85} m.
Keep the decimals until the end. Round only the final total, not S_5 and S_4 on the way.
✓

Key ideas

Add or multiply? A fixed amount each step is arithmetic (d). A fixed percent or multiple is geometric (r; growth 1+\text{rate}, decay 1-\text{rate}).
One value, a total, or forever? One value: t_n. A total: S_n. Forever: S_\infty=\tfrac{t_1}{1-r}, geometric only, and only if -1<r<1.
Count the jumps. From t_p to t_q is q-p jumps. Time 0 is t_1, so “after n years” is t_{n+1}. k means make k+1 jumps.
Working backwards. t_n=S_n-S_{n-1}. Finding n: linear, quadratic (factor), or a table for a geometric exponent. A ball: drops series + rises series.
