The Ambiguous Case

Two sides and an angle that is not between them (SSA) can give no triangle, one triangle or two triangles. Today: how to tell which, and how to find every solution.

  • use the height h=b\sin A to decide whether SSA gives 0, 1 or 2 triangles

Gus the Goat

Gus is tied to a post 5 m from a long, straight fence. Dandelions grow all along the fence.

2

A None
B One
C Two
D Seven
C, two. The 7 m circle crosses the fence twice, \sqrt{7^2-5^2}=\sqrt{24}\approx4.9 m on each side of the closest point. He can eat every dandelion between them.
The 5 m line straight across decides it: a shorter rope gives none, an equal rope one, a longer rope two. Today that line is the height h.

Challenge your neighbour: how long must the rope be so that the two spots are exactly 24 m apart?

1

Which Measurements Fix a Triangle?

Three measurements, at least one of them a side, describe a triangle. With SSS, SAS (the angle is between the two sides), ASA and AAS, every triangle drawn from the measurements is congruent to every other: there is exactly one triangle.
With SSA (two sides and an angle that is not between them) this is not guaranteed. There may be no triangle, one triangle or two different triangles. SSA is called the ambiguous case.
Example 1

SSS, SAS, ASA, AAS or SSA?

For each \triangle PQR, name the type of information given (SSS, SAS, ASA, AAS or SSA) and state whether it fixes exactly one triangle.

(a) p=7 cm, q=9 cm, r=12 cm
SSS: exactly one triangle.
(b) \angle P=50^\circ, q=8 cm, r=11 cm
Sides q=PR and r=PQ meet at P, so \angle P is between them. SAS: exactly one triangle.
(c) \angle P=50^\circ, \angle Q=72^\circ, r=10 cm
Side r=PQ is between \angle P and \angle Q. ASA: exactly one triangle.
(d) \angle P=50^\circ, p=7 cm, q=8 cm
\angle P is opposite p, not between p and q. SSA: not guaranteed (ambiguous case).

Where the trouble comes from: two angles with the same sine

The sine law gives the sine of the unknown angle, and two angles between 0^\circ and 180^\circ have the same sine (sine is positive in quadrants I and II). The calculator’s sin⁻¹ key gives only the acute one.

For 0^\circ<\theta<180^\circ:   \sin\theta=\sin(180^\circ-\theta)
If \sin B=k with 0<k<1, then \textcolor{#12805a}{B=\sin^{-1}k} (acute) or \textcolor{#7b4fc4}{B=180^\circ-\sin^{-1}k} (obtuse).
Each one gives a triangle only if the angles still add to less than 180^\circ.
Example 2

Mia and Theo are both right

Two students each sketched \triangle ABC with \angle A=40^\circ, AC=10 cm and BC=7 cm. Mia’s triangle has an acute angle at B. Theo’s has an obtuse angle at B.

(a) Use the sine law to find both possible measures of \angle B, to the nearest tenth of a degree.
b=AC=10, a=BC=7.
\dfrac{\sin B}{10}=\dfrac{\sin40^\circ}{7}\Rightarrow\sin B=\dfrac{10\sin40^\circ}{7}\approx0.9183
B=\sin^{-1}(0.9183)\approx\boldsymbol{66.7^\circ}  or  B=180^\circ-66.7^\circ=\boldsymbol{113.3^\circ}
(b) Explain how both students can be right.
Both angles work: 40^\circ+66.7^\circ<180^\circ and 40^\circ+113.3^\circ<180^\circ. The 7 cm side can swing to two different positions, so two different triangles fit the same measurements.

Picturing SSA: The Swinging Side

Suppose \textcolor{#a33357}{\angle A}, side \textcolor{#1f5bd6}{b=AC} and side \textcolor{#a33357}{a=BC} (opposite \angle A) are given. Draw \angle A and mark C on one arm, b units from A. Side a hangs from C and swings like a compass; B is wherever it lands on the other arm. The shortest distance from C to that arm is the height h. From the right triangle, \sin A=\dfrac{h}{b}.

\textcolor{#c27a0e}{h}=b\sin A
Compare \textcolor{#a33357}{a} (the side opposite the given angle) with \textcolor{#c27a0e}{h} and with \textcolor{#1f5bd6}{b}.
Think Why can an obtuse \angle A never give two triangles?
C leans back over the extension of the arm, so the near landing spot is always behind A. Only the far one can be on the arm, and only when a>b. (A triangle can’t have two obtuse angles anyway.)

3

Solving the Ambiguous Case

Solving SSA (given \angle A, a and b; find \angle B)
  1. Sketch. Optionally count the triangles first with h=b\sin A.
  2. Sine law: \sin B=\dfrac{b\sin A}{a}. If this is greater than 1, there is no triangle.
  3. \textcolor{#12805a}{B_1=\sin^{-1}(\sin B)} (acute) and \textcolor{#7b4fc4}{B_2=180^\circ-B_1} (obtuse).
  4. Keep B_2 only if A+B_2<180^\circ.
  5. Finish each triangle: C=180^\circ-A-B, then the third side with the sine law.
Keep unrounded values in the calculator until the final answers.
Example 5

Solve both triangles

Solve all possible triangles ABC in which \angle A=38^\circ, a=9.0 cm and b=13.0 cm. Round angles to the nearest degree and sides to the nearest tenth.

h=13.0\sin38^\circ\approx8.0. Since 8.0<9.0<13.0, there are two triangles.
\sin B=\dfrac{13.0\sin38^\circ}{9.0}\approx0.8893\Rightarrow B_1\approx62.8^\circ,   B_2=180^\circ-62.8^\circ=117.2^\circ   (check: 38^\circ+117.2^\circ<180^\circ ✓)
Triangle 1: C=180^\circ-38^\circ-62.8^\circ=79.2^\circ;   c=\dfrac{9.0\sin79.2^\circ}{\sin38^\circ}\approx14.4 cm
So \boldsymbol{\angle B\approx63^\circ}, \boldsymbol{\angle C\approx79^\circ}, \boldsymbol{c\approx14.4} cm.
Triangle 2: C=180^\circ-38^\circ-117.2^\circ=24.8^\circ;   c=\dfrac{9.0\sin24.8^\circ}{\sin38^\circ}\approx6.1 cm
So \boldsymbol{\angle B\approx117^\circ}, \boldsymbol{\angle C\approx25^\circ}, \boldsymbol{c\approx6.1} cm.
(Rounding C to 25^\circ before finding c gives 6.2 cm: keep the unrounded values.)

Example 6

One, two or none?

Determine all possible measures of the indicated angle(s), to the nearest degree. If there is no triangle, explain why.

(a) \triangle PQR: \angle P=47^\circ, p=15 m, q=11 m. Find \angle Q and \angle R.
p>q (the side opposite the given angle is longer): one triangle.
\sin Q=\dfrac{11\sin47^\circ}{15}\approx0.5363
Q\approx32.4^\circ (or 147.6^\circ, but 47^\circ+147.6^\circ>180^\circ: rejected)
\boldsymbol{\angle Q\approx32^\circ},   \angle R=180^\circ-47^\circ-32.4^\circ\approx\boldsymbol{101^\circ}
(b) \triangle XYZ: \angle X=62^\circ, x=8 cm, y=10 cm. Find \angle Y.
\sin Y=\dfrac{10\sin62^\circ}{8}\approx1.104>1
No angle has a sine greater than 1, so there is no triangle.
(Also h=10\sin62^\circ\approx8.8 and x=8<h: side x cannot reach.)
(c) \triangle KLM: \angle K=108^\circ, k=20 cm, l=14 cm. Find \angle L, \angle M and side m (nearest tenth).
\angle K is obtuse and k>l: one triangle.
\sin L=\dfrac{14\sin108^\circ}{20}\approx0.6657\Rightarrow L\approx41.7^\circ (the obtuse option 138.3^\circ is impossible beside 108^\circ)
\boldsymbol{\angle L\approx42^\circ},   \angle M=180^\circ-108^\circ-41.7^\circ\approx\boldsymbol{30^\circ} (30.3^\circ),   m=\dfrac{20\sin30.3^\circ}{\sin108^\circ}\approx\boldsymbol{10.6} cm

Problems with Two Possible Positions

In a word problem the ambiguous case shows up when a fixed length swings from a fixed point: a gate on a hinge, a strut, a rope, or the edge of a range (radio signal, sprinkler, sound). Sketch both positions, solve both triangles, then decide whether each one makes sense.
Example 7

The ferry and the beacon

A ferry leaves port P and travels in a straight line. A buoy B is 15 km from P, and the angle between the ferry’s path and the line PB is 22^\circ. The buoy’s radio beacon reaches 8 km. At what distances from P along its path is the ferry exactly 8 km from the buoy? Round to the nearest tenth of a kilometre.

In \triangle PBF: \angle P=22^\circ, BF=8 (opposite \angle P), PB=15 (opposite \angle F).
h=15\sin22^\circ\approx5.6<8<15: two positions.
\sin F=\dfrac{15\sin22^\circ}{8}\approx0.7024\Rightarrow F_2\approx44.6^\circ  or  F_1=180^\circ-44.6^\circ=135.4^\circ
F=44.6^\circ: \angle B=180^\circ-22^\circ-44.6^\circ=113.4^\circ,   PF=\dfrac{8\sin113.4^\circ}{\sin22^\circ}\approx19.6 km
F=135.4^\circ: \angle B=180^\circ-22^\circ-135.4^\circ=22.6^\circ,   PF=\dfrac{8\sin22.6^\circ}{\sin22^\circ}\approx8.2 km
The ferry is exactly 8 km from the buoy when it is about 8.2 km and 19.6 km from port (it is within range between those points).
Chain with Ans. 2ndsin⁻¹15×sin22)÷8)ENTER gives F\approx44.6^\circ; then 8×sin180−22−2ndAns)÷sin22ENTER gives 19.60… For the near point, put 180−2ndAnsENTER in between: 8.21…
Example 8

The roof strut

A roof rafter AC is 6.0 m long and makes an angle of 28^\circ with the horizontal ceiling beam at A. A 3.5 m support strut is hinged at the top end C of the rafter and swings down to rest on the beam. How far from A can the foot of the strut be? Round to the nearest tenth of a metre.

In \triangle ACD (D = foot of the strut): \angle A=28^\circ, CD=3.5 (opposite \angle A), AC=6.0 (opposite \angle D).
h=6.0\sin28^\circ\approx2.8<3.5<6.0: two positions.
\sin D=\dfrac{6.0\sin28^\circ}{3.5}\approx0.8048\Rightarrow D\approx53.6^\circ or 126.4^\circ
D=53.6^\circ: \angle C=98.4^\circ,   AD=\dfrac{3.5\sin98.4^\circ}{\sin28^\circ}\approx7.4 m
D=126.4^\circ: \angle C=25.6^\circ,   AD=\dfrac{3.5\sin25.6^\circ}{\sin28^\circ}\approx3.2 m
The foot of the strut can be about 3.2 m or 7.4 m from A.
✓

Key ideas

One triangle or not? SSS, SAS, ASA and AAS fix exactly one triangle. SSA (the angle is not between the sides) is the ambiguous case: 0, 1 or 2 triangles.
Two angles, one sine. \sin B=\sin(180^\circ-B). sin⁻¹ gives only the acute B_1; the other is B_2=180^\circ-B_1. Keep it only if A+B_2<180^\circ.
Count with the height. h=b\sin A. Acute A: a<h none, a=h one (right), h<a<b two, a\ge b one. Obtuse A: a\le b none, a>b one.
Solve every triangle. For each B: C=180^\circ-A-B, then c by the sine law. Keep unrounded values. In word problems, sketch both positions and check each makes sense.
